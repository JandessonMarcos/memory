Why does it matter where you buy a men's vitality gummy?

Because this is the category where tainted products are most common. The FDA runs a dedicated page on tainted sexual enhancement products and has listed hundreds of them over the years. The pattern is consistent. A product sold as "all natural" or "herbal" turns out, under lab testing, to contain sildenafil (the active ingredient in Viagra), tadalafil (Cialis) or chemical cousins of those drugs that were never approved at all.

Some of these come in forms that look harmless: honey packets, instant coffee, chocolates and candy-style products. A gummy fits right into that list. That's not a statement about Vigor Boost, whose label lists only botanicals, an amino acid and caffeine. It's a statement about what happens when the same name and picture can be printed on any bottle by anyone.

Counterfeiters target this category for obvious reasons. Demand is high, buyers are often embarrassed to ask questions, and a product that "works" because it secretly contains a prescription drug gets repeat customers. The cost lands on the buyer.

What are the risks of a counterfeit or tainted product?

The main danger is a hidden PDE5 inhibitor, the class of drug that includes sildenafil and tadalafil. On its own, under a doctor's care, that class is well studied. Hidden and unmeasured, it's a different story.

  • Nitrates. Men who take nitroglycerin or other nitrates for chest pain can't safely take PDE5 inhibitors. The combination can drop blood pressure to dangerous levels. A man who's been told not to take Viagra might take a "herbal" gummy and get the same drug anyway.
  • Unknown dose. Lab tests of tainted products have found drug amounts that vary from bottle to bottle, sometimes above the prescribed maximum.
  • Unapproved analogs. Some products contain drug-like compounds that were never tested in people at all.
  • Other blood pressure effects. Men on alpha-blockers or several blood pressure drugs are also at risk of a sharp drop.
  • No recourse. If something goes wrong, there's no seller to contact and no record of what you took.

Even the genuine Vigor Boost formula deserves a conversation with your doctor if you take nitrates, ED drugs or blood pressure medicine, since it contains L-arginine. Our page on Vigor Boost side effects and interactions covers that. A counterfeit simply removes the one thing you could check: the label.

How do you make sure you're on the official Vigor Boost checkout?

A few checks take less than a minute:

  • Start from a trusted link. Use the button on a page you trust, or type the brand's site yourself, instead of clicking through a chain of ads and redirects.
  • Check the packages. You should see 2, 3 and 6 bottle options with the guarantee written out.
  • Look for a one-time charge. There should be no subscription, no auto-ship box pre-ticked, and no trial that converts later.
  • Check the address bar. The page should be on HTTPS, and the domain shouldn't change to something unrelated at the payment step.
  • Pay by credit card. It gives you the strongest dispute rights if something goes wrong.
  • Save proof. Screenshot the package, price and guarantee, and keep the confirmation email.

After you order, watch for two things. First, the confirmation email, which should show the package you chose and the total you agreed to. Second, your card statement. The charge may appear under a payment processor or company name rather than the words "Vigor Boost", which is normal for online stores. What isn't normal is a second charge you didn't approve, or a smaller recurring one a few weeks later. If you see either, contact the seller first, then your card issuer.

What if you already bought Vigor Boost somewhere else?

Don't panic, and don't take it until you've checked it.

  • Compare the bottle against the label details above, line by line.
  • If anything doesn't match, or the seal is broken, set it aside. Contact the marketplace for a refund under its own buyer-protection policy.
  • If you've taken it and felt a strong, fast effect, a headache, flushing, vision changes or dizziness, those are signs commonly associated with hidden PDE5 drugs. Stop and talk to a doctor, especially if you take any heart or blood pressure medicine.
  • Report suspected fakes or bad reactions to the FDA through its MedWatch program. Reports are how tainted products end up on the FDA's warning list.

Should you see a doctor before buying any men's vitality product?

If what's driving the purchase is a change in erections, yes. New or persistent erection problems can be an early sign of heart disease, diabetes or low testosterone. A doctor can check for those and, if appropriate, prescribe a medication with a known dose from a real pharmacy, which is the opposite of the tainted-product gamble. Vigor Boost isn't an ED treatment, and it isn't meant to replace one.
