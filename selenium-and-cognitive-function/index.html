Selenium and Cognitive Function: What the Research Shows

Selenium is a trace mineral you need in tiny amounts, and it happens to play an outsized role in protecting the brain from oxidative stress and keeping thyroid hormone running properly.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published August 26, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Selenium builds selenoproteins, a family of enzymes that defend brain cells against oxidative stress.
  • It's essential for thyroid function, and the brain is highly sensitive to thyroid hormone levels.
  • Low selenium status has been linked to worse cognitive performance in some older-adult studies, though this doesn't prove supplementing prevents decline.
  • One or two Brazil nuts a day typically covers the full daily requirement.
  • More is not safer. Selenium has a narrow margin between an adequate dose and a harmful one.

Short answer: selenium is genuinely important for brain health, mainly through its role in antioxidant defense and thyroid hormone activation, but the evidence that taking more of it improves memory in well-nourished people is thin. The mineral matters most when someone is actually short on it, which is a smaller group than supplement marketing suggests.

Here's how selenium fits into brain health, what the research on cognition actually shows, and where the practical advice lands.

What does selenium actually do for the brain?

Selenium's job is to get built into a family of proteins called selenoproteins, and the brain holds onto its selenium more tightly than almost any other organ when intake runs low. One group of selenoproteins, the glutathione peroxidases, neutralizes reactive oxygen species before they damage neurons. Another, selenoprotein P, is the main carrier that shuttles selenium into brain tissue in the first place. A third group, the iodothyronine deiodinases, converts inactive thyroid hormone (T4) into its active form (T3), and brain development and function depend heavily on adequate thyroid hormone.

What does the research say about selenium and cognitive decline?

Several observational studies in older populations have found that people with lower blood selenium levels tend to score worse on cognitive testing, and some have linked low selenium status to a higher rate of decline over time. This kind of study can show an association without proving cause. People with poorer diets overall, more chronic illness, or lower general health status often have lower selenium too, and any of those factors could explain the link on their own. Clinical trials testing whether selenium supplements actually slow cognitive decline have been smaller and less consistent, and none give a clear, reliable answer for people who are not deficient to begin with.

The thyroid connection matters more than people realize

Because selenium is required to activate thyroid hormone, a deficiency can contribute to an underactive thyroid, and thyroid problems produce their own kind of brain fog: sluggish thinking, poor concentration and memory complaints. If you're investigating unexplained cognitive symptoms, thyroid function is worth checking alongside selenium status rather than instead of it. Who is actually likely to run low?

Selenium deficiency is rare in the US and most of North America, largely because agricultural soil provides adequate amounts and food is traded widely across regions. It's more common in parts of the world with genuinely selenium-poor soil, including regions of China and parts of Europe. People with severe malabsorption conditions, those on long-term restrictive diets, and people relying entirely on food grown in a single low-selenium region are the groups most likely to fall short. For most readers eating a normal, varied diet, deficiency isn't a realistic concern.

Selenium matters a great deal when you're short on it. For everyone else, more isn't the same as better, and here that gap is unusually wide.

Why did the SELECT trial change how selenium is viewed?

A large US trial called SELECT tested whether selenium and vitamin E supplements could prevent prostate cancer in men who were not selenium deficient. Selenium showed no benefit, and the vitamin E arm was later associated with a small increase in prostate cancer risk. The trial is often cited as a caution against assuming that more of a beneficial nutrient is automatically better, and it's part of why current guidance focuses on correcting actual shortfalls rather than broad supplementation in people who already get enough.

How much do you need, and is a supplement worth it?

The adult reference intake is 55 micrograms a day. Brazil nuts are the standout food source, with a single nut sometimes providing close to or above a full day's amount, though the actual content swings widely depending on the soil where the tree grew, so treat them as a strong source rather than a precise dose. Seafood, organ meats, eggs, sunflower seeds and whole grains all add meaningful amounts. The safe upper limit is 400 micrograms a day, and selenium toxicity from chronic over-supplementation is a documented problem, with hair loss, brittle nails and gastrointestinal upset among the early warning signs. For most people, food comfortably covers the requirement, and the better use of a supplement budget is on nutrients with clearer cognitive benefits, covered in our best vitamins for brain fog guide.

When is it worth getting tested?

Testing makes sense for people with a diagnosed malabsorption condition, those on very restrictive long-term diets, or anyone with unexplained thyroid dysfunction alongside fatigue and cognitive complaints. Otherwise, selenium status isn't typically part of a routine cognitive workup, and other, more common contributors deserve attention first, which our broader guide to improving your memory walks through.

Frequently asked questions

Does selenium improve memory or cognitive function?

In people who are low in selenium, correcting the deficiency may support cognitive health, and some observational studies link low selenium status to worse cognitive performance in older adults. In people who already get enough selenium, there is no good evidence that extra selenium sharpens memory.

What are signs of selenium deficiency?

True selenium deficiency is uncommon in the US because most soil provides adequate levels. When it does occur, it tends to cause fatigue, muscle weakness, a weakened immune response and, over time, thyroid problems, since selenium is required to activate thyroid hormone.

How much selenium do you need per day?

Adults need about 55 micrograms a day. A single Brazil nut can provide close to or above that amount, though the exact content varies a lot depending on the soil it grew in, so treat Brazil nuts as a strong source rather than a precise dose.

Can too much selenium be harmful?

Yes. Selenium has a narrow safety margin compared with many nutrients. Regularly exceeding the upper limit of 400 micrograms a day can cause hair loss, brittle nails, nausea and, in more severe cases, nerve problems, so high-dose supplements aren't advisable without a documented deficiency.

What foods are high in selenium?

Brazil nuts are by far the richest source. Seafood, organ meats, eggs, sunflower seeds and whole grains grown in selenium-rich soil also contribute meaningfully. Most people eating a varied diet reach the recommended intake without needing a supplement.
