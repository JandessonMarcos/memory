Chewing and Cognitive Function: Does Poor Dental Health Affect Memory?

Losing your natural teeth changes more than what you can eat. Researchers have found that how well you chew is tied to brain activity, nutrition and, in some long-running studies, dementia risk.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published October 6, 2026

Photo: Frankie Fouganthin / Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA 4.0

Key takeaways

  • Brain-imaging research has found that chewing activates regions tied to memory and attention, including areas near the hippocampus, during the act itself.
  • Large population studies, several out of Japan, link fewer remaining teeth and weaker chewing ability with higher rates of cognitive decline. The association is consistent, but it's observational.
  • Part of the link may simply be mechanical. Fewer teeth usually means a softer diet, less fiber and fewer of the nutrients tied to brain health.
  • Reduced blood flow during chewing is one proposed pathway, though the human evidence for this is thinner than the population data.
  • The relationship may run both ways. Early cognitive decline can itself make dental care harder to keep up with.
  • Replacing missing teeth and keeping up dental care is good medicine either way, regardless of how the dementia question eventually settles.

It's an odd question to take seriously at first. Does chewing actually affect your memory? The honest, hedged answer is that there's a real and repeated association between how well someone chews, how many natural teeth they have left, and how their cognition holds up over time. What's missing is proof that fixing one directly protects the other.

What does the research actually show about chewing and the brain?

Two threads of evidence feed this topic, and it's worth keeping them apart. The first comes from brain imaging. Researchers who have scanned people while they chew have reported activity in motor and sensory regions, as expected, but also in areas involved in attention and memory, including structures near the hippocampus. That's a real, reproducible finding about what chewing does to brain activity in the moment. It is not, by itself, evidence that chewing more will make your memory better long-term.

The second thread is population-level. Long-running cohort studies that track older adults over many years have repeatedly found that people with fewer remaining natural teeth, or who report more difficulty chewing, show higher rates of cognitive decline and dementia diagnosis during follow-up compared with people who chew well and have most of their teeth. This pattern has shown up across several different research groups and countries, which is part of why it's taken seriously. It's still observational data, meaning it shows a pattern, not a guaranteed cause.

Why would chewing affect brain function at all?

A few mechanisms have been proposed, and none of them is fully nailed down. One is sensory. Chewing sends a constant stream of signals from the jaw muscles and the ligaments around each tooth up into the brain, and some researchers think that steady input helps maintain activity in nearby memory and attention circuits. Lose enough teeth, and you lose some of that input.

A second is blood flow. Some studies, mostly in animals and smaller human trials, have found that the act of chewing temporarily increases blood flow to parts of the brain. Whether a lifetime of reduced chewing meaningfully reduces average brain blood flow in people is still an open question, not an established fact.

The third mechanism is the simplest and probably does the most work: diet. People who struggle to chew tend to shift toward softer, more processed foods, and away from raw vegetables, nuts, and fibrous fruit. That shift alone can lower intake of fiber, vitamins and the nutrients that show up again and again in brain-health research, independent of anything happening in the jaw itself.

Could this just be reverse causation?

It's a fair question, and researchers studying this ask it too. Someone in the early, often unnoticed stages of cognitive decline may find it harder to keep up with brushing, flossing, and regular dental visits, which can itself lead to more tooth loss over time. In that version of events, fading cognition is partly a cause of worse dental health, not only an effect of it. Most researchers in this space think both directions are probably happening at once, which is exactly why clean cause-and-effect claims are hard to make.

The most honest reading of this research is that a healthy mouth and a healthy brain tend to travel together as people age, not that losing a molar will cost you a memory.

What should you actually do with this information?

Treat your teeth like they matter on their own terms, because they do. See a dentist about replacing missing teeth with dentures, bridges or implants that genuinely restore chewing function, rather than leaving gaps. Favor foods that actually require chewing: raw vegetables, whole fruit, nuts, and whole grains, which also happen to be the foods that show up in most brain-healthy eating patterns. Good oral hygiene matters here too; our piece on gum disease and dementia risk covers a closely related angle on chronic mouth inflammation.

Frequently asked questions

Does losing teeth cause dementia?

That hasn't been proven. Population studies find that people with fewer natural teeth and weaker chewing ability show higher rates of cognitive decline, but tooth loss shares risk factors with dementia, including age, smoking and poor nutrition, so a direct cause-and-effect claim goes further than the evidence supports.

Can dentures or implants restore the brain benefits of chewing?

Some research suggests that replacing missing teeth with dentures or implants that actually restore chewing function is associated with better outcomes than leaving gaps unaddressed. It hasn't been tested as a dementia-prevention treatment, but restoring the ability to chew a varied diet is reasonable on its own merits.

Does chewing gum improve memory or focus?

Small short-term studies have found modest improvements in alertness or attention during or shortly after chewing gum, though results are inconsistent and the effect, if real, is brief. It isn't comparable to the long-term population research on tooth loss and cognitive decline.

Should I be worried if I've already lost a few teeth?

Worry isn't the useful response, attention is. A missing tooth or two matters less than cumulative tooth loss over many years. Seeing a dentist about replacement options and keeping up with the other habits that protect memory matters more than any single number.
