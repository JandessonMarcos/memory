Metabolism After 40: What Really Changes

"Your metabolism slows down after 40" gets thrown around a lot. Here's what the research actually shows is changing, and what makes the biggest difference.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published November 9, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Resting metabolism changes less than people assume in your 40s and 50s; the bigger drop tends to come later, after 60.
  • Muscle loss is the real driver. Adults can lose roughly 3 to 8% of muscle mass per decade after 30 without strength training.
  • Lower daily activity, not a "broken" metabolism, explains most of the calorie gap people notice in midlife.
  • Resistance training is the single most useful habit for keeping metabolic rate from declining further.
  • No supplement replicates the effect of muscle, sleep and daily movement, whatever the label claims.

Metabolism after 40 does change, just not as dramatically, or as suddenly, as the phrase "metabolism slows down" suggests. Resting metabolic rate stays fairly stable through most of your 40s and 50s in most studies, with a steeper decline typically showing up later, after 60. What usually shifts earlier is muscle mass, which drops gradually if you aren't doing anything to maintain it, and that loss is what quietly lowers how many calories your body burns at rest.

That distinction matters, because it changes what's actually worth doing about it. A "slow metabolism" sounds like something that happens to you. Muscle loss and lower activity are things you have real influence over.

Does metabolism actually slow down at 40?

Large studies measuring total energy expenditure across the lifespan have found that metabolism stays remarkably stable from the 20s through the 50s, once body composition is accounted for. The more meaningful decline tends to start closer to 60. That doesn't mean nothing changes in your 40s; it means the common story, that metabolism cliffs off the moment you turn 40, isn't quite right.

What does change earlier is harder to see on a bathroom scale: the balance between muscle and fat. Lose muscle and gain fat at the same weight, and resting energy use still drops, because muscle tissue burns more calories at rest than fat tissue does.

What's actually driving the change in your 40s?

Resting metabolic rateFairly stable through the 40s and 50s; bigger decline after 60
Muscle massGradual loss, roughly 3–8% per decade without strength training
Daily activity (NEAT)Often declines with busier schedules, desk jobs, less incidental movement

Hormones (women)Perimenopause shifts fat distribution and can affect muscle maintenance

Three of these five rows are things you have meaningful control over. That's the more useful way to read this table than as a list of inevitable losses.

The daily activity row deserves more attention than it usually gets. Researchers call the calories burned through ordinary movement, fidgeting, standing up, taking the stairs, walking to a car, non-exercise activity thermogenesis, or NEAT. It can vary by several hundred calories a day between two people of similar size, and it tends to quietly shrink as careers get busier and commutes get longer. A desk job at 45 often involves thousands fewer daily steps than the same person's twenties did, and that gap adds up over a year far more than most people assume.

How much does muscle loss really matter?

More than most people expect. Muscle is metabolically active tissue, meaning it burns calories even while you're sitting still, at a noticeably higher rate than fat does. Left unaddressed, adults can lose a meaningful share of muscle mass per decade starting around age 30, a process sometimes called age-related muscle loss. That loss accelerates without resistance training and adequate protein, and it's the single biggest reason resting calorie burn can drift downward even when the number on the scale hasn't moved.

The encouraging part: muscle responds to training at any age. Adults in their 50s, 60s and beyond consistently build measurable strength and muscle with regular resistance training, which is why it's the habit most consistently recommended for this exact problem.

Can strength training actually reverse it?

It's the closest thing to a reliable fix that exists. Two to three resistance-training sessions a week, covering the major muscle groups, is enough for most people to maintain or rebuild muscle mass, which in turn supports resting metabolic rate. The effect isn't instant; measurable changes in body composition usually take eight to twelve weeks of consistent training to show up clearly.

Cardio still matters for heart health and calorie burn during the activity itself, but it doesn't build or preserve muscle the way resistance training does. A combination of both, with resistance training prioritized as people get older, has the best evidence behind it.

How much muscle someone can realistically regain depends a lot on starting point and consistency. Someone who has never trained often sees the fastest early gains, a pattern sometimes called "newbie gains," while someone returning after years away tends to rebuild lost muscle faster than they built it the first time, a phenomenon researchers link to lingering cellular changes from previous training. Neither case requires anything exotic: full-body sessions two or three times a week, enough weight to make the last few repetitions genuinely hard, and progressing gradually over months.

Does diet need to change after 40?

Protein needs don't drop with age, and there's a reasonable case they go up slightly, since older muscle needs more protein stimulus to build and maintain than younger muscle does. Many adults over 40 eat roughly the same amount of protein they did at 25, which may undersupply what their muscles now need to hold onto mass.

Total calorie needs often do drop a bit, mainly because of the muscle and activity changes above rather than age by itself. Rather than cutting calories sharply, most people do better adjusting gradually and watching the trend over several weeks, since day-to-day weight naturally bounces around.

Spreading protein across meals, rather than loading most of it at dinner, appears to matter too. Muscle-building signals in the body respond to protein in a roughly dose-dependent way up to a point per meal, so three servings of 25 to 30 grams spread through the day tend to support muscle maintenance better than one large 70-gram serving at night with little protein earlier on. This is a small adjustment compared with training, but it's a free one, and it tends to help more after 40 than it did at 25.

What about hormones, especially for women?

Perimenopause and menopause bring real hormonal shifts that affect where fat is stored, often more toward the abdomen, and can make muscle maintenance somewhat harder. Men experience a slower, more gradual decline in testosterone starting around the same age range, with similar downstream effects on muscle and fat distribution. In both cases, the hormonal shift and the ordinary muscle loss of aging overlap, which makes it hard to say how much of any individual's change comes from one versus the other. Do metabolism-boosting supplements actually work?

Mostly no, not in any way that competes with muscle, sleep and movement. Caffeine and green tea extract show small, short-lived increases in calorie burn in some studies, typically a few percent for a few hours, nowhere near enough to offset a sedentary lifestyle or meaningful muscle loss. Products marketed as metabolism boosters rarely disclose evidence at the dose actually used in the bottle, which is worth checking before spending money on one.

Metabolism after 40 isn't a switch that flips. It's a slow drift, mostly driven by muscle you can keep if you train for it.

When should you see a doctor about metabolism or weight changes?

See a doctor if weight changes are sudden, unexplained, or paired with other symptoms like fatigue, hair or skin changes, or irregular periods, since thyroid issues and other medical conditions can affect metabolism and are worth ruling out. Frequently asked questions

Does your metabolism really slow down after 40?

Yes, modestly. Research suggests resting metabolic rate stays fairly stable through most of your 40s and 50s, then declines more noticeably after 60. The bigger driver in your 40s is usually muscle loss and lower activity, not age itself.

How many calories less do you need after 40?

There's no universal number. For many people, calorie needs drop gradually by roughly 1 to 2% per decade through midlife, mostly tied to muscle loss and lower activity rather than age alone. Tracking your weight trend over a few weeks is more useful than any formula.

Can strength training fix a slow metabolism?

It helps more than almost anything else available. Building and keeping muscle raises resting energy use because muscle burns more at rest than fat does, and strength training is the most reliable way to preserve muscle as you age.

Do metabolism-boosting supplements actually work?

Most have little to no meaningful effect on their own. Caffeine and green tea extract can produce a small, temporary bump in calorie burn, but nothing sold as a metabolism booster comes close to the effect of muscle, sleep and daily movement.

Does menopause affect metabolism?

The hormonal shifts around menopause are linked to a change in fat distribution, more toward the abdomen, and some muscle loss, both of which can affect metabolic rate. The timing often overlaps with midlife muscle loss that happens in men too, which makes it hard to separate hormones from aging alone.

Why do I gain weight easier after 40 even though I eat the same?

It's usually a combination of small factors: less muscle than a decade ago, somewhat lower daily activity, and a calorie intake that hasn't adjusted to match. Each piece is small, but they add up over months and years.

What's the single most useful habit for metabolism after 40?

Resistance training two to three times a week, done consistently. It's the one habit with strong evidence for preserving the muscle that keeps resting metabolism from declining further.
