Prebiotics vs Probiotics: What's the Difference?

The two words get used like synonyms. They're not. One is live bacteria, the other is what feeds them, and mixing them up is a common reason a supplement doesn't seem to do much.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published October 25, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Probiotics are live microorganisms that add to the bacteria already living in your gut. Prebiotics are fiber and other compounds that feed the bacteria you already have.
  • You don't have to choose. They work on different ends of the same system, and most guts benefit from both.
  • Food covers most people's needs. Yogurt, kefir and sauerkraut supply probiotics; onions, garlic, oats and bananas supply prebiotics.
  • Supplements help in specific situations: after antibiotics, with a diagnosed condition, or when food sources are limited. They're not a required daily habit for everyone.
  • Starting slowly matters. A sudden jump in either one can cause bloating and gas before it settles down.

Probiotics are live bacteria you add to your gut, found in yogurt, kefir and sauerkraut, or in capsule form. Prebiotics are fiber compounds, mostly from plants, that feed the bacteria already living there. Neither replaces the other, and most people's digestion improves most from getting enough of both, not from picking a side.

The two get marketed side by side, sometimes blended into the same bottle labeled “synbiotic,” and the names are close enough that it's easy to assume they do the same job. They don't. One is a guest you're inviting in. The other is food you're putting out for the guests already there. Mixing that up is a common reason a supplement doesn't seem to do much of anything.

What's the difference between prebiotics and probiotics?

Probiotics are specific, living strains, think Lactobacillus rhamnosus or Bifidobacterium longum, that are still alive when they reach your gut and can briefly add to the mix of bacteria already there. Prebiotics aren't alive. They're usually a type of fiber, such as inulin, resistant starch or certain oligosaccharides, that your own digestive enzymes can't break down but that specific gut bacteria can ferment and use as fuel.

The practical difference shows up in how each one works. A probiotic supplement is judged by which strain it contains, how many live organisms survive to the gut (measured in CFU, or colony-forming units), and whether that strain has actually been studied for the symptom you're trying to fix. A prebiotic is judged mostly by the type and amount of fiber, since it isn't trying to introduce anything new. It's trying to feed what's already growing.

How do probiotics actually work once you swallow them?

Most probiotic strains aren't meant to move in permanently. They pass through, interact with your existing bacteria and your immune system along the way, and are mostly gone within a couple of weeks of stopping. That's a normal part of how they work, not a sign the product failed. Any benefit usually depends on keeping a strain around through regular use, not a single dose.

Effects are strain-specific, which is the detail most marketing skips. Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG has decent evidence for shortening certain types of diarrhea. Certain Bifidobacterium strains have been studied for irritable bowel syndrome symptoms. A probiotic that doesn't name its strain on the label, just “probiotic blend,” is handing you much less information than it looks like at first glance.

How do prebiotics work, and why do they matter just as much?

When prebiotic fiber reaches your colon undigested, certain bacteria ferment it and produce short-chain fatty acids, including butyrate, which feed the cells lining your gut wall and appear to support a calmer immune response there. That fermentation also tends to increase the numbers of a few specific, generally beneficial bacterial groups, like Bifidobacteria, fairly reliably across different people.

This is arguably the less glamorous half of the story, since you can't buy a prebiotic strain the way you can a probiotic one. What you're really buying, or eating, is a type and dose of fiber. That's also why food usually gets you further here than a supplement does: a varied, high-fiber diet supplies a mix of fermentable fibers that no single prebiotic capsule replicates.

Which foods give you prebiotics, and which give you probiotics?

Most people can meet both needs through food most of the time, which is worth knowing before reaching for a supplement aisle.

  • Yogurt with “live and active cultures” on the label
  • Kefir, a fermented milk drink with a wider range of strains than most yogurt
  • Sauerkraut and kimchi, unpasteurized versions only (pasteurization kills the live cultures)
  • Miso and tempeh
  • Kombucha, in moderation (it does contain some sugar and caffeine)

Prebiotic fiber is less visible on a label but just as easy to find in a normal grocery cart.

  • Garlic, onions and leeks
  • Asparagus and artichokes
  • Bananas, especially slightly underripe ones
  • Oats and barley
  • Beans and lentils
  • Chicory root, which shows up in some fiber supplements as inulin

Do you need to take both, or is one enough?

Neither one on its own covers everything the other does, so “enough” depends on what you're trying to fix. Someone recovering from a course of antibiotics is usually better served by reintroducing live bacteria with a probiotic, since antibiotics reduce the population directly. Someone eating a low-fiber diet day to day has a prebiotic gap no probiotic capsule will close, because there's nothing for the extra bacteria to eat once they arrive.

For general, non-specific “gut health,” most clinicians lean toward fixing the diet first: more plant variety covers a lot of the prebiotic side on its own, and fermented foods can cover a meaningful share of the probiotic side too. Supplements tend to matter more for a defined problem than as a baseline habit everyone needs.

A practical way to think about it: probiotics are closer to a short-term intervention, useful around a specific disruption like antibiotics, travel, or a new diagnosis, while prebiotic fiber is closer to a long-term maintenance habit, since the bacteria it feeds turn over every few days whether or not you're paying attention to them. Someone who only ever does one of the two tends to get a smaller result than someone who treats them as two separate, ongoing habits.

A shelf-stable probiotic uses a strain, and usually a specific manufacturing process, selected to survive outside refrigeration for the length of its stated shelf life. That's a real engineering difference, not a marketing word, and it's why some capsules sit safely in a pantry while a yogurt with the same bacterial family needs the fridge. It doesn't make the shelf-stable version automatically better or stronger; it just means it was built for a different kind of handling.

Where this matters most is in how a product was shipped and stored before it reached you. A refrigerated probiotic that sat in a hot delivery van for a day, or a shelf-stable one stored somewhere humid for months past its print date, can both lose a meaningful share of their live count before you ever open the bottle. Checking the “CFU at time of manufacture” language on the label, rather than just trusting the number on the front, is a small habit that tells you more than the marketing claims around it.

Is a supplement necessary, or can food usually cover it?

For most people without a specific digestive complaint, food covers it. The exceptions tend to be practical rather than medical: someone who genuinely can't tolerate fermented foods, or whose diet is low in fiber and isn't likely to change quickly, might get more consistent results from a supplement in the short term while working on the food side.

When a supplement is the right call, strain and dose matter more than the word “probiotic” on the front of the bottle. A product that lists its CFU count at the time of manufacture, not at expiration, names its exact strains, and has some published research behind that specific product is a meaningfully different purchase than a generic multi-strain blend with no studies attached to it.

What's a synbiotic, and is it just marketing?

A synbiotic combines a specific probiotic strain with a prebiotic fiber chosen to feed that exact strain, in theory giving the live bacteria something to eat right away instead of competing for whatever fiber happens to already be in your gut. The idea has real biological logic behind it.

Whether a given synbiotic product lives up to that logic depends entirely on whether the pairing was actually tested together, not just assembled because it sounded good on a label. Some combinations have decent small studies. Plenty of others are just a probiotic blend with a scoop of inulin added, with no evidence that the specific pairing does more than either ingredient would alone.

Who should be cautious with prebiotics or probiotic supplements?

People who are immunocompromised, critically ill, or have a central venous catheter have, in rare cases, developed infections traced back to a probiotic strain, which is why hospitals treat probiotic use in those patients as a medical decision, not a casual one. If that applies to you or someone you're caring for, check with a doctor before starting any live-culture product.

Prebiotics carry a different, much more common downside: for people with IBS or small intestinal bacterial overgrowth, fermentable fibers (the “FODMAP” group overlaps heavily with common prebiotics) can make bloating and gas noticeably worse rather than better. If high-FODMAP foods already bother you, that's a sign to go slowly, or to ask a doctor or dietitian before adding a concentrated prebiotic supplement. How much of each should you actually aim for?

There's no single official target for probiotics, since recommended amounts are tied to the specific strain and the specific use, not a universal number. For prebiotic fiber, there's a clearer benchmark: most adults are advised to get roughly 25 to 38 grams of total fiber a day, and current intake for most people in the U.S. falls well short of that, often by close to half.

Whichever one you're adding, start small. Jumping straight to a high dose of either, a strong probiotic or several servings of chicory-root fiber in one day, is a common reason people quit before giving it a fair trial. A few extra grams of fiber or one serving of a fermented food every few days, building up over two to three weeks, tends to go a lot more smoothly than starting at full dose.

When is it worth talking to a doctor before adding either one?

Most people can experiment with food sources of both without any real risk. A conversation with a doctor is worth having before starting a supplement if you're immunocompromised, pregnant, dealing with a chronic digestive diagnosis like IBD, or noticing new symptoms, severe bloating, persistent pain, or a real change in bowel habits, rather than mild adjustment-period gas.

It's also worth asking if you've been taking a probiotic or prebiotic supplement for more than a month without any noticeable change. That isn't automatically a sign something's wrong, but it's a reasonable point to check whether a different strain, a different fiber, or a different approach makes more sense. Frequently asked questions

Can you take prebiotics and probiotics at the same time?

Yes, and many people do, either as separate products or as a combined synbiotic. There's no established risk in taking both together; the main thing is starting with modest amounts of each so your gut has time to adjust.

Do probiotics need to be refrigerated?

Some do and some don't, and it depends on the strain and how it was processed, not on probiotics as a category. Check the label: many shelf-stable capsules use a strain selected to survive at room temperature, while refrigerated yogurt and kefir need the cold chain to keep their live cultures intact.

How long before you notice a difference?

Digestive changes from added fiber can show up within days. Shifts in gut bacteria from a probiotic generally take two to four weeks of consistent use to become noticeable, if they do at all, since not everyone responds the same way to the same strain.

Can prebiotics cause bloating?

Yes, especially if you add a lot at once. The fermentation that makes prebiotics useful also produces gas as a byproduct, which is why starting with small amounts and increasing gradually causes far less discomfort than jumping straight to a full dose.

Are prebiotic and probiotic supplements regulated like drugs?

No. In the U.S. they're regulated as dietary supplements, which means the FDA doesn't review them for effectiveness before they reach shelves the way it does medications. Look for products with third-party testing or published research behind the specific strain or formula.

Is yogurt enough, or do you need a separate supplement too?

For a lot of people, yes, plain yogurt with live cultures covers a meaningful share of their probiotic intake. A supplement makes more sense when you're targeting a specific, well-studied strain for a specific issue rather than general maintenance.

Can children take prebiotics or probiotics?

Some pediatric formulations exist and certain strains have been studied in children, but dosing and strain choice differ from adult products. Check with a pediatrician before giving a child a supplement rather than assuming an adult product scaled down is appropriate.
