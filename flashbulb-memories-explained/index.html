Flashbulb Memories: Why Some Moments Feel Burned Into Memory

You can probably describe exactly where you were and who you were with the moment you heard a piece of major news. That vividness feels like proof. Research suggests it's mostly a feeling, not a fact.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published September 14, 2026

Photo: Shixart1985 / Wikimedia Commons / CC BY 2.0

Key takeaways

  • A flashbulb memory is an unusually vivid, detailed memory of the moment you learned about a surprising or emotionally significant event, not of the event itself.
  • The term was coined in 1977 by psychologists Roger Brown and James Kulik, who studied how people remembered hearing about the assassination of President John F. Kennedy.
  • Flashbulb memories feel unusually accurate, but research suggests they aren't necessarily more accurate than ordinary memories, they just come with more confidence.
  • Emotional intensity, surprise and personal significance seem to drive the effect, not the size of the event itself.
  • Like other memories, flashbulb memories can fade and shift over the years, even while the person recalling them feels certain nothing has changed.

Ask someone where they were when they heard a piece of genuinely shocking news, and most people can answer instantly, and in detail: the room, the time of day, who told them, what they were doing a second before. That specific, vivid kind of recall has a name. Psychologists call it a flashbulb memory, and decades of research into it have turned up something a little humbling: it feels far more reliable than it actually is.

What exactly is a flashbulb memory?

A flashbulb memory is an unusually vivid, detailed recollection of the circumstances in which you first learned about a surprising, emotionally significant event. It's not a memory of the event itself, most people learn about major news secondhand, but of the moment of learning: where you were, what you were doing, who else was there, how you felt. The name suggests something like a photograph, a single flash that captures everything at once and holds it perfectly.

That photographic quality is exactly the part later research pushed back on.

Where the idea comes from

The term was introduced in 1977 by psychologists Roger Brown and James Kulik, who asked people to recall the moment they heard about the assassination of President John F. Kennedy, an event that, for their study's older participants, had happened more than a decade earlier. Respondents described their memories with striking confidence and detail. Brown and Kulik proposed that a special neural mechanism might essentially "print" these high-stakes moments into memory with unusual permanence.

Later researchers tested that idea directly, and the results complicated it considerably.

Are flashbulb memories actually more accurate?

One of the better-known tests came from psychologists Ulric Neisser and Nicole Harsch, who asked college students about the Space Shuttle Challenger disaster the morning after it happened in 1986, then asked the same students to recall it again roughly three years later. Confidence stayed high across both interviews. Accuracy did not: many participants' later accounts diverged noticeably from what they had reported the very next day, sometimes on basic details like who they were with or how they heard, and some were confident in versions of events that didn't match their own earlier report at all.

That pattern has shown up in other flashbulb-memory research since. People are consistently more confident in these memories than in ordinary ones, but that confidence doesn't track especially well with actual accuracy over time. The memory doesn't stay frozen the way a photograph does. It gets reconstructed a little differently each time it's recalled, the same way any other memory does, while still carrying the emotional certainty of the original moment.

Why do these moments feel so burned in, then?

A few factors keep showing up across the research. Surprise matters: an event you didn't see coming tends to trigger stronger encoding than one you expected. Emotional intensity matters too, especially for events that feel personally consequential or threatening. And how often you talk about or think back on an event afterward matters as well. Each retelling reinforces the sense that the memory is solid, even as the details underneath quietly shift a little with each retelling.

Personal relevance plays a role. National tragedies produce strong flashbulb memories in people who felt directly affected or geographically close to the event, and weaker ones in people farther removed, even when everyone heard the same news at roughly the same time. The vividness isn't really about the event's objective scale. It's about how much your own brain flagged that moment as mattering.

It's not only about national news

Most flashbulb-memory research uses public tragedies because they give researchers a fixed, verifiable starting point, everyone can be asked about the same event on the same timeline. But the phenomenon isn't limited to headlines. People commonly report flashbulb-style memories for intensely personal moments too: the birth of a child, a serious accident, a call with unexpected medical news, a proposal, a sudden death in the family. The underlying ingredients look the same, surprise, strong emotion and a sense that the moment mattered, whether the trigger was a nationally broadcast event or something that happened in one room with only a few people in it.

This is worth keeping in mind because it means the same caution about accuracy applies just as much to personal milestones as to world events. A memory feeling sharp and certain says more about how emotionally significant it felt at the time than about how precisely it's still being stored years later.

What's happening in the brain, roughly

Researchers generally point to the same systems involved in ordinary emotional memory, just working harder. Strong emotion activates the amygdala, a small structure involved in tagging experiences as significant, and that heightened arousal appears to strengthen how vividly the surrounding details get encoded in the hippocampus, the brain region central to forming new memories. That's a real, well-supported mechanism for why emotional moments tend to stick better than mundane ones in general, it isn't unique to flashbulb memories specifically. What it doesn't do is create some separate, more permanent storage format. The memory is still built, and rebuilt, using the same reconstructive process as everything else you remember, just with an emotional boost at the moment of encoding.

What this means for trusting your own memory

None of this means flashbulb memories are false. Most people do accurately remember the broad strokes of where they were and how they learned major news. What the research does suggest is that the specific details, and especially the unwavering certainty attached to them, deserve a little more skepticism than they typically get. Confidence and accuracy are two different things, and flashbulb memories are one of the clearest examples of how far apart they can drift.

That's actually consistent with how memory works generally, not an exception to it. Every act of recall involves some reconstruction rather than pure playback, which is part of why techniques like spaced repetition and consistent review matter for information you actually need to keep accurate over time, rather than relying on how vivid or certain a single memory feels. Frequently asked questions

What is a flashbulb memory?

It's an unusually vivid, detailed memory of the moment you learned about a surprising, emotionally significant event, such as where you were and who told you, rather than a memory of the event itself.

Who coined the term flashbulb memory?

Psychologists Roger Brown and James Kulik introduced the term in 1977, after studying how people recalled the moment they heard about President John F. Kennedy's assassination.

Are flashbulb memories more accurate than normal memories?

Not reliably. Research, including a well-known study on the Challenger disaster, found that people stay highly confident in these memories over years even when the specific details drift or change, so confidence doesn't guarantee accuracy.

Why do some events create flashbulb memories and others don't?

Surprise, emotional intensity and personal relevance appear to be the main drivers, along with how often the event gets discussed or replayed afterward, which reinforces the memory's vividness.

Can a flashbulb memory be wrong?

Yes. Studies that compare early and later recollections of the same event have found real inconsistencies, even though the person recalling the memory usually feels just as certain as ever.
