Hearing Loss and Dementia Risk: What the Evidence Shows

Untreated hearing loss ranks among the largest modifiable dementia risk factors researchers have identified. Here's why the link exists, what the trial data shows, and what to actually do about it.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published July 30, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Hearing loss is a major modifiable risk factor. Large studies rank it among the biggest single contributors to preventable dementia risk.
  • Three mechanisms likely drive the link: cognitive strain from decoding sound, faster brain changes from reduced input, and social withdrawal.
  • It's moderate to severe, untreated loss that carries the clearest risk. Mild loss and treated loss look different in the data.
  • Hearing aids may help. A major randomized trial found benefit for some higher-risk groups, though research is still developing.
  • Get tested proactively. Hearing loss creeps in slowly, and most people underestimate how much they've adapted around it.

If you're wondering whether hearing loss and memory problems are actually connected, the answer from the research is yes, and the connection is stronger than most people expect. Large population studies tracking thousands of adults over years have consistently found that moderate to severe untreated hearing loss shows up alongside a meaningfully higher rate of cognitive decline and dementia diagnosis later in life.

How strong is the link between hearing loss and dementia?

Strong enough that major dementia-prevention frameworks now list hearing loss as one of the largest single modifiable risk factors, alongside things like smoking, physical inactivity and social isolation. That doesn't mean hearing loss causes dementia in a simple, direct way. It means the two are statistically linked closely enough, across multiple large studies and different populations, that researchers take it seriously as a target for prevention.

The strength of the association also tends to scale with severity. Mild hearing loss shows a smaller association. Moderate to severe untreated loss shows a considerably larger one. That pattern (a dose-response relationship) is one of the things that makes researchers more confident this isn't just coincidence.

Why would hearing loss affect memory and thinking?

Researchers point to three mechanisms that likely work together. The first is cognitive load: when hearing is impaired, the brain has to work much harder to decode speech and fill in gaps, pulling mental resources away from encoding new memories and following complex conversation. Straining to understand someone at dinner isn't free. It borrows attention from everything else you're trying to hold in mind.

The second is a "use it or lose it" effect on brain structure. Some imaging studies suggest that reduced auditory input over years is associated with faster shrinkage in brain regions involved in memory and processing speed, though this remains an active area of research rather than settled fact.

The third mechanism is social. Hearing loss makes conversation exhausting, and many people respond by withdrawing from social situations rather than constantly asking others to repeat themselves. Social isolation is, on its own, a well-established dementia risk factor. Our guide on how social connection protects the aging brain covers why that link exists in more depth. Hearing loss can quietly feed into isolation, which then compounds the direct cognitive effect.

Do hearing aids actually reduce dementia risk?

The most encouraging evidence so far comes from a large randomized controlled trial that gave hearing aids to some participants and standard health education to others, then tracked cognitive change over years. Among people already at higher risk for cognitive decline, those who used hearing aids showed meaningfully slower decline than those who didn't. The effect was less clear in lower-risk participants, which suggests the benefit may be biggest for people who need it most.

This is still an evolving area, and one trial doesn't settle the question completely. But given that treating hearing loss carries essentially no downside and plenty of quality-of-life benefits on its own, it's a reasonable, low-risk step for anyone with diagnosed hearing loss to take seriously.

Who should get a hearing test, and when?

Hearing loss develops so gradually that most people don't notice the full extent of it. A general guideline is a baseline hearing test in your 50s or 60s, sooner if you notice yourself asking people to repeat themselves, turning the TV up louder than others need, or struggling to follow conversation in restaurants and other noisy settings. Family history of hearing loss and a history of loud noise exposure both raise the case for testing earlier.

A hearing test is quick, painless and often available through an audiologist or ENT without a lengthy wait. If loss is identified, treatment options range from hearing aids to assistive devices to addressing an underlying medical cause, and an audiologist can walk through what fits your specific situation.

What else protects memory alongside treating hearing loss?

Hearing care works best as one piece of a broader picture. Regular aerobic exercise, consistent sleep, and staying mentally and socially engaged all have strong, independent evidence for protecting memory as you age. Frequently asked questions

Does hearing loss really increase dementia risk?

Multiple large studies have found that moderate to severe untreated hearing loss is associated with a meaningfully higher risk of cognitive decline and dementia. It's classified as one of the largest single modifiable risk factors identified in major dementia-prevention research. It's a risk factor, not a guarantee, and treating hearing loss appears to reduce that added risk.

Why would hearing loss affect memory and the brain?

Three mechanisms are thought to combine: the brain devotes more resources to decoding sound, leaving less for memory and thinking; reduced sound input may speed up shrinkage in memory-related brain regions; and hearing loss often leads to social withdrawal, and isolation is itself a known dementia risk factor.

Do hearing aids actually lower dementia risk?

Growing evidence, including a large randomized trial, suggests hearing aid use can slow cognitive decline in people at higher risk, particularly older adults with other risk factors. Research is still developing, but treating hearing loss looks like one of the more promising, low-risk steps available.

When should I get my hearing checked?

Get a baseline hearing test in your 50s or 60s, or sooner if you notice asking people to repeat themselves, turning up the volume more than others need, or struggling to follow conversation in noisy settings. Hearing loss develops gradually, so many people don't notice how much they've adapted around it.
