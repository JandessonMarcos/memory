The Serial Position Effect: Why You Remember the Start and End of a List

Ask someone to recall a list a minute after hearing it, and a pattern shows up almost every time. They nail the beginning and the end. The middle is where things go missing.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published September 12, 2026

Photo: Kenneth Allen / Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA 2.0

Key takeaways

  • The serial position effect is the tendency to recall the first and last items in a list better than the ones in the middle.
  • It's made of two separate effects: the primacy effect (early items) and the recency effect (late items).
  • Primacy happens because early items get more rehearsal time and a better shot at reaching long-term memory.
  • Recency happens because the last items are still sitting in short-term memory when you're asked to recall them.
  • You can use this deliberately by putting your most important point first, your second most important point last, and skipping the middle for anything that has to land.

The serial position effect is the well-documented tendency to remember the first few and last few items in a list far better than whatever came in between. Ask a room full of people to recall a list of 15 words a minute after hearing it, and the results plot out as a U-shaped curve almost every time: strong recall at the start, a dip through the middle, and a second peak at the end.

What is the serial position effect?

It's actually two effects working at once, and researchers usually name them separately: the primacy effect (better recall for early items) and the recency effect (better recall for the most recent items). Middle items get squeezed from both sides. By the time you reach them, the early items have already used up some of your attention, and by the time the list ends, the middle items have been pushed out by everything that came after.

The pattern shows up outside the lab constantly. It's why you can usually recite the first line of a poem and the last line, but blank on the third stanza. It's why people remember the opening and closing acts of a conference more clearly than the sessions in between.

Where the research comes from

Serial position effects were noted as far back as Hermann Ebbinghaus's memory experiments in the 1880s, but the modern, more detailed picture comes largely from free-recall studies in the 1960s, most notably work by psychologist Bennet Murdock. Participants heard a list of words read aloud and then wrote down as many as they could remember, in any order. Plotting recall accuracy against each word's position in the list produced that same U shape again and again, across list lengths and presentation speeds.

One detail from that research turned out to matter a lot: if you add a short distraction task, like counting backward, between hearing the list and recalling it, the recency effect mostly disappears while the primacy effect stays intact. That single finding is a big part of why researchers believe the two effects come from different memory systems rather than one shared mechanism.

Why the beginning sticks: the primacy effect

Early items in a list get an advantage that has nothing to do with where they sit and everything to do with timing. When you hear the first item, it has your full, undivided rehearsal capacity. You can silently repeat it a few times before the second item arrives. By item six or seven, your attention is split between holding onto what already came and processing what's new, so nothing gets the same depth of rehearsal the first couple of items got.

That extra rehearsal is what's thought to push early items from short-term into long-term memory more reliably. It's also why the primacy effect survives a delay or a distraction task: once something has made it into long-term storage, a few minutes of counting backward doesn't erase it.

Why the end sticks: the recency effect

The last few items in a list benefit from a completely different mechanism. When you're asked to recall the list immediately, those items are often still sitting in short-term memory, essentially still active and easy to pull up because you haven't had to hold onto them for long. There's no long-term storage involved yet, which is exactly why the recency effect is fragile. Interrupt it with even a short delay or a distracting task, and it drops off fast, while the primacy effect barely moves.

This is a useful diagnostic in its own right. If someone can recall the end of a list right after hearing it but loses that advantage after a short delay, that's consistent with normal short-term memory doing its job, not a warning sign.

The middle: where things actually get lost

Middle items lose on both counts. They don't get the rehearsal advantage of the earliest items, because attention is already split by the time they arrive. And they don't get the freshness advantage of the last items, because more information has piled up after them by the time you're asked to recall anything. Nothing dramatic is happening; it's simply the point in the sequence with the least support from either mechanism.

How to actually use this

Once you know the shape of the curve, you can work with it instead of against it. A few practical applications:

  • Presentations and pitches: put your single most important point first and your second most important point last. Don't bury the ask in slide 12 of 20.
  • Studying: the middle of a study session is where information leaks out fastest, so take a short break partway through to effectively create a new "start" and "end," or use chunking to break a long list into shorter ones, each with its own primacy and recency boost.
  • Meetings and interviews: people remember the first and last few minutes of a conversation more clearly than the middle. If something needs to land, don't schedule it for minute 25 of a 40-minute meeting.
  • Lists and instructions: if every item genuinely matters, don't rely on serial position alone. Combine it with mnemonic devices or repetition for the items stuck in the middle.

None of this requires memorizing the research. The practical version is simple: whatever you want someone to remember, put it near the beginning or the very end, and give the middle extra help if it has to compete for attention.

Does this apply to everyday memory, not just lab lists?

Yes, with a caveat. The clean U-shaped curve shows up most clearly with the kind of list psychologists use in a lab: simple, unrelated items, read at a steady pace. Real-world material, a conversation, a chapter, a grocery run, has more structure and more meaning attached to it, and meaning is one of the strongest predictors of what actually gets remembered, arguably stronger than position. Serial position is a real, reliable bias, not the only thing determining what sticks. Frequently asked questions

What is the serial position effect in simple terms?

It's the tendency to remember the first and last items in a list better than the ones in the middle, producing a U-shaped pattern when you plot recall accuracy against each item's position.

What's the difference between the primacy and recency effects?

The primacy effect is better recall for early items, thought to happen because they get more rehearsal time and a better chance of reaching long-term memory. The recency effect is better recall for the last items, because they're still active in short-term memory at the moment of recall.

Why do people forget the middle of a list?

Middle items miss out on both advantages: they don't get the extra rehearsal that early items get, and they've been pushed out of short-term memory by the time later items arrive.

Does a delay affect the serial position effect?

It affects the recency effect specifically. A short delay or distraction task between hearing a list and recalling it tends to wipe out the recency advantage while leaving the primacy effect mostly intact, which is part of why researchers believe the two rely on different memory systems.

How can I use the serial position effect in real life?

Put your most important point first and your second most important point last in a presentation, list, or conversation. For material where every item matters, break it into shorter chunks or add repetition so the middle isn't left to compete unaided.
