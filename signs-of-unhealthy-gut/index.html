Signs of an Unhealthy Gut: What Your Body Might Be Telling You

Your gut rarely stays quiet when something's off. The clues often show up well before any diagnosis, and not always in your stomach.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published October 21, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Ongoing bloating, gas, or irregular bowel habits are the most common signs of an unhealthy gut, especially when they show up most days rather than after one big meal.
  • Fatigue, skin flare-ups, and sugar cravings can also trace back to the gut, since gut bacteria influence inflammation, nutrient absorption, and even mood-related chemicals.
  • A low-fiber diet, frequent antibiotic use, chronic stress, and poor sleep are common drivers of an out-of-balance gut microbiome.
  • There's no single home test that reliably diagnoses an unhealthy gut. Consumer microbiome kits aren't yet validated for clinical decisions.
  • See a doctor for blood in the stool, unexplained weight loss, pain that wakes you at night, or symptoms lasting more than a few weeks.

The clearest signs of an unhealthy gut are ongoing bloating, gas, or irregular bowel habits, along with fatigue, skin flare-ups, sugar cravings, and getting sick more often than usual. None of these symptoms alone proves a problem. Together, and over weeks rather than days, they're worth paying attention to.

Your gut does more than digest lunch. It houses trillions of bacteria that help break down fiber, make certain vitamins, and communicate constantly with your immune system and your brain. When that ecosystem gets knocked off balance, a state researchers often call dysbiosis, the fallout can show up in places that have nothing to do with your stomach.

What counts as "unhealthy" here usually isn't one harmful bacterium doing damage. It's more often about diversity: a wider range of bacterial species tends to support steadier digestion and a more even immune response, while a narrower, less varied population shows up more often in people with digestive complaints. Researchers still debate how much diversity actually counts as "enough," which is part of why no single test can hand you a clean verdict.

What are the most common signs of an unhealthy gut?

Most people notice digestive symptoms first, since that's where gut trouble usually starts. But an unhealthy gut tends to announce itself in more than one system at once.

  • Bloating, gas, or stomach cramping that shows up most days, not just after a big meal
  • Diarrhea, constipation, or bowel habits that swing between the two
  • Unexplained fatigue that a full night's sleep doesn't fix
  • Skin issues such as acne, eczema, or rosacea flare-ups
  • New or worsening reactions to foods you used to tolerate fine
  • Getting colds or infections more often than people around you
  • Strong cravings for sugar or refined carbs
  • Unintentional weight changes without a real change in diet
  • Bad breath that brushing doesn't solve
  • Trouble falling or staying asleep

No single item on that list is diagnostic. Plenty of people get occasional bloating or one bad night's sleep for reasons that have nothing to do with their gut. What tends to matter more is the pattern: several of these showing up together, lasting for weeks, and not explained by something else going on.

How does digestion itself change when your gut is out of balance?

When gut bacteria shift out of their usual balance, a few things tend to happen at once. Certain bacteria ferment undigested carbohydrates and produce extra gas, which shows up as bloating and cramping. Changes in the microbial mix can also speed up or slow down gut motility, the muscle contractions that move food along, which is part of why symptoms can swing between loose stools and constipation rather than settling on one or the other.

Those ranges are general guidelines, not a diagnosis. Bowel habits vary a lot between healthy people, and what matters most is a change from your own usual pattern, not matching someone else's.

Some people who notice this pattern for months eventually meet the clinical criteria for irritable bowel syndrome, which a doctor diagnoses from the pattern of symptoms rather than from a single test. A temporarily out-of-balance gut and IBS can look very similar on the surface, which is one more reason persistent symptoms deserve a real evaluation rather than a guess from an article.

Can skin problems really trace back to gut health?

There's a real, if still developing, link between gut bacteria and skin, sometimes called the gut-skin axis. Several studies have found that people with acne, eczema, or rosacea are more likely to show differences in their gut bacteria compared with people who don't have those conditions. One leading theory is that an imbalanced gut can increase low-grade inflammation throughout the body, and skin is one of the places that inflammation tends to show up.

This research is mostly observational, meaning it shows a connection without fully proving that gut changes cause the skin flare-up, rather than the other way around, or both being driven by something else like diet or stress. Still, a skin issue that resists the usual topical treatments and comes along with digestive symptoms is a reasonable thing to mention at a doctor's visit.

Diet sits at the center of this triangle more often than any single gut bacterium does. A diet heavy in added sugar and refined carbs has its own documented association with acne, independent of gut bacteria, which makes it genuinely hard to tease apart how much of a flare-up is "gut" versus diet acting on the skin more directly.

Why do fatigue and brain fog show up as gut symptoms?

Your gut and brain are in constant two-way contact through the vagus nerve and a steady stream of chemical signals. Gut bacteria help produce or regulate several compounds that affect mood and alertness, including a large share of the body's serotonin, even though most of that gut-made serotonin works locally rather than reaching the brain directly.

There's also a more mechanical explanation. An out-of-balance gut can interfere with how well you absorb certain nutrients, including iron and B vitamins, and low levels of either are well-established causes of fatigue on their own. If tiredness is your main complaint, it's worth reading about how iron deficiency and poor sleep each show up as brain fog, since gut health is one of several overlapping explanations rather than the only one.

Do new food intolerances mean something changed in your gut?

Sometimes. A gut thrown off by a round of antibiotics, a stomach infection, or a long stretch of poor eating can temporarily lose some of the bacteria that help break down specific foods, which is one reason people occasionally develop a new sensitivity to dairy or high-fiber foods after being sick. This is different from a true food allergy, which involves the immune system and can be dangerous; an intolerance is usually uncomfortable rather than risky.

Keeping a simple food and symptom diary for a couple of weeks is more useful than guessing. It helps separate a real pattern, like bloating an hour after dairy, from coincidence, and gives your doctor something concrete to work with if symptoms don't settle on their own.

What usually causes these changes in the first place?

  • A diet low in fiber and high in ultra-processed foods. Fiber is what feeds beneficial gut bacteria; without enough of it, that population tends to shrink. Our piece on ultra-processed food covers the wider pattern.
  • Antibiotics. They kill harmful bacteria but also reduce helpful ones, and recovery can take anywhere from a few weeks to several months depending on the course and the person.
  • Chronic stress. Stress hormones can change gut motility and the makeup of gut bacteria, part of the same gut-brain loop mentioned earlier.
  • Poor or irregular sleep. Gut bacteria follow something like their own daily rhythm, and disrupted sleep appears to disturb it.
  • Low physical activity. Regular movement is linked to a more diverse gut bacterial population in several studies.
  • Heavy alcohol use. It can irritate the gut lining and shift bacterial balance over time.
  • Underlying digestive conditions. IBS, celiac disease, and inflammatory bowel disease all involve gut changes and need their own diagnosis and care.

It's worth noting these causes usually stack rather than acting one at a time. Someone finishing a course of antibiotics while also under heavy work stress and eating less fiber than usual is dealing with three gut-disrupting pressures at once, which is part of why recovery can take longer than any single cause would suggest on its own.

How is gut health actually tested?

There isn't one simple, universally accepted test that tells a healthy person "your gut is unhealthy" with a single number. Mail-in microbiome tests have become popular, but major medical groups note that the science connecting a specific bacterial profile to a specific health outcome, for most people, isn't developed enough yet to guide treatment decisions on its own.

What a doctor can actually do is more targeted. Depending on your symptoms, that might include a stool test for infection or inflammation markers like calprotectin, blood work to check for celiac disease or anemia, or a referral to a gastroenterologist if symptoms are persistent, severe, or don't fit a simple explanation. That's a very different process from an at-home kit, and it's the one that can change how you're treated.

Part of the gap is cost and standardization. Mail-in kits vary in which bacteria they report on, use different lab methods, and compare your sample against different reference databases, so two kits run on the same stool sample can come back with meaningfully different results. That's a quality-control problem researchers are still working through, not something a consumer test can currently promise to solve.

What helps support a healthier gut?

  • Eat a range of plant foods, not just more of one. Different fibers feed different bacteria, so variety across the week matters more than any single "superfood."
  • Include fermented foods when you tolerate them. Yogurt, kefir, kimchi, and sauerkraut supply live cultures; our fermented foods article goes into the gut-brain evidence.
  • Cut back on ultra-processed foods where you can. They tend to crowd out fiber and come loaded with additives some early research ties to gut changes.
  • Stay hydrated. Water helps fiber do its job and keeps stool from becoming too hard.
  • Move regularly. Even brisk walking is linked to a more favorable gut bacterial mix.
  • Protect your sleep and manage stress. Both feed back into the gut-brain loop described above.
  • Use antibiotics only when they're actually needed. Ask your doctor if a suspected viral infection really calls for one.

Probiotic supplements get a lot of attention, and some strains have decent evidence for specific situations, like preventing antibiotic-associated diarrhea. They're not a universal fix, and "probiotic" on a label covers a wide range of strains and doses with very different research behind each one. Our probiotics explainer walks through what the evidence actually supports.

None of this needs to be perfect to help. Small, sustained changes, like adding one more vegetable at dinner or swapping soda for water most days, tend to add up faster than a short-lived, strict overhaul that's hard to keep going for more than a week or two.

When should you see a doctor about gut symptoms?

Most gut-related discomfort is manageable and improves with time and a few diet or lifestyle changes. Certain signs deserve a prompt call instead of a wait-and-see approach: blood in the stool, unexplained weight loss, pain severe enough to wake you at night, a fever alongside digestive symptoms, or any change that's lasted more than two to three weeks without improving. A family history of inflammatory bowel disease or colon cancer is also a good reason to bring symptoms up sooner rather than later.

None of this is a substitute for an actual exam. A short visit that rules out something serious, or catches something treatable early, is almost always worth more than months of guessing from symptoms alone. For more on how the gut connects to the rest of the body, see our gut health coverage.

Frequently asked questions

Can stress alone cause gut symptoms?

Yes. The gut and brain communicate constantly, and stress hormones can change gut motility and bacterial balance even without any change in diet.

Is bloating after every meal normal?

Occasional bloating after a large or rich meal is common. Bloating that happens most days, or isn't tied to what you ate, is more worth mentioning to a doctor.

Do probiotics fix an unhealthy gut?

Sometimes they help, particularly for specific situations like antibiotic-associated diarrhea, but they're not a universal fix. Strain, dose, and the reason you're taking one all matter.

Can antibiotics damage your gut long-term?

They typically reduce beneficial bacteria along with harmful ones, and most people's gut bacteria recover within weeks to a few months. Repeated or long courses can take longer to bounce back from.

Is gut health connected to immunity?

Yes. A large share of the body's immune tissue sits along the digestive tract, and gut bacteria help train and regulate immune responses.

How long does it take to notice a difference after changing your diet?

Some people notice less bloating within days of cutting back on a trigger food. Broader changes in gut bacteria generally take a few weeks of consistent habits to show up.

Can an unhealthy gut cause weight changes?

It can play a role indirectly, through effects on appetite-regulating hormones, cravings, and nutrient absorption, though weight change usually has more than one cause worth looking into.
