GPS Navigation and Spatial Memory: Is Your Phone Dulling Your Sense of Direction?

You haven't had to actually find your way anywhere in years. Turn-by-turn directions handle it. Here's what the research says that habit is doing to the part of your brain that used to do the job.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published October 7, 2026

Photo: Artem Beliaikin / Wikimedia Commons / CC0

Key takeaways

  • Brain-imaging studies of London taxi drivers found larger hippocampi in those who had memorized the city's street layout without GPS, compared with people who hadn't done that kind of training.
  • Behavioral studies link heavier GPS use with weaker performance on wayfinding tasks done without assistance, though most of this evidence is observational.
  • The proposed mechanism is practice, not damage. Spatial memory, like most skills, seems to respond to how often it's actually exercised.
  • This isn't unique to phones. Researchers raised similar questions about calculators and long division decades ago.
  • Occasional GPS use for an unfamiliar city is not a problem. The research concern is substituting it for wayfinding entirely, all the time.
  • Sense of direction appears to be trainable at any age by deliberately building a mental map instead of following arrows.

Short answer: the evidence doesn't show that GPS breaks your brain, but it does show that the brain regions used for wayfinding respond to practice, and GPS removes most of that practice from daily life. Several independent lines of research point the same direction without quite proving cause and effect in either one.

Does using GPS actually weaken spatial memory?

The most-cited evidence here comes from brain scans of licensed London taxi drivers, who spend years memorizing roughly 25,000 streets to pass "The Knowledge," the city's famously brutal cabbie exam. Researchers found their posterior hippocampi, a region tied to spatial representation, were measurably larger than in comparison groups, and the effect grew with years on the job. That's a striking demonstration that intense, sustained spatial learning can reshape this part of the brain. It says nothing directly about what happens when someone follows a phone instead.

The GPS-specific research is smaller and newer, but points the same way. Studies that track people's brain activity and behavior during wayfinding tasks have found that habitual GPS users tend to engage hippocampal-dependent strategies less, and rely more on simpler route-following, than people who navigate from memory. Several behavioral studies have also found that people who use turn-by-turn directions regularly perform worse afterward on tasks that ask them to recall or redraw a route they just took. None of this is a randomized trial proving GPS causes decline. It's a consistent correlation across different research groups, which carries real weight even without that proof.

What is spatial memory, and why does the hippocampus do this work?

Spatial memory is the system that builds and stores a mental map of your surroundings: where things are relative to each other, which way you came from, how to get back. The hippocampus, working with nearby regions, contains cells that researchers have literally named for this job. "Place cells" fire when you're in a specific location. "Grid cells" fire in a repeating pattern that functions something like internal coordinates. Together they let you build what scientists call a cognitive map, an internal layout of a space that you can mentally rotate and search without actually being there.

Turn-by-turn directions work differently. Instead of building that map, you're following a sequence of instructions: turn left, then right, then arrive. It gets you there reliably, but it asks much less of the mapping system. Over many trips, that's a lot of skipped reps for a skill that, like most skills, seems to need repetition to stay sharp.

Is this actually something to worry about?

Mostly, no. Needing GPS in an unfamiliar city isn't a red flag at any age, it's just a sensible use of a useful tool. The pattern researchers are more interested in is what happens with long-term, near-total reliance: people who no longer build mental maps of even familiar areas because an app has always done it for them. That's a different thing than looking up directions to a new restaurant once.

There's also a second, less discussed layer. Some researchers argue that wayfinding is a form of what's called cognitive reserve, the kind of regular mental effort that keeps a brain network active and, some evidence suggests, more resilient over decades. Our piece on cognitive reserve goes into that idea in more depth, including where the evidence is solid and where it's still speculative.

The London taxi driver studies don't prove GPS damages your brain. They prove that heavy spatial practice changes it, which raises an obvious question about what happens when that practice disappears.

Can you rebuild a sense of direction if you've relied on GPS for years?

Probably, at least to some degree. Spatial skills aren't fixed at a set number by age twenty-five. Small studies on wayfinding training have found that people who deliberately practice navigating without directions, paying attention to landmarks and building a route from memory, improve their performance on spatial tasks over weeks. Nothing has tested this as a formal treatment for cognitive decline, so treat it as a reasonable mental habit rather than a medical intervention.

A simple way to start: pick a route you already know reasonably well, put the phone away, and navigate it from memory, checking the map only if you're genuinely stuck. Over time, try that with routes you know less well. Frequently asked questions

Does using GPS actually shrink the hippocampus?

No single trip will shrink anything. The research that gets cited here is about long-term patterns: London taxi drivers who memorized the city's streets without GPS showed measurably larger posterior hippocampi than controls. That's evidence that heavy, sustained spatial practice can change brain structure, not proof that using a map app does the opposite.

Is relying on GPS a sign of early dementia?

No. Needing directions in an unfamiliar city is completely normal at any age and isn't a warning sign on its own. Getting lost in long-familiar, routine places, like your own neighborhood, is the kind of change worth mentioning to a doctor.

Can you rebuild a sense of direction if you've relied on GPS for years?

Spatial skills respond to practice at any age, though nothing has been studied as a formal medical treatment. People who deliberately navigate without turn-by-turn prompts, building a mental map instead of following arrows, tend to perform better on wayfinding tasks than habitual GPS users.

Does this apply to all GPS users equally?

Not quite. Studies have found that people vary a lot in baseline sense of direction, and the ones who already navigate by landmarks and mental maps tend to keep doing so even with a phone in hand. The effect shows up most clearly in people who use turn-by-turn directions as a full substitute for paying attention to the route.
