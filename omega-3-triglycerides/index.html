Omega-3 and Triglycerides: What the Research Shows

Omega-3 fatty acids are one of the better-studied ways to bring down a high triglyceride number. Here's the dose that actually works, and where the evidence runs thin.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published November 12, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Prescription-strength omega-3, 2 to 4 grams of EPA and DHA daily, lowers triglycerides by roughly 20 to 50 percent in people who start out high.
  • Lower, food-level doses barely move the number. The effect is dose-dependent, and most dietary intake falls well short of the therapeutic range.
  • Some mixed EPA/DHA formulas can modestly raise LDL, while purified EPA-only prescription products generally don't.
  • The benefit is strongest for people with genuinely elevated triglycerides, not for someone whose level is already normal.
  • Cutting added sugar and alcohol often moves triglycerides as much as supplements do, and it's usually the first step.

Omega-3 and triglycerides have one of the more solidly documented relationships in nutrition research. At high enough doses, omega-3 fatty acids lower triglycerides by roughly 20 to 50 percent in people who start out elevated, an effect strong enough that prescription omega-3 medications exist specifically for this purpose. The catch is dose: the amount that actually moves the number is higher than what most people get from fish or a standard supplement bottle.

Here's what the research actually supports, what dose it takes, how long it takes to work, and where fish oil and prescription omega-3 part ways.

What are triglycerides, and why do they matter?

Triglycerides are the main form of fat circulating in your blood, built from extra calories your body doesn't use right away for energy. They rise after eating, particularly after meals high in sugar, refined carbs or alcohol, and settle back down between meals in most people. A fasting triglyceride level under 150 mg/dL is considered normal; 150 to 199 is borderline high, 200 to 499 is high, and 500 or above is very high and carries its own risk of pancreatitis, separate from heart disease risk.

Elevated triglycerides often travel alongside low HDL and insulin resistance, a cluster sometimes called atherogenic dyslipidemia. Our guide to LDL vs HDL cholesterol covers how triglycerides fit into the rest of a standard lipid panel.

How much do omega-3s actually lower triglycerides?

Dose (EPA + DHA)Typical triglyceride change
Under 1 gram daily (typical food intake)Small, often not clinically meaningful
1 to 2 grams dailyModest reduction, roughly 5 to 15 percent in some trials
2 to 4 grams daily (prescription range)20 to 50 percent reduction in people starting out elevated

The relationship is dose-dependent and most pronounced in people whose triglycerides are already high. Someone starting at a normal level has little room to drop further and typically sees a much smaller change, if any, at the same dose.

Is fish oil the same thing as a prescription omega-3 medication?

Not quite. Over-the-counter fish oil capsules vary a lot in how much EPA and DHA they actually contain per capsule, and purity and freshness vary by brand. Prescription omega-3 medications are standardized, tested at specific doses in large randomized trials, and some are purified to contain EPA only, with little or no DHA. That distinction turns out to matter for a side effect discussed next.

Can omega-3 supplements raise LDL cholesterol?

Sometimes, depending on the formula. Mixed EPA/DHA products, the kind found in most fish oil supplements, have been shown in some studies to modestly raise LDL in certain people, particularly at higher doses. Purified EPA-only prescription formulations generally don't show this effect, which is one reason a doctor might choose one over the other for someone who already has high LDL alongside high triglycerides. This is worth discussing before starting a high-dose regimen rather than assuming all omega-3 products behave the same way on a lipid panel.

How long does it take to see a change?

Most clinical trials measuring omega-3's effect on triglycerides see meaningful change within 4 to 8 weeks of consistent dosing. A repeat fasting lipid panel around that point is the usual way to check whether a given dose is working for a specific person, since individual responses vary more than for some other lipid-lowering strategies.

Do I need high triglycerides to benefit, or does everyone see a drop?

The effect scales with how elevated triglycerides are to begin with. Large trials recruit people with triglycerides well above normal specifically because that's where the measurable benefit shows up most clearly. Someone with a triglyceride level already in the normal range taking the same dose is working with less room to improve, and most research doesn't support a meaningful further reduction in that case.

What about omega-3 and heart disease risk more broadly?

This is a more contested area than the triglyceride-lowering effect itself. Some large trials of prescription-strength EPA have found a reduction in cardiovascular events in people with high triglycerides and other risk factors, while other omega-3 trials, often using lower doses or different formulations, have found little to no cardiovascular benefit. The triglyceride-lowering effect is well established; whether that translates into fewer heart attacks depends heavily on the specific product, dose and population studied, and researchers are still working out why results differ as much as they do between trials.

What side effects come with high-dose omega-3?

  • Fishy aftertaste or burping, often reduced by taking the dose with food or choosing an enteric-coated capsule
  • Mild digestive upset, nausea or loose stools, especially when starting a new dose
  • Increased bleeding risk at high doses, a real consideration for anyone on blood thinners or scheduled for surgery
  • A possible, small increase in LDL with mixed EPA/DHA formulas, as covered above

None of these are usually dangerous, but they're worth mentioning to a doctor before starting a prescription-strength dose, particularly the bleeding risk if you take aspirin or an anticoagulant already.

Does diet alone lower triglycerides as well as supplements?

For many people, diet changes move the number more than they expect, and they're the first step most doctors recommend regardless of whether omega-3 gets added later. Added sugar and alcohol are two of the biggest dietary drivers of high triglycerides, more so than dietary fat itself for most people. Cutting back on both, along with losing excess weight and increasing physical activity, often produces a meaningful drop on its own. Our guide to lowering blood pressure naturally covers several of the same lifestyle habits, since they overlap heavily with triglyceride management.

The dose makes the difference. A fish oil capsule at dinner and a prescription-strength regimen are not the same intervention, even though the bottle says the same two words.

Does omega-3 do anything for the brain, not just the heart?

Yes, though that's a separate line of research from triglycerides specifically. DHA is a major structural fat in brain cell membranes, and omega-3 intake has its own body of research tied to cognitive health, covered in more depth in our guide to omega-3 and brain health. The triglyceride-lowering effect discussed here is a distinct, heart-focused benefit; the two aren't the same claim, even though they come from the same fatty acids.

Who should talk to a doctor before starting high-dose omega-3?

Anyone with triglycerides over 500 mg/dL should be under a doctor's care regardless, given the pancreatitis risk at that level. People on blood thinners, those with a bleeding disorder, anyone scheduled for surgery soon, and people already being treated for high LDL should also check in before starting a prescription-strength dose, since the formulation and monitoring plan matter more at this level than at a basic dietary-supplement dose.

Pregnant and breastfeeding women, and anyone managing diabetes alongside high triglycerides, should also loop in a doctor rather than self-dosing from an over-the-counter bottle. Triglycerides and blood sugar interact closely, and a treatment plan that only looks at one number can miss something a doctor would otherwise catch early.

How often should triglycerides be rechecked once you start treatment?

Once someone starts a high-dose omega-3 regimen for elevated triglycerides, a repeat fasting lipid panel at around 6 to 8 weeks is typical, matching the timeline most trials use to see a measurable change. After that, many doctors move to checking every few months initially, then stretch to roughly once or twice a year once the number stabilizes in a safer range. This isn't a one-time test and done; triglycerides respond to short-term changes in diet, weight and alcohol intake, so a single good result doesn't mean the underlying pattern is fixed for good.

People who stop a high-dose regimen, whether on their own or under medical guidance, usually see triglycerides drift back toward their pre-treatment level over weeks, not months, since omega-3's effect doesn't appear to build any lasting structural change the way some other interventions might. That's worth knowing going in: this is a treatment to maintain, not a short course that fixes the number permanently.

Frequently asked questions

How much do omega-3s actually lower triglycerides?

Prescription-strength doses, typically 2 to 4 grams of combined EPA and DHA daily, lower triglycerides by roughly 20 to 50 percent in people who start out high. Lower, food-level intakes produce a much smaller effect.

Can fish oil supplements raise LDL cholesterol?

Some mixed EPA/DHA formulas modestly raise LDL in certain people, especially at higher doses. Purified EPA-only prescription products are generally associated with little to no LDL increase, which is one reason a doctor may prefer one formulation over another.

How long does it take for omega-3 to lower triglycerides?

Most clinical trials see meaningful drops within 4 to 8 weeks of consistent, adequate dosing. A follow-up lipid panel around that point is typical to see whether the dose is working.

Is fish oil the same as a prescription omega-3 medication?

No. Over-the-counter fish oil varies widely in EPA and DHA content and purity. Prescription omega-3 medications are regulated, standardized in dose and tested in large trials, which matters most for people with very high triglycerides.

Do I need high triglycerides to benefit from omega-3?

The triglyceride-lowering effect is strongest in people who start out elevated. Someone with normal triglycerides is unlikely to see a meaningful further drop, since there's less room for the number to fall.

What side effects come with high-dose omega-3?

Fishy aftertaste, mild digestive upset and burping are common and usually manageable by taking it with food or splitting the dose. High doses can also increase bleeding risk, which matters for anyone on blood thinners.

Does diet alone lower triglycerides as well as omega-3 supplements?

Cutting added sugar and alcohol often has a bigger effect on triglycerides than diet changes alone usually get credit for, and it's the first step most doctors recommend before or alongside omega-3 treatment.
