Magnesium for Women: Benefits, Daily Needs and Best Food Sources

Magnesium touches far more than muscle cramps. It plays a role in sleep, mood, bone density and blood sugar, and a lot of women fall short of the target without ever being told.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published November 14, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Most women need about 310 to 320 mg of magnesium a day, rising to 350 to 360 mg during pregnancy.
  • Magnesium supports muscle and nerve function, bone density, blood sugar regulation and sleep, not just "energy."
  • A true deficiency is uncommon in healthy women, though many people eat less than the target without any obvious symptoms.
  • Leafy greens, nuts, seeds, beans and whole grains are the best sources, and food should come before supplements.
  • The evidence for magnesium easing PMS symptoms is mixed, with small trials showing some benefit and larger reviews calling for better research.

Most women need roughly 310 to 320 mg of magnesium a day, a little more during pregnancy. It supports muscle and nerve function, bone density, blood sugar regulation and sleep. A true deficiency is uncommon in healthy women eating a varied diet, but plenty of people fall short of the target without reaching dangerously low levels.

Here's how the daily target breaks down by life stage, which foods close the gap, and where magnesium genuinely helps versus where the evidence is still thin.

How much magnesium do women actually need?

Life stageRecommended daily magnesium
Women, 19–30 years310 mg
Women, 31–50 years320 mg
Women, 51 and older320 mg
Pregnant350–360 mg
Breastfeeding310–320 mg

These figures, set by the National Academies and published by the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, count magnesium from every source: food, beverages and any supplement. For supplements and medication alone, there's a separate upper limit of 350 mg a day, since high-dose supplemental magnesium is more likely to cause diarrhea than the amount naturally found in food. Despite that gap between the target and the upper limit, surveys consistently find that a meaningful share of women don't hit even the lower, recommended amount from food alone, particularly women who eat fewer than the recommended servings of vegetables, legumes and whole grains most days.

What does magnesium actually do in the body?

It's involved in more than 300 enzyme reactions, including energy production, protein synthesis, and muscle and nerve signaling. It helps regulate blood pressure and blood sugar, supports normal heart rhythm, and works alongside calcium and vitamin D to build and maintain bone. That breadth is exactly why low intake can show up as several unrelated-seeming issues at once: muscle cramps, poor sleep, low mood or irregular heartbeat, rather than one clear symptom.

Because magnesium is involved in so many systems at once, a mild shortfall rarely produces a single, obvious red flag the way a vitamin C or iron deficiency might. Instead it tends to show up as several small, easy-to-dismiss things happening together: a cramp that wakes you up at night, a stretch of restless sleep, or feeling more wound up than usual under ordinary stress. None of those symptoms point to magnesium on their own, which is part of why low intake is so often overlooked rather than diagnosed.

What are the best food sources of magnesium?

  • Pumpkin seeds: roughly 150 mg per ounce, one of the most concentrated common sources.
  • Almonds and cashews: around 80 mg per ounce.
  • Cooked spinach and other leafy greens: about 75–80 mg per half-cup.
  • Black beans and other legumes: roughly 60 mg per half-cup, cooked.
  • Whole grains, including brown rice and oats: 40–60 mg per serving.
  • Dark chocolate (70% cocoa or higher): around 50 mg per ounce.

A diet built around a few of these most days gets most women close to their target without a supplement at all.

Does magnesium help with PMS or menstrual cramps?

The evidence here is genuinely mixed. A small older trial found magnesium eased premenstrual mood symptoms, and some women report less cramping and bloating. But a broader review of the research concluded the trials were too small and inconsistent to recommend magnesium confidently for PMS, and noted that calcium currently has the stronger evidence of the two minerals for this specific use. It's reasonable to try it for a cycle or two if you're curious, but it shouldn't replace other PMS strategies that have firmer support.

Does magnesium help prevent migraines?

This is one of magnesium's more credible, if still debated, uses. A 2012 guideline from the American Academy of Neurology and the American Headache Society rated oral magnesium "probably effective" for preventing episodic migraine, a Level B rating, based on a handful of controlled trials. More recent systematic reviews have been more cautious, downgrading the evidence to "possibly effective" and noting the trials behind it are small and not consistently replicated. Studies generally used 400 to 600 mg of oral magnesium daily. Women who get frequent migraines might reasonably discuss a trial of magnesium with their doctor, understanding that the evidence, while genuinely promising, isn't as strong as it's sometimes made out to be in supplement marketing.

Can magnesium help with perimenopause or menopause symptoms?

There's early, mostly observational interest in magnesium for sleep disruption and mood changes during perimenopause, since both are areas magnesium plausibly touches. It isn't established as a treatment for hot flashes or other core menopause symptoms. Women navigating this stage are often better served starting with a broader look at perimenopause's early signs and discussing options with a doctor, with magnesium as one piece of a bigger picture rather than the main plan.

Does magnesium affect sleep and mood?

This is one of magnesium's better-supported everyday roles. It's involved in regulating neurotransmitters that calm the nervous system, and low levels have been associated with poorer sleep quality and higher stress reactivity in observational research. That's part of why magnesium glycinate in particular has become popular as a bedtime supplement; the glycine itself may add a calming effect on top of the mineral. It touches the brain closely enough that it's worth reading our separate guide to magnesium for memory and brain fog if sleep and focus are both concerns.

Who is most likely to fall short on magnesium?

Women with gastrointestinal conditions that reduce absorption, such as Crohn's disease or celiac disease, those with poorly controlled type 2 diabetes, people who drink alcohol heavily, and older women in general are at higher risk of low intake or low absorption. Iron deficiency and low magnesium can also show up together in women with heavy menstrual periods and a generally low-nutrient diet, so it's worth looking at the whole picture rather than one mineral in isolation. Certain medications, including some diuretics and long-term proton pump inhibitor use for acid reflux, can also lower magnesium levels over time, which is worth mentioning to a doctor if you take either regularly.

Is a magnesium supplement worth taking, and which form?

If your diet regularly includes the foods above, a supplement usually isn't necessary. If it doesn't, or you have a condition that increases your needs, magnesium citrate or glycinate are generally better absorbed and gentler on the stomach than magnesium oxide, which is the cheapest and most common form on store shelves but also the most likely to cause diarrhea at effective doses. Multivitamins often include a modest amount of magnesium too, which is worth checking before adding a separate, higher-dose product on top.

Magnesium's real story isn't a dramatic one. It's a quiet, background mineral that touches sleep, mood, bone and blood sugar all at once, which is exactly why falling short of it rarely announces itself clearly.

Are there side effects or risks from too much magnesium?

From food, essentially no, since healthy kidneys clear the excess. From supplements, the main risk is diarrhea and stomach cramping at higher doses, which is uncomfortable rather than dangerous for most people. Women with reduced kidney function should talk to a doctor before supplementing, since impaired kidneys can't clear excess magnesium as efficiently, which raises the risk of more serious effects like an irregular heartbeat at very high levels.

Frequently asked questions

Is it better to take magnesium in the morning or at night?

Either can work; the timing matters less than consistency. Many women prefer taking it in the evening since some forms promote relaxation, but if it causes drowsiness or stomach upset, mornings or with a meal may suit you better.

What's the difference between magnesium citrate, glycinate and oxide?

Citrate and glycinate are generally absorbed better and cause less stomach upset than oxide, which is cheaper but more likely to have a laxative effect. Glycinate is often chosen specifically for sleep or anxiety-related use, though direct head-to-head evidence for that advantage is limited.

Can magnesium cause diarrhea?

Yes, especially magnesium oxide or citrate at higher doses, since unabsorbed magnesium draws water into the intestines. Lowering the dose or switching forms usually resolves it.

Does magnesium help with anxiety?

Some small studies suggest a mild benefit for anxiety symptoms, but the trials are generally short, small and inconsistent. It isn't established as a reliable anxiety treatment on its own.

Can you get too much magnesium from food alone?

Essentially no. Healthy kidneys clear excess dietary magnesium efficiently, so toxicity from food is very rare. The upper limit of 350 mg applies to supplements and medications, not food.

Should women on birth control pills take extra magnesium?

There's no established need for extra magnesium specifically because of birth control pills. If you have symptoms you suspect are nutrient-related, it's worth discussing your full supplement and medication list with a doctor rather than guessing.

Does magnesium interact with calcium or vitamin D supplements?

They work together rather than against each other; vitamin D helps the body absorb magnesium, and magnesium helps activate vitamin D. Very high doses of calcium taken at the same time can modestly reduce magnesium absorption, so spacing large doses apart is a reasonable precaution.
