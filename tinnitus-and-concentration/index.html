Tinnitus and Concentration: Why Ringing Ears Affect Focus

The sound itself is only part of the problem. What tinnitus really does is occupy attention you were planning to spend elsewhere.

By Sarah Coleman, Health Editor  ·  Reviewed by Dr. Marcus Reed, MD  ·  Published August 25, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Tinnitus competes for attention, and attention is the resource concentration and memory both draw on.
  • Sleep disruption compounds it, since quiet bedrooms make the sound more noticeable.
  • Most tinnitus involves some hearing loss, and untreated hearing loss carries its own cognitive concerns.
  • Distress predicts impairment better than loudness does. How much it bothers you matters more than how loud it is.
  • CBT has the strongest evidence, not for silencing the sound but for reducing its impact.

People with tinnitus often describe a second complaint that gets far less clinical attention than the ringing. They can't concentrate. Reading takes longer. They lose the thread in meetings. Some worry it means something is happening to their memory.

The connection is real and reasonably well understood, and importantly, several parts of it are treatable.

Attention is the bottleneck

Tinnitus is a sound with no external source, generated by activity within the auditory system and brain. Unlike real sounds, you cannot move away from it, and your brain cannot habituate to it as easily as it habituates to a fan or traffic, partly because it carries an implicit alarm quality.

Novel or potentially threatening sounds capture attention automatically. That's useful when a sound signals something real. With tinnitus the capture keeps happening for a signal that means nothing, and suppressing it requires effort.

That effort has a cost. Working memory, the system holding information over seconds while you use it, has limited capacity. Some of yours is now allocated to monitoring and suppressing an internal sound. Research in tinnitus populations has found impairments on attention and working memory tasks, with the effect linked more closely to tinnitus-related distress than to loudness.

That last point matters practically. Two people can have identical measured tinnitus and entirely different levels of impairment, depending on how much distress it causes them.

The sleep loop

Tinnitus is worst in quiet, and bedrooms are quiet by design. Without background sound to mask it, the ringing becomes the most prominent thing in the room exactly when you're trying to switch off.

Difficulty falling asleep is among the most common complaints, and night waking with the sound is frequent too. Poor sleep then worsens concentration the next day independently, and it also lowers tolerance for the tinnitus itself, which increases distress. The loop closes.

Sleep disruption may account for a substantial share of the concentration difficulty. It's also the most tractable part, which makes it the sensible place to start. Our piece on sleep and memory covers why those hours matter so much for recall.

The hearing loss underneath

Most tinnitus occurs alongside some degree of hearing loss, often in the high frequencies and often unnoticed by the person themselves. The prevailing explanation is that reduced input from the ear leads to compensatory changes in auditory brain regions, which generate the phantom sound.

Hearing loss matters here for a second reason. Research has consistently associated untreated hearing loss with higher dementia risk, and it's identified as one of the modifiable risk factors in major reviews of dementia prevention. Proposed mechanisms include the cognitive load of straining to hear, reduced social engagement, and changes in brain structure from reduced auditory input.

Our article on hearing loss and dementia risk covers that evidence. The practical implication is straightforward: if you have tinnitus, get your hearing tested properly.

What helps

Hearing aids, where hearing loss is present, often reduce tinnitus perception by restoring ambient sound that partially masks it. They also reduce the listening effort that consumes attention all day, and many people report improved concentration for that reason alone.

Sound therapy uses background noise to reduce the contrast between tinnitus and silence. A fan, white noise, rain sounds, or a dedicated sound generator. For sleep specifically this is often the single most useful intervention, and it costs almost nothing to try.

Cognitive behavioural therapy has the strongest evidence base of any tinnitus treatment. It doesn't make the sound quieter. It changes the relationship with it, reducing distress, and since distress predicts impairment better than loudness, that translates into real improvement in daily functioning. Tinnitus retraining therapy combines counselling with sound therapy along similar lines.

Address sleep directly with the usual measures, plus masking sound. If insomnia has become established, cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia works well and avoids adding sedating medication that would blunt cognition further.

Managing stress helps because stress reliably worsens tinnitus perception, creating another loop worth interrupting. Our piece on box breathing for focus covers one low-effort option for acute moments.

What to be sceptical about

Tinnitus attracts a lot of supplement marketing, and the evidence behind most of it is weak. Ginkgo biloba has been studied repeatedly for tinnitus, and reviews have generally not found convincing benefit. Zinc supplementation has been examined with unclear results, possibly relevant only where deficiency exists. Melatonin has some limited evidence for the sleep component rather than the tinnitus itself.

Nothing has been established as a reliable treatment that eliminates tinnitus, and products claiming to cure it are overstating. Being clear about that is more useful than hedging.

When to see a doctor

Certain features warrant prompt assessment rather than management at home. Tinnitus in one ear only. Pulsatile tinnitus that beats in time with your heartbeat. Sudden onset, particularly with hearing loss, which can be a medical urgency. Tinnitus with vertigo, or with neurological symptoms.

Also see someone if tinnitus is causing significant distress, affecting sleep persistently, or contributing to low mood. Those are treatable problems, and untreated they carry the cognitive cost this article describes.

How loud it is matters less than how much it bothers you. Distress, not volume, predicts how much concentration you lose.

Frequently asked questions

Can tinnitus affect concentration and memory?

Tinnitus competes for attention, and attention is required to concentrate and to form new memories. Studies in tinnitus populations have found impairments on attention and working memory tasks, with the effect linked more closely to tinnitus-related distress than to the measured loudness of the sound.

Why is tinnitus worse at night?

Tinnitus is most noticeable in quiet environments, and bedrooms are quiet by design. Without background sound to mask it, the ringing becomes the most prominent thing in the room. This makes falling asleep harder, and the resulting poor sleep worsens both concentration and tolerance for the tinnitus.

Does tinnitus mean I have hearing loss?

Most tinnitus occurs alongside some hearing loss, often in high frequencies and often unnoticed. The leading explanation is that reduced input from the ear triggers compensatory changes in auditory brain regions. Anyone with tinnitus should have their hearing tested properly.

What is the most effective treatment for tinnitus?

Cognitive behavioural therapy has the strongest evidence base. It does not reduce the volume of the sound but changes the relationship with it, lowering distress. Since distress predicts functional impairment better than loudness, this translates into meaningful improvement. Hearing aids and sound therapy also help many people.

When should tinnitus be checked by a doctor?

Seek assessment for tinnitus in one ear only, pulsatile tinnitus that beats with your heartbeat, sudden onset especially with hearing loss, or tinnitus with vertigo or neurological symptoms. Also see a doctor if it causes significant distress, persistent sleep problems or low mood.
