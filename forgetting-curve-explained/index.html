The Forgetting Curve Explained: Why We Lose Information So Fast

You read a page, close the book, and by dinner half of it is gone. That's not a weak memory failing you. It's a predictable curve your brain has followed for well over a century of study.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published September 11, 2026

Photo: Les Hull / Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA 2.0

Key takeaways

  • The forgetting curve describes how quickly newly learned information fades without reinforcement, dropping fastest in the first hours and leveling off after that.
  • It comes from Hermann Ebbinghaus's 1885 self-experiments memorizing nonsense syllables and timing how much he could still recall.
  • Meaningful, well-organized material forgets more slowly than isolated facts with no context.
  • Sleep, review timing and how the material was first learned all change the steepness of the curve.
  • Spaced repetition and active recall are the two habits with the best evidence for flattening it.

The forgetting curve is a graph of how much information you lose over time after learning it once, and the shape is almost always the same: a steep early drop, then a slower, flatter decline. Most of what you're going to forget from a single reading disappears within the first day. What survives past that point tends to stick around much longer, which is the whole reason review timing matters as much as it does.

What is the forgetting curve?

The forgetting curve plots retention against elapsed time since learning, and it isn't a straight line. Retention falls sharply at first, then the rate of loss slows, producing a curve that looks steep on the left and nearly flat on the right. The practical takeaway is blunt: the biggest risk to something you just learned isn't next month, it's tomorrow morning.

This isn't specific to any one kind of fact. It shows up for vocabulary lists, historical dates, phone numbers, even the plot points of a movie you half paid attention to. What changes from person to person and topic to topic is how steep the early drop is and where the curve eventually levels out.

Where the curve came from

German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus published the original research in 1885, and he ran the experiments on himself. He memorized long lists of nonsense syllables, meaningless three-letter combinations with no existing associations, then tested his own recall at different intervals and tracked how many repetitions it took to relearn a list versus learning it fresh. That gap between original learning and relearning became his measure of forgetting.

His results suggested something close to half of freshly memorized nonsense material was gone within about an hour, with the majority lost inside the first day, before the rate of loss slowed considerably over the following weeks. Later researchers have refined and debated the exact numbers, but the general shape, fast early loss followed by a long, slow tail, has held up remarkably well across more than a century of replication with different kinds of material.

Why forgetting happens so fast at first

New information doesn't automatically get filed away for the long term. It sits in a fragile state right after learning, and without reinforcement, the brain treats it as low priority and lets it fade. That's arguably useful: holding onto every stray detail you ever encountered would be its own kind of problem. The forgetting curve is, in part, a picture of your brain being selective on your behalf, just not always in a way that matches what you actually wanted to keep.

Nonsense syllables sit at the extreme end of this. They have no meaning, no connections to anything you already know, and nothing to anchor them, so they fall off the curve fast. Meaningful material behaves differently, and that difference is one of the more useful things this research tells you.

What changes the shape of the curve

A few factors reliably flatten or steepen the drop. Meaning matters most: information tied to something you already understand, or that fits a story or a pattern, tends to survive longer than isolated facts. Depth of initial processing matters too. Skimming a page produces a steeper curve than actually working with the material, explaining it out loud, or connecting it to something you already know.

Sleep plays a real role as well. Our guide to sleep and memory consolidation covers this in more depth, but the short version is that sleep after learning appears to help stabilize new memories, which shows up as a shallower curve the next day compared with staying up. And review timing changes everything: a single well-placed review before the curve bottoms out can reset much of the early loss, which is the entire logic behind spaced repetition.

How to flatten your own forgetting curve

Two habits have the strongest evidence behind them. The first is spaced repetition, reviewing material at increasing intervals rather than once and never again. Our guide to spaced repetition walks through how to schedule that review so each pass lands close to when you'd otherwise start forgetting. The second is active recall, testing yourself on the material instead of rereading it, which is covered in our guide to active recall. Rereading feels productive but does surprisingly little to change the curve. Retrieving the answer from memory does far more.

Both habits work by interrupting the curve before it bottoms out, not by fighting it directly. You're not trying to stop forgetting. You're trying to review at the moments when review does the most good, then let the interval between reviews stretch as the memory gets sturdier. It's a small shift in timing that changes the whole shape of the graph.

A common misreading: cramming versus the curve

Cramming looks like it works because it does, briefly. Pack enough repetition into the hours before a test and you can push a lot of information past the exam. The problem shows up afterward. Because the material was only learned once, in a single sitting, it sits on the steep part of the curve the whole time, and most of it is gone within a few days. Spaced review isn't a slower version of cramming. It's a different strategy aimed at a different outcome, durable recall weeks or months later instead of a single good test.

There's also a common misconception that a steep early drop means something is wrong with your memory specifically. It doesn't. The steep early drop is the normal shape of the curve for anyone, for almost any kind of new information. What varies between people is less the shape of the curve and more how they respond to it, whether they let the material fade untouched or catch it with a review before most of the loss happens.

Where this fits with everything else

The forgetting curve isn't a life sentence. It describes what happens to information you never revisit, and revisiting it is entirely within your control. Our broader guide to improving your memory covers the habits, sleep, nutrition, stress management, that determine how steep your curve is to begin with, on top of whatever review schedule you build.

None of this requires tracking the exact math of your own personal curve, which isn't practical day to day anyway. The useful version is simpler: assume today's fresh information is the most fragile it will ever be, plan a review within the next day or two, then space the next ones out further as the memory holds. That one habit does more to change your outcomes than anything else this research points to.

The curve isn't a verdict on your memory. It's a schedule for when review actually pays off.

Frequently asked questions

What is the forgetting curve in simple terms?

It's a graph showing that we forget newly learned information fastest in the hours right after learning it, then the rate of loss slows down considerably over the following days and weeks.

Who discovered the forgetting curve?

German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus described it in 1885 after running memory experiments on himself, memorizing nonsense syllables and tracking how much he could recall at different intervals.

How much do we actually forget, and how fast?

Ebbinghaus's original data suggested a large share of newly memorized nonsense material was lost within the first day, with the rate of forgetting slowing considerably after that. Meaningful material tends to fade more slowly than the arbitrary syllables he used.

Does the forgetting curve apply to everything we learn?

The general shape shows up across many kinds of information, but meaningful, well-organized material with existing connections tends to have a shallower curve than isolated, arbitrary facts like the nonsense syllables in the original study.

What's the best way to fight the forgetting curve?

Spaced repetition and active recall have the strongest evidence. Reviewing material at increasing intervals, and testing yourself instead of just rereading, both interrupt the curve before most of the loss happens.
