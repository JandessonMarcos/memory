Forest Bathing and Cognitive Function: Can Time in Nature Sharpen Your Mind?

Forest bathing sounds like a wellness trend, but there's a real, if modest, body of research behind it. Here's what time spent deliberately in nature does and doesn't do for attention and stress.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published November 3, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Forest bathing means slow, deliberate time in a natural setting, using the senses rather than exercising or hiking for distance.
  • Small studies link it to lower cortisol, lower blood pressure and better scores on attention tasks compared with spending the same time in a city setting.
  • The leading explanation is Attention Restoration Theory: natural environments ask less of the brain's directed-attention system, letting it recover.
  • The research base is still fairly small and short-term. It shows real, immediate effects more clearly than it shows lasting cognitive benefits.
  • You don't need an actual forest. A park, a tree-lined street or a quiet garden appears to offer a smaller version of the same effect.

Forest bathing, a slow, unhurried walk through a natural setting using all five senses rather than covering distance, is linked in small studies to lower stress hormones, lower blood pressure and modest improvements on tests of attention and working memory. It isn't exercise in the traditional sense and it isn't a cure for anything. The research that exists is real but limited, mostly short-term studies comparing a couple of hours in a forest with the same time in a city.

The term comes from the Japanese practice of shinrin-yoku, developed in the 1980s partly as a public health response to urban stress. It's since been studied by researchers in Japan, South Korea, the US and parts of Europe, with a small but growing set of findings worth taking seriously, alongside some that get overstated online.

What is forest bathing, exactly?

It's not hiking, and it's not a workout. Forest bathing is deliberately slow: walking without a destination, pausing often, and paying attention to sounds, smells, light and texture rather than mileage or heart rate. Most study protocols have participants spend one to four hours in a forest setting at an easy pace, sometimes guided, sometimes alone, with instructions to notice their surroundings rather than talk, read or use a phone.

That slowness is part of the point. A brisk hike burns more calories, but researchers studying forest bathing are testing something closer to attention and stress recovery than cardiovascular fitness. The distinction matters for anyone trying this themselves: rushing through a trail to hit a step count works against the mechanism being studied, not with it.

What does the research actually find?

Several controlled studies, most conducted in Japan and South Korea, compare physiological and cognitive measures before and after time in a forest versus the same amount of time in an urban setting. The consistent findings across this body of work include lower salivary cortisol, a stress hormone, lower blood pressure and heart rate, and better mood scores after forest time compared with city time. A smaller number of studies have also found improved performance on attention and working memory tasks measured right after a forest session.

One frequently cited line of research built on this is Attention Restoration Theory, developed by psychologists Rachel and Stephen Kaplan in the 1980s, which proposes that natural environments engage a gentler, more involuntary kind of attention, sometimes called "soft fascination", that lets the brain's effortful, directed-attention system rest and recover. A city street, by contrast, constantly demands directed attention: traffic, signals, crowds, decisions. The theory predicts exactly the pattern researchers tend to find, lower stress and better attention scores after time in nature.

How much time in nature does it take to see an effect?

Study durationWhat's typically measured
15 to 20 minutesMeasurable drop in cortisol in some smaller studies
1 to 2 hoursThe most common study length; consistent mood and attention improvements
A full day or multi-day retreatLarger effect sizes in some studies, though harder to separate from the break from work and routine itself

Most of the practical research points to somewhere between twenty minutes and two hours producing a measurable, if temporary, effect. There isn't strong evidence that a longer session produces a proportionally larger benefit; the bigger jump seems to be between no nature time and some, rather than between a little and a lot.

Does forest bathing actually improve memory, or just mood?

Both show up in the research, though mood and stress measures have the stronger evidence base. Several studies have found improved scores on standard attention and working memory tasks, like digit span or sustained attention tests, measured shortly after time in nature compared with time in a city. Fewer studies have tracked longer-term memory or whether repeated forest time changes cognitive trajectories over months or years, so the honest summary is: clear short-term attention benefits, promising but thinner evidence for anything longer-lasting.

Do you need an actual forest, or does any green space work?

The research most often uses actual forests, partly because that's the tradition the practice comes from and partly because it's easier to standardize in a study. But a separate line of research on green space and urban parks finds a similar, usually smaller, pattern: time in parks, tree-lined streets and gardens is linked to lower stress and some attention benefits compared with built environments without greenery. For someone without forest access, a quiet park or even a window view of trees appears to offer a scaled-down version of the same effect, not nothing. Is this just exercise and fresh air, or something specific to nature?

Several of the better-designed studies control for this by comparing a walk in nature against a walk of the same length and pace in a city, which strips out the exercise variable and isolates the setting itself. Those studies still find a difference favoring the natural setting on stress and mood measures, which suggests the environment is doing something beyond just getting people moving and outdoors. That said, movement and fresh air aren't nothing either, and a walk in a city park almost certainly beats staying indoors on both counts. What does a typical forest bathing session actually look like?

A guided session usually starts with a few minutes of standing still and breathing before any walking begins, letting the senses settle before moving. The pace afterward is slower than a casual stroll, with the guide or the person themselves stopping often, sometimes for a minute at a time, to notice something specific: the texture of bark, the sound of wind through a particular tree, the smell after rain. There's often an invitation to touch something, sit somewhere for a while, or simply stand and look at one view for longer than feels natural at first. Some sessions end with a small ritual, like sharing a cup of tea made with water that was carried along, though that part is cultural tradition rather than anything studied for its own effect.

Self-guided versions skip the structure but keep the core idea: no destination, no pace goal, deliberate attention to the senses instead of a podcast or a phone. People who've only ever hiked for exercise sometimes find the slowness strange at first; it isn't meant to feel like a workout.

What's the proposed mechanism beyond attention restoration?

A few other explanations get discussed alongside Attention Restoration Theory. Stress Reduction Theory, an earlier related idea, proposes natural scenes trigger an automatic, partly unconscious calming response rooted in how humans evolved alongside natural landscapes. Some researchers also point to phytoncides, airborne compounds released by trees and plants, with small studies suggesting a possible, modest effect on immune markers, though this line of evidence is thinner and more preliminary than the attention and stress findings. None of these mechanisms are mutually exclusive, and the field doesn't treat any one of them as fully settled.

What are the limits of this research?

Most studies are small, often a few dozen participants, and many are short-term, measuring effects right after a single session rather than tracking people over months. Publication patterns in this field also likely favor positive findings, a common limitation across wellness research generally. None of this means the effect isn't real. The cortisol and blood pressure findings in particular have been replicated across multiple independent studies and research groups, which is a meaningfully stronger signal than a single small trial. But claims about forest bathing preventing dementia or producing large, lasting cognitive gains go further than the current evidence supports.

How can you try it without overcomplicating it?

  • Pick a natural setting nearby, a park, trail, or tree-lined street works if a real forest isn't accessible
  • Leave the phone in your pocket or bag for the walk
  • Slow down well below your normal walking pace
  • Notice specific details: bark texture, bird sounds, the smell of the air, the quality of light through leaves
  • Start with 20 to 30 minutes rather than planning a long outing

There's no required ritual or certification involved, despite how it's sometimes marketed. The research protocols are simply structured versions of slow, attentive time outdoors, which is accessible to almost anyone with any patch of greenery nearby.

Who might benefit most from this?

People under high chronic stress, or those whose daily environment is almost entirely indoor and urban, likely have the most room to benefit, since the contrast between their baseline environment and a natural one is largest. That said, nobody in the research literature appears to be harmed by it, and it pairs easily with other evidence-backed habits rather than competing with them. How does this compare with other stress-reduction habits researchers study?

Forest bathing sits alongside meditation, breathing exercises and general time outdoors as part of a broader category of low-cost, accessible stress-reduction practices with real, if modest, evidence behind them. It doesn't appear to be dramatically stronger than those other options in head-to-head comparisons where they exist, and some studies suggest a walk in a city park captures a meaningful share of the benefit without needing an actual forest nearby. What sets it apart is mostly the explicit instruction to slow down and engage the senses deliberately, a structure that's easy to borrow even without using the term "forest bathing" at all. Someone who already meditates or walks regularly outdoors is likely getting a version of this benefit already, whether or not they've ever called it that.

The theory isn't that trees have special powers. It's that a natural scene asks less of the brain's attention system than a city street does, which gives it room to recover.

Frequently asked questions

Is forest bathing the same as hiking?

No. Hiking is typically faster-paced and distance- or fitness-focused. Forest bathing is slow, unstructured and sensory, with no destination or pace goal.

How long does a forest bathing session need to be?

Most research uses one to two hours, though some studies found measurable stress reductions in as little as 15 to 20 minutes.

Can forest bathing replace therapy or medication for anxiety?

No. It may help with stress and mood as a complementary habit, but it isn't a substitute for professional mental health care when that's needed.

Does forest bathing work in winter or bad weather?

The research doesn't suggest the season matters much for the core effect, though comfort and willingness to go outside often drop in cold or wet weather, which matters for actually doing it consistently.

Is there an app or certification needed to do this?

No. Guided sessions with certified practitioners exist and some people find them helpful for structure, but the core practice doesn't require any special training or equipment.

Does forest bathing help with dementia prevention?

There isn't direct long-term evidence for that specific claim. The research supports short-term stress and attention benefits more clearly than any lasting effect on dementia risk.

Can city parks give the same benefit as a real forest?

A smaller version, based on related green-space research. It's not identical to the forest-specific studies, but time in parks and tree-lined areas is linked to similar, usually smaller, stress and mood benefits.
