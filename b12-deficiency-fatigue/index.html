Vitamin B12 Deficiency and Fatigue: Signs You Might Be Low

Fatigue has dozens of causes, but B12 deficiency is one of the few that's cheap to test for and straightforward to fix once you know. Here's how to tell whether it's worth asking your doctor about.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published November 16, 2026

Key takeaways

  • B12 deficiency fatigue tends to feel heavier and more persistent than ordinary tiredness, and sleep alone doesn't fix it.
  • Older adults, strict vegans and people with certain gut conditions face the highest risk because of poor absorption, not just low intake.
  • A simple blood test confirms it, and treatment is usually effective within weeks.
  • If your B12 is already normal, supplementing more won't boost energy further. It isn't a stimulant.
  • Untreated deficiency can affect nerves and the brain, not just energy, which is why testing matters more than guessing.

B12 deficiency can cause real, measurable fatigue because the vitamin is essential for making healthy red blood cells, which carry oxygen through your body. Without enough B12, cells don't get the oxygen they need, producing a heavy, whole-body tiredness that's often paired with nerve or concentration symptoms. A blood test is the only reliable way to confirm it, and treatment typically helps within a few weeks.

Here's how to recognize this specific kind of fatigue, who's actually at risk, and what to do about it.

What does B12 deficiency fatigue actually feel like?

It's usually described as a deep, grinding exhaustion that doesn't respond to a full night's sleep the way normal tiredness does. According to Mayo Clinic, the condition often starts slowly over months and worsens gradually, so people sometimes don't connect their declining energy to anything specific until it's fairly advanced. Fatigue rarely shows up alone. Pale or slightly yellow skin, shortness of breath on mild exertion, and a racing or irregular heartbeat frequently come along with it.

Why does low B12 cause this particular kind of tiredness?

B12 is required to make healthy, normally shaped red blood cells. When it's low, the body produces fewer, larger, less functional red cells, a pattern called megaloblastic anemia. Those cells carry oxygen less efficiently, so tissues throughout the body, including muscles and the brain, run short on the oxygen they need to function normally. That's the physiological root of the fatigue, which is also why it can feel different from the tiredness of a bad night's sleep or ordinary stress.

What are the other warning signs besides fatigue?

Mild deficiency may only cause fatigue and little else at first. One important caveat worth knowing: some people have measurable B12 deficiency with nerve-related symptoms even when a standard blood count looks fine, which is why doctors sometimes order additional markers if symptoms persist despite a "normal" basic test.

Who is actually at risk of B12 deficiency?

Risk climbs sharply with a few specific factors rather than being evenly spread across the population. People over 50 absorb B12 from food less efficiently as stomach acid production naturally declines with age, which is why the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements recommends older adults lean on fortified foods or supplements rather than food alone. Vegans and strict vegetarians are at risk because B12 occurs naturally only in animal products. People with Crohn's disease, celiac disease, or a history of gastric or intestinal surgery absorb it poorly regardless of diet. Long-term metformin users and heavy alcohol users round out the higher-risk groups.

How common is this, really?

More common than most people assume, particularly with age. Estimates cited by the NIH suggest B12 deficiency affects somewhere between 3% and 43% of older adults, a wide range that reflects differences in how studies define and measure it. Even taking the conservative end of that range seriously, it's common enough that unexplained, persistent fatigue in someone over 50 is a reasonable reason to ask a doctor about testing.

How is B12 deficiency diagnosed?

A standard blood test checks your B12 level directly, often alongside a complete blood count that can reveal the telltale large, misshapen red blood cells. When results are borderline, doctors sometimes add a methylmalonic acid test, since this marker can rise even when B12 itself looks only mildly low, catching deficiencies that a basic panel might miss. None of this requires fasting or special preparation in most cases, and it's a quick, inexpensive step compared with chasing fatigue through more invasive testing first.

Can B12 deficiency affect memory and brain function, too?

It can, especially if the deficiency is more pronounced or has gone untreated for a while. Mayo Clinic lists confusion and mental fogginess among the possible neurological effects, alongside the numbness and balance issues that come from nerve involvement. This overlaps with what we cover in our broader guide to brain fog causes and fixes, since B12 is one of the more fixable, testable causes on that list. The encouraging part: these cognitive symptoms usually improve once B12 levels are restored, though severe, long-standing deficiency can occasionally leave effects that take longer to resolve fully.

Will taking a B12 supplement fix your fatigue?

Only if B12 deficiency is actually driving it, which is exactly why testing first matters more than guessing and supplementing. If your levels come back low, correcting it, usually through injections for malabsorption issues like pernicious anemia, or high-dose oral tablets for dietary causes, typically restores energy over several weeks. If your B12 is already adequate, taking more doesn't add extra energy on top of normal, since the vitamin works by supporting existing processes rather than acting like a stimulant. Fatigue with normal B12 almost always has a different explanation, covered in more depth in why am I always tired.

Fatigue is the most common reason people suspect a vitamin problem, and the least specific symptom to diagnose from alone. A blood test turns a guess into an answer in a single visit.

How long before you feel better after treatment starts?

Many people notice some lift in energy within 1 to 2 weeks of starting B12 replacement, whether by injection or high-dose oral supplementation. Fuller recovery, especially of any nerve-related symptoms, tends to take 1 to 3 months and depends heavily on how low levels were and how long the deficiency went uncorrected. People with pernicious anemia, where the immune system attacks the stomach cells needed to absorb B12 naturally, often need ongoing shots indefinitely rather than a one-time fix.

Can a plant-based diet provide enough B12 without supplements?

Not reliably, since B12 is naturally found only in animal foods like meat, eggs, dairy and fish. Anyone eating vegan or largely plant-based needs fortified foods, nutritional yeast products specifically fortified with B12, or a regular supplement to stay covered; relying on plant foods alone, even a varied diet, isn't considered a dependable source. If you've been plant-based for years without ever checking levels, that's a reasonable thing to bring up at your next physical, alongside a broader look at energy through our piece on the afternoon energy slump if fatigue specifically clusters later in the day.

When should you see a doctor about possible B12 deficiency?

Book an appointment for fatigue that persists for more than a couple of weeks despite decent sleep, any tingling or numbness in your hands or feet, unexplained balance problems, or new memory and concentration issues, especially if you fall into a higher-risk group like strict vegetarians, people over 50, or anyone with a digestive condition affecting absorption. It's also worth mentioning if you've taken metformin for several years, since that medication is linked to lower B12 levels over time, independent of diet. Frequently asked questions

What does B12 deficiency fatigue feel like compared to normal tiredness?

It tends to be a heavier, whole-body exhaustion that doesn't clear up with a good night's sleep, often paired with other signs like tingling hands, a sore tongue, or trouble concentrating. Ordinary tiredness usually improves with rest; B12-related fatigue often doesn't.

Who is most at risk of B12 deficiency?

Older adults, people who eat little or no animal products, people with Crohn's disease or celiac disease, those who've had stomach or intestinal surgery, long-term users of certain acid-reducing or diabetes medications, and heavy alcohol users face higher risk. Age-related changes in stomach acid production are a major factor after 50.

How is B12 deficiency diagnosed?

A simple blood test measures B12 levels, sometimes alongside a complete blood count and markers like methylmalonic acid, which can catch a deficiency even when B12 itself looks borderline. A doctor decides which tests make sense based on symptoms and risk factors.

Can B12 deficiency cause brain fog or memory problems?

Yes, in more significant or prolonged deficiency, B12 can affect nerve and brain function, leading to confusion, poor concentration and memory lapses alongside the physical fatigue. These symptoms typically improve once B12 levels are corrected, though severe, long-standing deficiency may leave some effects that take longer to resolve.

Will taking a B12 supplement fix my fatigue?

Only if B12 deficiency is actually the cause. If a blood test confirms low B12, supplementing usually improves energy over several weeks. If your B12 is normal, taking more won't create extra energy, since the vitamin doesn't provide a stimulant-like boost in people who already have enough.

How long does it take to feel better after starting B12 treatment?

Many people notice some improvement in energy within 1 to 2 weeks of starting injections or high-dose oral B12, with fuller recovery over 1 to 3 months depending on how low levels were and how long the deficiency went untreated.

Can you get enough B12 from a plant-based diet?

Not reliably from whole plant foods alone, since B12 occurs naturally only in animal products. People eating vegan or mostly plant-based need fortified foods or a regular supplement, and periodic blood testing is a reasonable habit rather than assuming diet alone covers it.

Does metformin affect B12 levels?

It can. Long-term metformin use is associated with lower B12 absorption and levels over time, which is one reason some doctors periodically check B12 in people who've taken it for years. This is a discussion to have with your prescriber, not a reason to stop the medication on your own.
