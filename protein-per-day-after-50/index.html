How Much Protein Do You Need per Day After 50?

The standard protein guideline was built to prevent deficiency, not to protect muscle as you age. Here's the higher range research actually supports after 50.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published October 27, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Most adults over 50 need more protein than the basic RDA of 0.8 grams per kilogram of body weight. Research on muscle preservation points to roughly 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg, with some experts recommending up to 1.5 g/kg for very active older adults.
  • For a 70 kg (154 lb) adult, that's roughly 70 to 105 grams of protein a day, spread across meals rather than concentrated in one.
  • Muscle loss (sarcopenia) speeds up after 50, and adequate protein, paired with resistance exercise, is one of the few interventions with strong evidence for slowing it.
  • Spreading protein across 3 to 4 meals works better than one large serving; the body can only use so much protein for muscle building at once, a ceiling sometimes called the "muscle-full" effect.
  • Healthy kidneys handle higher protein intake fine. The old blanket caution about protein and kidney damage applies mainly to people who already have reduced kidney function, not to the general population.

Most adults over 50 should aim for roughly 1.0 to 1.2 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day, higher than the general 0.8 g/kg guideline, to help offset the muscle loss that accelerates with age. For a 70 kg (154 lb) adult, that works out to about 70 to 105 grams a day, and some researchers recommend going even higher, up to 1.5 g/kg, for older adults who are very active or recovering from illness or surgery.

That range isn't arbitrary. It comes from a specific body of research on sarcopenia, the age-related loss of muscle mass and strength that begins around the fourth decade of life and speeds up after 50. Here's where that number comes from, how to hit it in practice, and where the real cautions are.

Why do protein needs change as you get older?

Starting around age 30, muscle mass declines gradually, and that decline accelerates after 50, with some estimates putting the loss at 1 to 2 percent of muscle mass per year from midlife onward if nothing is done to counter it. Part of this is a phenomenon researchers call anabolic resistance: aging muscle becomes less responsive to the same amount of dietary protein that would have triggered robust muscle protein synthesis at a younger age. In practice, that means older adults often need more protein per meal than younger adults do to get the same muscle-building signal.

The 0.8 g/kg RDA was calculated to prevent protein deficiency in a healthy, largely sedentary young adult, with a built-in safety margin. It was never designed to optimize muscle maintenance in a 65 or 75-year-old, which is the gap most of the newer recommendations are trying to close.

What does the research actually say about higher protein intake after 50?

SourceRecommended protein for adults 50+Context
General RDA (U.S.)0.8 g/kg body weightMinimum to prevent deficiency; not muscle-protective
PROT-AGE expert group1.0–1.2 g/kg body weightHealthy older adults, general recommendation
PROT-AGE, active or ill older adults1.2–1.5 g/kg body weightHigher physical activity, recovering from illness or injury
ESPEN (European clinical nutrition society)1.0–1.2 g/kg, up to 1.5 g/kgSimilar range, with higher end for malnutrition risk

The consistent theme across these expert groups is the same: the basic RDA is a floor, not a target, for anyone over 50 who wants to actively preserve muscle rather than just avoid outright deficiency.

How should protein be spread across the day?

Research on muscle protein synthesis suggests the body can only make efficient use of roughly 25 to 40 grams of protein for muscle building in a single sitting; amounts beyond that aren't wasted nutritionally, but they don't add much extra muscle-building benefit per meal. This is sometimes called the "muscle-full" effect. Spreading your daily total across three or four meals, each with a meaningful protein portion, tends to work better for muscle maintenance than concentrating most of it at dinner, which is the common pattern for a lot of people.

Breakfast is usually the weakest link. A lot of typical breakfasts (toast, cereal, coffee) provide very little protein, which means the first real dose of the day often doesn't arrive until lunch or later. Adding eggs, Greek yogurt, or a protein smoothie in the morning is one of the easier ways to close that gap without a major diet overhaul.

What are the best protein sources after 50?

  • Eggs: roughly 6 grams per egg, a complete protein that's easy to prepare and inexpensive.
  • Greek yogurt and cottage cheese: 15 to 20 grams per serving, plus calcium that matters for bone health in the same age range.
  • Fish and poultry: roughly 20 to 30 grams per 4-ounce (113 g) serving, lower in saturated fat than most red meat.
  • Legumes (beans, lentils, chickpeas): 12 to 18 grams per cup cooked, with fiber as a built-in bonus most animal sources don't offer.
  • Tofu and tempeh: 15 to 20 grams per half cup, a solid plant-based option that also provides calcium in some fortified forms.
  • Whey or plant protein powder: a convenient way to close a gap, particularly for anyone with a reduced appetite, though it's a supplement to food, not a replacement for it.

None of these need to be eaten in isolation. A realistic day might include eggs at breakfast, a lentil or chicken salad at lunch, a yogurt snack, and fish or beans at dinner, which adds up to the 70 to 105 gram range for most body sizes without needing to track every gram.

Can eating too much protein cause problems?

For healthy adults, the evidence doesn't support the old fear that moderately high protein intake, in the range discussed here, damages the kidneys or thins the bones. Those concerns were extrapolated mostly from people who already had reduced kidney function, where protein restriction is genuinely part of standard care, not from healthy populations eating more protein. Very high intakes, well beyond what's needed for muscle maintenance, haven't shown clear added benefit and can crowd out other nutrients if protein-heavy foods replace vegetables, fruit and whole grains rather than supplementing them.

Does higher protein intake affect kidney health in people without kidney disease?

Large reviews of healthy adults have generally not found that protein intake in the 1.0 to 1.5 g/kg range causes kidney damage. The caution that circulates widely, protein is hard on the kidneys, applies specifically to people with pre-existing chronic kidney disease, where a doctor or dietitian may recommend a lower, carefully managed protein intake as part of treatment. Anyone with diagnosed kidney disease, diabetes affecting kidney function, or a family history of kidney problems should get individualized advice rather than following a general guideline.

How does protein intake connect to muscle loss and sarcopenia?

Sarcopenia is the clinical term for the age-related loss of muscle mass, strength and function, and it's linked to a higher risk of falls, fractures and loss of independence in later life. Adequate protein intake is one of the two interventions with the strongest evidence for slowing it, and it works best alongside, not instead of, the second: resistance exercise. Protein supplies the raw material for muscle repair and growth; resistance training provides the stimulus that tells the body to actually use it.

Does exercise change how much protein you need?

Yes. Someone doing regular resistance training needs more protein than a sedentary person of the same age and weight, generally toward the higher end of the 1.0 to 1.5 g/kg range, since muscle repair after training increases the demand. This is one reason the guidance isn't a single flat number: a very active 60-year-old lifting weights three times a week has meaningfully different needs than a mostly sedentary 60-year-old, even at the same body weight.

Protein timing around a workout matters less than total daily intake for most people, though having a protein-containing meal or snack within a couple of hours of resistance training is a reasonable, low-effort habit rather than a strict requirement.

How much protein is realistic to track day to day?

Most people don't need to weigh every meal to land in a reasonable range. A simple way to estimate your own target: take your weight in pounds, divide by 2.2 to get kilograms, then multiply by 1.0 to 1.2 for a general starting point, or up to 1.5 if you're very active. A 160-pound (73 kg) adult lands around 73 to 88 grams a day at the standard range, or up to about 109 grams at the higher end for an active lifter.

  • Check one meal at a time rather than overhauling the whole day at once; most people are shortest on protein at breakfast.
  • Use your palm as a rough guide for a meat, fish or tofu portion, roughly 20 to 30 grams depending on the food and portion size.
  • Pair, don't replace: adding a protein source to a meal you already eat is usually easier to sustain than redesigning your diet from scratch.
  • Revisit the target after any major health change, including surgery, a new diagnosis, or a period of reduced appetite, since needs can shift.

Who should be careful with a higher-protein approach?

People with diagnosed chronic kidney disease should work with a doctor or renal dietitian rather than following a general higher-protein target, since their situation genuinely calls for a different approach. People with gout may need to moderate certain high-purine protein sources, like organ meats and some shellfish, while still getting adequate total protein from other sources. Anyone on a restricted diet for another medical reason should fold protein planning into that broader conversation rather than treating it separately.

When should you talk to a doctor or dietitian about protein?

It's worth raising with a doctor or registered dietitian if you've noticed unintentional weight loss, a decline in strength or grip, more frequent falls, or a reduced appetite that's making it hard to eat enough of anything, protein included. A dietitian can also help translate the gram targets above into a realistic eating pattern for your preferences, any digestive issues, and whether a protein supplement makes sense for your situation. Does protein intake matter for the brain as well as muscle?

There's a connection worth knowing about: adequate protein supports the amino acids the brain uses to make certain neurotransmitters, and some research links very low protein intake in older adults to a higher risk of frailty, which itself is associated with faster cognitive decline. Frequently asked questions

Is whey protein necessary after 50, or can food alone work?

Whole food can absolutely get you to the target range on its own. Whey or another protein powder is simply a convenient option for anyone with a reduced appetite or a busy schedule, not a requirement.

Can a plant-based diet provide enough protein after 50?

Yes, with some planning. Combining varied plant sources like legumes, tofu, tempeh, nuts and whole grains across the day can meet the higher gram targets, though it may take slightly more attention than an omnivorous diet to hit the same total.

Does protein intake affect bone health, not just muscle?

Yes. Adequate protein is linked to better bone density outcomes, particularly when paired with enough calcium and vitamin D, countering an older assumption that high protein intake was bad for bones.

Is there a best time of day to eat protein for muscle maintenance?

Spreading it evenly across meals matters more than any single "best" time. That said, adding protein to breakfast specifically helps close a gap most people have, since breakfast is typically the lowest-protein meal of the day.

Can a high-protein diet cause dehydration?

Protein metabolism does require some extra water for processing waste byproducts, so staying well hydrated matters more on a higher-protein diet, but this isn't typically dangerous for someone with normal kidney function who drinks adequately.

Do protein needs differ between men and women after 50?

The gram-per-kilogram targets are similar for both sexes; the main practical difference is that men generally weigh more on average, so their total daily gram target tends to be higher in absolute terms, not per kilogram.

Is protein powder as effective as whole-food protein for older adults?

For the specific goal of hitting a daily protein target, research generally shows they perform comparably. Whole food brings along fiber, vitamins and minerals that an isolated powder doesn't, so it's reasonable to treat powder as a supplement to a food-first approach rather than a replacement for it.
