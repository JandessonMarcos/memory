Pet Ownership and Cognitive Decline: What the Research Actually Shows

A pet asks for a walk, a feeding schedule and a bit of daily attention. That ordinary routine turns out to matter more for an aging brain than most people expect.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published November 13, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Long-term pet ownership (5+ years) is linked to slower decline in verbal memory and word fluency in several aging studies, not to cognition across the board.
  • The pattern is strongest in people who live alone, which fits the idea that a pet fills in some of the routine and company a housemate would otherwise provide.
  • Dogs show up more consistently than cats or other pets, likely because daily walks add exercise and outdoor social contact on top of companionship.
  • This is correlational, not causal. No trial has randomly assigned older adults to get a pet and tracked the outcome.
  • Getting a pet late in life has real downsides to weigh too: cost, physical demands and what happens to the animal if your own health changes.

Owning a pet is linked to a slower rate of cognitive decline in several long-running studies of older adults, particularly for verbal memory and word fluency, and particularly among people who live alone. The effect is modest and the research is observational, so it shows a pattern worth paying attention to, not proof that a pet protects your brain.

Here's what the actual data says, which pets and which people show the clearest pattern, and where the evidence runs out.

Does owning a pet really slow cognitive decline?

That's the specific claim worth checking, rather than the softer idea that pets are "good for you" generally. A 2023 analysis using a large, long-running US aging survey, the Health and Retirement Study, compared cognitive test scores over roughly six years between pet owners and non-owners. Long-term owners, people who'd kept a pet for five years or more, showed a slower decline in verbal memory and verbal fluency than non-owners of similar age and health. The same analysis found no clear advantage on other cognitive measures, like working memory or processing speed, which is an important detail the headline version of this finding often drops.

That selectivity actually makes the finding more credible, not less. A result that improved every single measure equally would look more like a side effect of who tends to own pets in the first place than a specific brain effect.

Why would a pet affect your brain at all?

Nobody is claiming a dog or cat directly changes brain tissue. The leading explanations point to what pet ownership does to daily behavior. A pet creates structure: feeding times, walks, a reason to get up at a consistent hour. It adds physical activity, especially with dogs. It reduces loneliness and gives someone to talk to, even one-sided conversation appears to matter for mood. And caring for another living thing provides a sense of purpose, a factor that shows up across unrelated research on loneliness and cognitive decline as well.

None of those four things are unique to pets. They're the same ingredients behind why social engagement, routine and movement protect cognitive health generally. A pet happens to be an efficient way to get several of them at once, which is probably why it shows up as a measurable pattern in the data.

Do dogs and cats show the same pattern?

Not quite. Dog ownership has more supporting data and a clearer proposed mechanism: daily walks mean regular movement, time outdoors and small bits of contact with neighbors or other dog walkers. Our piece on walking and brain health covers why that movement piece alone carries weight independent of anything to do with pets.

Cat ownership has been studied less, and the findings are mixed and less consistent. That doesn't mean cats provide nothing. It likely reflects a thinner research base plus a real difference: a cat rarely requires a 7 a.m. walk around the block. The companionship and routine pieces may still apply; the built-in exercise piece mostly doesn't.

Does it matter how long you've had the pet?

Based on the available data, yes. The clearest associations came from people who had owned a pet continuously for five years or longer, not from short-term or recent owners. Researchers haven't tracked what happens cognitively in the first year or two of new pet ownership with the same rigor, so it's genuinely unclear whether a brand-new pet produces a similar benefit quickly or whether the pattern only emerges after sustained, long-term care.

What are the downsides or risks worth weighing?

A pet isn't a cost-free intervention. There's the expense of food, vet bills and pet-sitting, which adds up over a pet's lifetime. There's the physical demand of walking a large dog, which isn't realistic for everyone with mobility limits. And there's a harder question worth asking honestly before adopting: what happens to the animal if your own health declines faster than expected. Many shelters run senior-to-senior adoption programs that match calmer, often older pets with older adults, partly to address exactly this concern.

Fall risk is another practical factor. A fast-moving young dog underfoot is a real hazard for someone with balance issues, which is a separate consideration from anything cognitive.

Does losing a pet affect cognition going the other way?

It's a reasonable question, and the honest answer is that solid long-term data on this specific point doesn't exist yet. What is well established is that losing a pet is a genuine grief event for many owners, and grief commonly disrupts sleep, increases stress and removes the daily routine and social contact the pet provided. All three of those are separately linked to how sharp someone feels day to day. It would be a stretch to say pet loss causes cognitive decline based on current evidence, but the mechanism connecting grief, disrupted routine and short-term cognitive fog is plausible and worth taking seriously if it happens to you or someone you're caring for.

A pet doesn't change brain chemistry on its own. What it reliably changes is whether someone gets up, moves and talks to another living thing most days, and those ordinary habits are what the research is actually picking up.

Is getting a pet later in life worth it for brain health alone?

If brain health were the only factor, the evidence is suggestive, not compelling enough to justify a major life decision by itself. It's better framed as one more reasonable point in favor of a decision someone is already leaning toward for companionship, security or simply wanting a pet again. Choosing an older, lower-energy animal and having a backup care plan in place addresses most of the practical downsides without giving up the potential upside.

What are the limits of this research?

The central limitation is the one that shows up across almost all lifestyle-and-cognition research: people who keep a pet for five-plus years tend to differ from non-owners in ways researchers can't fully account for statistically, including income, housing stability, baseline health and existing social support. No study has randomly assigned people to get a pet or not and then tracked cognitive outcomes, which is the design that would answer the causal question cleanly. Until that kind of trial exists, "linked to" remains the accurate phrase, not "slows" or "prevents."

Frequently asked questions

Does owning a pet actually prevent dementia?

No study has shown that. Long-term pet ownership is linked to a slower rate of decline on some cognitive measures in observational research, which is different from preventing dementia. Think of it as one supportive habit among many, not a safeguard on its own.

Do I need to have owned a pet for years to see any benefit?

The studies showing the clearest pattern followed people who'd owned a pet for five years or more. Shorter-term or recent pet ownership hasn't been studied as thoroughly, so it's not clear whether a new pet produces the same association quickly.

Are dogs really better for the brain than cats?

Dog ownership shows up more often in the research, likely because dogs require daily walks, which add physical activity and outdoor social contact on top of companionship. That doesn't mean cats or other pets offer nothing, just that the dog studies have more supporting data behind them.

I live alone. Does that change anything?

Several studies found the association between pet ownership and slower cognitive decline was strongest in people living alone, which fits the idea that a pet partly substitutes for the routine and company a household member would otherwise provide.

Is it a bad idea to get a pet late in life?

Not inherently, but it's worth being realistic about physical demands, cost and what happens to the pet if your own health changes. Many shelters offer senior-to-senior adoption programs that match older, calmer pets with older adults for exactly this reason.

Can losing a pet hurt cognitive health?

Grief after losing a pet can bring on stress, disrupted sleep and a temporary loss of routine and social contact, all of which affect how sharp someone feels in the short term. There isn't solid long-term data on cognition after pet loss specifically, but the mechanism is plausible given how linked those factors are to brain health generally.

What should I actually take away from this research?

If you already have a pet and enjoy it, the evidence gives you one more reason to keep walking and caring for it. If you're deciding whether to get one purely for brain health, weigh the real responsibilities honestly. A pet is a plausible, modest piece of a bigger picture, not a substitute for sleep, movement, diet and social contact.
