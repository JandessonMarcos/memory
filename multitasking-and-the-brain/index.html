Why Multitasking Hurts Your Memory and Focus

It feels like getting more done. Brain research says otherwise: switching between tasks quietly drains memory, accuracy, and time.

By Sarah Coleman, Health Editor  ·  Reviewed by Dr. Marcus Reed, MD  ·  Published July 27, 2026

Key takeaways

  • "Multitasking" is mostly fast switching. The brain handles one demanding task consciously at a time, not two at once.
  • Every switch has a cost: a moment to drop one task's context and load another's, plus a higher chance of a small error.
  • Memory takes the biggest hit. Forming a solid memory needs focused attention, so divided attention produces weaker, patchier recall.
  • Phones are the worst offender, since they pull in a completely unrelated context rather than a related one.
  • Batching and short single-task blocks measurably beat constant switching for both speed and what you actually remember.

If you're wondering whether multitasking hurts your memory, the short answer is yes, for the vast majority of people and tasks. Your brain isn't built to run two attention-demanding jobs side by side. It alternates between them, and each handoff costs a moment of processing time along with a smaller, less durable trace in memory. Here's what's actually happening when you answer email during a call or scroll while half-watching a show, and what to do instead.

Is multitasking even a real thing the brain does?

Mostly, no. A small number of well-practiced, largely automatic pairings, like walking while chatting, can run together because one of them barely needs conscious attention. But two tasks that both require thought, writing an email while listening to a meeting, say, aren't processed in parallel. The brain queues them and switches rapidly back and forth, giving each one a sliver of full attention before moving to the next. What feels like doing two things at once is really doing one thing, then the other, then the first again, over and over.

What does each switch actually cost you?

Researchers who study attention describe a "switch cost": a brief lag while the brain disengages from one task's rules and goals and reloads the other's. On its own, one switch is small. Stack dozens of them across a workday and the lost time adds up, and so do the mistakes. Studies on task-switching consistently find more errors and slower completion when people bounce between unrelated activities compared with finishing one before starting the next. The effect is usually stronger the more different the two tasks are from each other.

Why does multitasking hurt memory specifically?

Encoding a new memory, the process of turning an experience into something you can retrieve later, leans heavily on focused attention and working memory. Working memory has limited capacity, and splitting it between two streams of information leaves less room for either one to be processed deeply. That's why you can sit through an entire meeting while texting and walk away barely able to recall what was decided. Your eyes and ears took in the information, but without full attention, it never moved into a form your brain could hold onto. Does checking your phone make it worse than switching between two work tasks?

It usually does. Two work tasks at least share some context; a phone notification yanks your attention toward something entirely unrelated, personal messages, social feeds, unrelated news, which makes the mental reload heavier. Some studies comparing heavy and light "media multitaskers" have found the heavy group performs worse on tasks that require filtering out irrelevant information, the opposite of what you'd expect if practice made multitasking easier. That research area is still developing, and not every study agrees, but nothing in it suggests frequent phone-checking sharpens attention.

Are some people genuinely good at multitasking?

A small share of people, sometimes called "supertaskers" in cognitive psychology research, do show little to no measurable cost when juggling certain tasks. They appear to be a real but small minority. The bigger issue is that most people rate their own multitasking ability as above average, which cannot be true for the population as a whole. Confidence in your own multitasking skill is a poor guide to how well you actually perform it.

What actually helps you get more done without hurting memory?

Batch similar tasks together instead of interleaving different ones. Silence notifications during focused work rather than trying to will yourself to ignore them. Work in single-task blocks of 25 to 45 minutes, then take a short break before switching to something else. Write things down the moment they occur to you instead of holding them in working memory "just for a second," which is exactly the kind of split attention that erodes recall. Multitasking feels efficient in the moment. What it actually buys you is more errors, weaker memories, and often more time spent later re-learning what didn't stick the first time.

Is any multitasking harmless?

Pairing a mentally demanding task with something close to automatic, folding laundry while listening to a podcast, is generally fine, since one side barely draws on attention. The trouble starts once both tasks compete for the same limited resource. Driving while on a call is the clearest real-world example: it feels manageable because driving is often semi-automatic, but on unfamiliar roads or in heavy traffic it draws heavily on attention too, and that's when the combination becomes genuinely risky rather than just inefficient.

Frequently asked questions

Does multitasking actually hurt memory?

Yes, in most cases. Forming a solid memory takes focused attention, and splitting that attention between tasks leaves a weaker trace behind. Information you were exposed to while multitasking is often technically "seen" but never properly encoded, which is why it's so hard to recall later.

Why does switching tasks feel slower than it seems like it should?

Every switch requires your brain to drop the mental context of one task and reload the rules and goals of another. That reloading isn't instant. Even brief switches add up across a day, and each one also raises the odds of a small error.

Is checking your phone while working worse than switching between two work tasks?

It tends to be, because a phone pulls in an unrelated context (social, personal, unrelated news) rather than a related one. Some research on heavy media multitaskers has linked frequent switching to more trouble filtering out irrelevant information, though this is an active area of study, not a settled fact.

Can you train yourself to get better at multitasking?

A little, with heavy practice on the exact same pair of tasks, but the gains don't transfer well to new combinations. Most people improve their real output far more by reducing how often they switch than by trying to get better at switching itself.
