The Protege Effect: Why Teaching Others Helps You Remember More

Explain something out loud and you find the gaps in your own understanding fast. That discomfort is doing real work, and it's the whole reason this technique holds up.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published September 9, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Preparing to teach changes how you study, pushing you toward organizing and simplifying rather than just re-reading.
  • Studies comparing 'study to teach' with 'study to be tested' find the teaching-prep group often retains more, even when neither group actually teaches anyone.
  • The expectation to explain seems to matter as much as the explaining itself. Knowing you'll have to make it clear to someone else changes how you process the material.
  • It works especially well for material with real structure — concepts and relationships, not disconnected facts.
  • You don't need a real student. Explaining out loud to no one, or writing a plain-language summary, captures most of the benefit.

Ask a teacher which topics they understand best and most will name the ones they've taught the most, not the ones they studied hardest in school. That's not just a nice story about the rewards of a teaching career. Researchers have a name for the underlying effect, the protege effect, and a decent amount of evidence backing the idea that preparing to teach something changes how well you learn it yourself.

What the research actually shows

The basic experiment is simple. One group of students is told to study material because they'll be tested on it. Another group is told to study the same material because they'll have to teach it to someone else. Sometimes the teaching group actually teaches; sometimes the study ends before that happens and everyone just takes the same test.

In several versions of this design, the group expecting to teach outperformed the group expecting a test, even when the teaching never occurred. That detail matters. It means at least part of the benefit comes from the mindset of preparing to teach, not only from the act of explaining itself.

The effect isn't universal or huge in every study, and some of the research has been run on small samples of college students learning short passages, which limits how confidently it generalizes to, say, a year of professional training. The direction of the evidence is consistent enough to take seriously, though, and it lines up with older research on how explaining things changes comprehension.

Why expecting to teach changes how you study

When you study for a test, the goal is recognizing or recalling the right answer. That pushes people toward re-reading, highlighting and reviewing notes, methods that feel productive but produce fairly shallow encoding.

When you study to teach, the goal shifts to being able to explain something clearly to someone who doesn't already know it. That requires you to organize the material, identify what actually matters, anticipate confusing points, and hold the whole structure in mind well enough to walk through it in a sensible order. All of that is a heavier, more active form of processing than most test preparation involves.

This connects to why active recall beats passive review generally: the harder and more generative the mental work, the more durable the resulting memory tends to be. Preparing to teach is, in effect, a way of forcing generative processing without needing a flashcard app.

Does it matter if you actually teach anyone?

Some studies suggest the act of explaining adds a further benefit on top of just preparing to explain, particularly for identifying and fixing gaps in your own understanding. You often don't discover what you don't understand until you try to say it out loud and stumble.

But the preparation effect alone appears to do most of the heavy lifting, which is good news if you don't have a willing student handy. Setting the intention to teach, and organizing your notes as if you had to, seems to capture a large share of the benefit even when no one is listening.

How to use this without a classroom

Pick the topic you're trying to learn and explain it out loud, alone in a room, as if a specific person who knows nothing about it were sitting across from you. Notice where you slow down, backtrack or reach for a word you can't quite find. Those stumbles are pointing at the parts you don't actually understand as well as you assumed.

Writing a short, plain-language summary works almost as well and leaves you with something to review later. Skip jargon. If a sentence only makes sense to someone who already knows the topic, it isn't a real explanation yet, and rewriting it is where a lot of the learning happens.

Study groups can use a simpler version of the same idea: assign each person a section to teach the others, briefly, before anyone opens their notes. It forces the same organize-to-explain step that drives the effect in the research, and it's a natural pairing with spaced repetition if you revisit the same explanations a few days later.

Where this technique falls short

It works best on material with real structure, ideas that connect to each other, cause-and-effect relationships, processes with steps. It has less to offer for pure memorization, like a list of dates or vocabulary with no underlying logic to explain. For that kind of material, straightforward retrieval practice and repetition still do more of the work.

It also takes longer than passive review, at least at first, because organizing an explanation is more effortful than rereading a page. For someone cramming the night before a test with limited time, the payoff may not be worth the setup. It earns its place best when you have a few extra days and want the material to actually stick, not just survive one exam.

There's a workplace version of this worth mentioning too. New hires who are asked to document a process for the next person, rather than just complete it and move on, often end up understanding that process better than colleagues who've quietly done it the same way for years without ever having to explain it. The instinct to write a clear how-to guide forces the same organize-and-simplify step that shows up in the classroom research, even outside anything resembling a classroom.

One more limit worth naming honestly: preparing to teach takes real time up front, and rushing it defeats the purpose. Skimming a topic for ten minutes and then trying to explain it usually just exposes how little you understood, which is useful information but not the same as having learned the material. The technique rewards people who give it enough runway to actually build an explanation, not a fast substitute for studying at all.

As with any study method, none of this substitutes for the fundamentals that support learning in general. Sleep and consistent routines still matter more than any single technique, and our guide on improving your memory covers the habits with the strongest evidence behind them.

You rarely find the hole in your understanding by rereading. You find it the moment you try to explain it to someone else and the sentence falls apart.

Frequently asked questions

What is the protege effect?

The protege effect describes the finding that preparing to teach material to someone else tends to improve how well you learn it yourself, compared with studying only to be tested. Several studies have found this benefit even when the person expecting to teach never actually does.

Do you need a real student for the protege effect to work?

Not necessarily. Much of the benefit appears to come from the mindset of preparing to explain something clearly, which changes how you organize and process the material, rather than from the act of teaching itself. Explaining out loud to no one, or writing a plain-language summary, captures a large share of the effect.

Why does teaching something help you remember it better?

Preparing to teach pushes you toward organizing information, identifying what matters most, and anticipating confusing points, which is a deeper form of processing than typical test preparation like rereading or highlighting. That extra effort during studying tends to produce more durable memories.

Does the protege effect work for all types of material?

It seems to work best for material with real structure, concepts, relationships, processes with steps, since that's what benefits from being organized into an explanation. For rote memorization, like isolated facts or vocabulary, straightforward retrieval practice tends to help more.

Is the protege effect just active recall by another name?

They're related but not identical. Active recall is about retrieving information from memory without looking at notes. The protege effect is broader: it's about how the goal of explaining something changes the way you study it in the first place, which includes organizing and simplifying the material before you ever try to recall it.
