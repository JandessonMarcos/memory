Thyroid Symptoms in Women: What to Watch For

Thyroid disease is far more common in women than men, and its symptoms overlap so heavily with everyday stress and aging that many cases go unnoticed for years. Here's what to actually watch for.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published November 17, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Women are five to eight times more likely than men to develop thyroid disease, mostly autoimmune types.
  • Fatigue, weight change and mood shifts are the most common symptoms, and the easiest to mistake for something else.
  • Period changes are a real clue. Heavier or lighter cycles than usual can trace back to the thyroid.
  • A simple TSH blood test is the standard first step toward a diagnosis.
  • Treatment is usually straightforward once the problem is identified and the right medication dose is found.

The most common thyroid symptoms in women are ongoing fatigue, unexplained weight change, feeling unusually cold or hot, hair thinning, dry skin, mood changes and irregular periods. An underactive thyroid tends to slow everything down; an overactive one speeds things up. Because the symptoms mimic stress, aging and other common conditions, many women live with thyroid disease for years before it's caught on a blood test.

That overlap is exactly the problem. A busy, tired, slightly-off-feeling stretch of life describes a lot of people who don't have a thyroid issue at all. Below is how to tell when it's worth asking a doctor to check.

Why is thyroid disease so common in women?

Thyroid conditions run roughly five to eight times more often in women than in men, and most of that gap comes from autoimmune thyroid disease, where the immune system mistakenly attacks the thyroid gland. Hashimoto's thyroiditis, the leading cause of an underactive thyroid, and Graves' disease, the leading cause of an overactive one, both skew heavily female. Hormonal shifts across puberty, pregnancy and menopause are thought to interact with this autoimmune tendency, though researchers are still working out exactly why women's immune systems target the thyroid more often.

What are the earliest signs something's off?

Fatigue that doesn't improve with rest is usually the first thing people notice, followed by some combination of weight change without a change in eating or exercise, feeling cold when others are comfortable (or the reverse), and skin or hair that suddenly feels different, drier, thinner, more brittle. None of these alone points clearly to the thyroid. It's the combination, and how long it lingers, that's worth paying attention to.

How do symptoms differ between an underactive and overactive thyroid?

An underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism) slows the body's metabolism, so symptoms lean toward fatigue, weight gain, feeling cold, constipation and a slowed heart rate. An overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism) speeds things up instead, producing symptoms like unintentional weight loss, a racing heart, feeling hot or sweaty, anxiety and trouble sleeping. Both can cause irregular periods and mood changes, just in somewhat different directions.

Can thyroid problems change your period?

Yes, and this is one of the more useful clues because it's specific. Thyroid hormone interacts directly with the hormones that regulate the menstrual cycle, so an underactive thyroid often brings heavier, longer or more frequent periods, while an overactive thyroid tends to make periods lighter, shorter or less frequent, sometimes stopping for a few cycles. If your periods have changed noticeably without an obvious reason, mentioning it alongside any fatigue or weight changes gives a doctor a more complete picture.

Does thyroid disease affect memory and concentration?

It can, in both directions. Thyroid hormone helps regulate how brain cells use energy, so too little or too much of it can leave people feeling mentally slow, forgetful, or unable to concentrate the way they normally would. This overlaps closely with ordinary brain fog, which is covered in more depth in our piece on thyroid and brain fog. The encouraging part is that this kind of cognitive fog tends to improve once thyroid hormone levels are brought back into a normal range with treatment.

Fatigue, a changed period and feeling cold or wired for no clear reason rarely get blamed on the thyroid first. They're often the thyroid's loudest signals.

What about hair, skin and weight changes?

Thyroid hormone affects nearly every cell in the body, which is why its symptoms show up in so many unrelated-seeming places. Hair can thin diffusely across the scalp, including the outer third of the eyebrows in some cases of hypothyroidism. Skin can turn dry and rough, or conversely warm and moist with hyperthyroidism. Weight changes happen because thyroid hormone sets the pace of metabolism; even a mild thyroid imbalance can shift the number on the scale by several pounds over months without any change in diet.

How is a thyroid problem actually diagnosed?

A TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) blood test is the usual starting point, since it's sensitive to even small thyroid imbalances. If TSH comes back abnormal, doctors typically order free T4 and sometimes free T3 to see how much actual thyroid hormone is circulating, plus thyroid antibody tests (like TPO antibodies) to check for an autoimmune cause such as Hashimoto's or Graves'. A physical exam of the neck and, occasionally, an ultrasound can check for nodules or an enlarged gland (goiter).

  1. TSH test: the standard first screen; abnormal results trigger follow-up testing.
  2. Free T4 / free T3: measures actual circulating thyroid hormone levels.
  3. Thyroid antibodies: checks for autoimmune thyroid disease.
  4. Physical exam or ultrasound: checks the gland itself for nodules or enlargement.

Who should ask their doctor for a thyroid check?

It's reasonable to ask for a TSH test if you have persistent, unexplained fatigue; noticeable weight change without a change in habits; new or worsening period irregularity; a family history of thyroid disease; or you're pregnant or planning to be, since thyroid levels matter for the baby's development too. Women over 60, and anyone with another autoimmune condition, also carry a higher baseline risk and may benefit from periodic screening even without obvious symptoms.

Can thyroid disease during pregnancy cause problems?

Untreated thyroid disease during pregnancy, both underactive and overactive, is linked to a higher risk of complications and can affect fetal development, which is why many obstetric practices check thyroid levels in early pregnancy or in anyone with a known thyroid history. Properly treated and monitored thyroid disease, by contrast, generally allows for a healthy pregnancy; the key is catching and managing it rather than leaving it unchecked.

What does treatment actually look like?

For an underactive thyroid, treatment is usually a daily synthetic hormone pill, most often levothyroxine, with the dose adjusted based on follow-up TSH tests every few weeks until levels stabilize, then checked periodically after that. For an overactive thyroid, options include anti-thyroid medication, radioactive iodine treatment, or in some cases surgery, depending on the cause and severity. Most women end up on a stable routine that needs only occasional blood test check-ins, especially around pregnancy, major weight changes or new symptoms.

If anything here sounds familiar and has lasted more than a few weeks, a thyroid panel is a low-cost, low-risk way to either rule it out or catch something that's genuinely manageable once named. It's one of the more fixable explanations behind symptoms that otherwise get written off as just being tired, busy or getting older.

Frequently asked questions

What are the first signs of thyroid problems in women?

Early signs often include persistent fatigue, unexplained weight change, feeling unusually cold or warm, and changes to periods. These are easy to blame on stress or age, which is part of why thyroid issues go unnoticed for so long.

Why are women more likely to get thyroid disease than men?

Women are roughly five to eight times more likely to develop thyroid disease, especially autoimmune types like Hashimoto's. Hormonal shifts during puberty, pregnancy and menopause, plus a stronger autoimmune tendency, are thought to play a role, though the exact reasons are still being studied.

Can thyroid problems affect periods?

Yes. An underactive thyroid can cause heavier, longer or more frequent periods, while an overactive thyroid often makes periods lighter or less frequent. Thyroid issues are a common, and treatable, cause of irregular cycles that many women never connect to the gland itself.

Does thyroid disease cause brain fog or memory problems?

It can. Thyroid hormone affects brain energy use, so both low and high thyroid levels are linked to slower thinking, forgetfulness and trouble concentrating. These symptoms typically improve once thyroid levels are corrected with treatment.

What blood test checks for thyroid problems?

A TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) test is the standard first step. If it's abnormal, doctors often add free T4, free T3 and thyroid antibody tests to pin down the type and cause of the problem.

Can thyroid problems during pregnancy affect the baby?

Untreated thyroid disease during pregnancy can raise the risk of complications and affect the baby's development, which is why many doctors check thyroid levels early in pregnancy. Treated, well-managed thyroid disease generally allows for a healthy pregnancy.

How is an underactive thyroid treated?

The standard treatment is a daily synthetic thyroid hormone pill, usually levothyroxine, dosed to bring TSH back into a normal range. Most people need periodic blood tests to fine-tune the dose, especially after major life changes like pregnancy or significant weight change.
