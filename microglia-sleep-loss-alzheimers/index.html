Alzheimer's Sleep Loss Traced to Brain Immune Cells, Not Plaques

A University of Kentucky study found removing the brain's overactive immune cells restored deep sleep in Alzheimer's mice, even though the amyloid plaques stayed put.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published July 27, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Poor sleep is common in Alzheimer's, and this study points to a specific driver behind it: overactive microglia, the brain's resident immune cells.
  • Researchers at the University of Kentucky's Sanders-Brown Center on Aging used a drug that reduced microglia by roughly 87% in a mouse model of Alzheimer's.
  • That reduction restored over two hours of deep, non-REM sleep per night, without clearing the amyloid plaques researchers expected to matter most.
  • The finding suggests inflammation from overactive immune cells, not the plaques themselves, may more directly disrupt sleep.
  • This is mouse research, not a human treatment. Years of further study would be needed before anything like it reaches patients.

Researchers at the University of Kentucky's Sanders-Brown Center on Aging reported that in a mouse model of Alzheimer's disease, the brain's own immune cells, not the amyloid plaques long blamed for the disease's damage, appear to be a major driver of lost sleep. Led by physiology researcher Shannon Macauley, the team found that reducing microglia, the brain's resident immune cells, restored more than two hours of deep sleep per night. The plaques were still there. The sleep came back anyway.

What did the researchers actually do?

The team worked with mice bred to develop amyloid plaques similar to those seen in human Alzheimer's disease. These mice typically sleep poorly, especially losing time in non-REM sleep, the deep, restorative stage linked to memory consolidation. The researchers gave the mice a drug that blocks a receptor microglia need to survive, and over about two weeks it reduced the brain's microglial population by roughly 87%.

What did they find?

Despite the amyloid plaques remaining in place, the mice with depleted microglia gained back more than two hours of non-REM sleep per night compared with untreated mice. That's a striking result, because the standard assumption has been that the plaques themselves are what disrupts sleep architecture in Alzheimer's. Here, clearing out the inflammatory immune response, without touching the plaques, was enough to bring sleep back.

Why does this challenge the usual story about Alzheimer's?

Amyloid plaques have dominated Alzheimer's research and drug development for decades, on the theory that clearing them should ease the disease's downstream effects. This study suggests that for sleep specifically, the more direct culprit may be the inflammatory response mounted by microglia as they react to those plaques, rather than the plaques themselves. Microglia are supposed to help clear cellular debris and support brain health; the idea here is that in Alzheimer's, they become chronically overactive and that overactivity, not the amyloid, is what wrecks sleep.

How does this fit into the bigger research picture?

This finding lands alongside a broader shift in Alzheimer's research toward looking beyond amyloid plaques on their own. Other recent work has explored blood tests that flag Alzheimer's-related changes years before symptoms appear, and separate proteins that seem to influence how tau tangles form and spread. Neuroinflammation, the immune response inside the brain, has moved from a side note to an active research focus in its own right. Taken together, these threads suggest Alzheimer's involves several overlapping processes rather than one dominant cause, and a finding like this one adds a piece to that picture without rewriting it.

The plaques stayed. The sleep came back anyway. That gap between cause and effect is exactly what makes this finding worth watching.

Is this something you can act on today?

No. This is early-stage mouse research using an experimental drug approach, not a treatment available to patients, and depleting immune cells throughout the brain carries its own risks that would need careful study before any human use. What it does is point future research toward microglia and neuroinflammation as a target distinct from amyloid clearance, an approach some other recent studies have also been exploring from different angles.

What this means for you

If sleep and memory concerns are on your mind, this study is a reason for interest, not a reason to change anything today. The tools with real, current evidence remain the same: protecting your own sleep, staying active, and managing known risk factors. Frequently asked questions

What did the University of Kentucky study find?

Researchers found that in a mouse model of Alzheimer's disease, depleting the brain's own immune cells (microglia) by roughly 87% restored more than two hours of deep, non-REM sleep per night, even though the amyloid plaques associated with the disease were not cleared.

Does this mean amyloid plaques don't matter for sleep loss in Alzheimer's?

Not exactly. It suggests that inflammation driven by overactive microglia, the brain's immune response to the plaques, may be a more direct cause of sleep disruption than the plaques themselves. The plaques may still matter for other aspects of the disease; this study is specifically about what's disrupting sleep.

The research is early. Protecting your sleep isn't.
