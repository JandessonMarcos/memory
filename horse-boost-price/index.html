Three packages, one-time charges and a 60-day refund window. Here's the per-day math, plus the questions worth asking before you choose a bottle count.

Key takeaways

  • Three packs, published at the time of writing: 2 bottles at $79 each, 3 at $69 each, 6 at $49 each.
  • That's $1.63 to $2.63 a day. Each bottle holds 30 gummies, one a day, so one bottle lasts a month.

The Horse Boost price at the time of writing is $79 a bottle for two bottles ($158 plus shipping), $69 a bottle for three ($207, free US shipping) or $49 a bottle for six ($294, free US shipping). With one gummy a day and 30 per bottle, that's $1.63 to $2.63 a day. Each order is a one-time charge.

Prices on supplement sites change, sometimes week to week, so treat these numbers as a snapshot and confirm them on the official page before you pay. What doesn't go stale is the method. Below we show how to work out the real daily cost, how the guarantee interacts with each package, and how to decide whether the HorseBoost price makes sense for you. Which HorseBoost package should you buy?

The right pack depends on how much you already know about how you respond, not on the discount.

  • Two bottles, if you're unsure. Sixty days lines up with the guarantee window, so you can finish a fair trial and still request a refund if it does nothing.
  • Three bottles, if you want a proper test. The ingredient research ran 8 to 12 weeks. Ninety days covers that, with free shipping and a lower daily cost.
  • Six bottles, only if you've already tried it. It's the best price per day, but it commits $294 to a product with no clinical trial of its own.

There's a middle path many buyers miss. Start with two or three bottles. If you're happy by the end, the six-pack is still there for the next order, and you'll be buying it with real information instead of hope. You'll pay a little more per day for the first two months, which is the price of not locking in $294 on a guess.

If you're considering HorseBoost because of new or ongoing erection problems, spend the money on a doctor visit first. Those problems can be an early sign of heart disease, diabetes or low testosterone, and a checkup will tell you far more than any supplement. Our article on whether Horse Boost works explains why.

One more option is worth naming: not buying yet. If you're curious but not sure, there's no deadline. Supplement prices on these pages rarely disappear, and no legitimate offer needs you to decide within minutes. Any countdown timer on a checkout page is a sales tactic, not a real expiry.

Is there a Horse Boost subscription or autoship?

No. At the time of writing, the official checkout charges once for the package you choose and doesn't enroll you in a recurring plan. That's worth confirming on the order page, because some supplement funnels add an autoship box or a pre-checked upsell.

Before you click the final button, check three things. Is the total exactly what you expected? Is there a box for monthly delivery, and is it unchecked? Did a second offer appear after checkout, and did you accept it or skip it? A minute here prevents the most common billing complaint in this industry, which is a charge the buyer didn't realize they'd agreed to.

After the order, look at your card statement once the charge posts. If anything doesn't match, contact the seller's support with your order number right away.

One caution on reading guarantees in general. "Money-back" rarely means every cent in every case. Some sellers refund the product price but not shipping, some accept only sealed bottles beyond a certain number, and most require a request inside the window rather than a postmark. None of that is unusual. It's simply worth knowing before you buy rather than after.

A simple habit helps. On the day the package arrives, set a reminder for day 50. That gives you ten days to decide and send a request without rushing.

What else should you budget for?

The sticker price isn't the whole cost. A few smaller items can change the math, and most buyers don't think about them until later.

  • Shipping on the two-bottle pack. It's added at checkout and raises the daily cost a few cents.
  • Return shipping if you ask for a refund. Many supplement guarantees ask the buyer to pay to send bottles back. Check whether this one does, and how much tracked postage costs from your area.
  • Post-purchase offers. Upsell pages after checkout can add a second product to the order. Skip anything you didn't plan to buy.
  • A doctor visit, if you need one. If you have symptoms, a checkup with basic blood work is the better first spend, and many insurance plans cover a yearly physical.

None of these is a reason to avoid the product. They're just the honest total. A buyer who ends up asking for a refund on a two-bottle order may get back the product price but not the shipping either way, which is a small, real cost of the experiment.

Is Horse Boost cheaper on Amazon or in stores?

The manufacturer sells HorseBoost through its official site. If you find a listing on a marketplace, an auction site or a store shelf, it's likely a reseller, an old batch or a copy using a similar name.

That matters for three reasons. A third-party listing usually isn't covered by the 60-day guarantee. You can't be sure how the product was stored, which affects gummies more than tablets because heat softens and degrades them. And male enhancement is a category where the FDA has repeatedly found counterfeit or tainted products sold online. A low price from an unknown seller isn't a bargain if you can't trust what's inside.

How do you know you're on the official page? Supplement funnels often pass through review sites and advertorials before reaching checkout, and that's normal. What matters is where you enter your card. The checkout should name the product exactly, show the same three packages, state the guarantee, and give you an order confirmation by email with a support contact. If a page asks for payment by gift card, wire transfer or crypto, leave.

If you do buy, check that the seal under the cap is intact. The label itself says not to use the product if it's broken or missing.

What are you actually paying for?

Each gummy contains an 82 mg proprietary blend of muira puama, maca, catuaba, green tea extract, 5 mg of caffeine, ashwagandha, L-arginine, tribulus and horny goat weed. The label doesn't print the individual amounts, except caffeine.

That makes value hard to judge. The two ingredients with the best evidence, maca and ashwagandha, are studied at 1,500 to 3,000 mg and 300 to 600 mg a day. A blend this size can't hold those amounts. So at $1.63 to $2.63 a day, you're paying mostly for convenience, a traditional formula in one daily gummy, and a refund window, rather than for research doses. Here's a fair way to compare. Plain maca root powder and single-ingredient ashwagandha capsules with printed doses usually cost a fraction of that per day and get you closer to studied amounts. What they don't give you is the combination, the gummy format or a money-back guarantee from one seller.

How does the price compare with Vigor Boost?

Vigor Boost uses the same 82 mg blend with the same nine ingredients. The differences are 10 mg of caffeine instead of 5 mg, 2 g of sugar instead of 3 g, and a different flavor and brand. Its official page uses the same three-package structure. Since the formulas are near-identical, choose on taste, caffeine preference and whichever is cheaper on the day you buy. Is Horse Boost worth the price?

It depends on what you expect. If you want a low-stimulant daily gummy built on traditional men's herbs, and you'll use the guarantee as a safety net, two or three bottles is a reasonable experiment at roughly $2.30 to $2.63 a day. If you want ingredients at the doses used in studies, single-ingredient supplements are better value. If you want a fix for erection problems, no supplement is worth the money until you've seen a doctor.

The price itself is in line with other direct-to-consumer men's supplements. What sets the value apart, for better or worse, is the label: open about which ingredients are inside, silent on how much of most of them. Factor that in when you compare it with anything else.
