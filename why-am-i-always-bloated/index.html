Why Am I Always Bloated? Common Causes and Fixes

A tight, swollen stomach after meals is common enough that most people assume it's normal. Often it is. Here's how to tell the ordinary kind from the kind worth a doctor's attention.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published October 29, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Everyday bloating usually has four sources: what you ate, how fast you ate it, the bacteria living in your gut, or hormonal shifts tied to your cycle or age.
  • Gas itself isn't the main problem. Bloating comes from stretching in the gut, and how sensitive your gut is to that stretch varies a lot from person to person.
  • Certain carbohydrates (FODMAPs) ferment faster than others and are behind a large share of diet-related bloating, beans, onions, wheat and some fruits among them.
  • Stress is a real contributor, not an excuse. The gut and nervous system are closely linked, and stress hormones can slow digestion and heighten gut sensitivity.
  • Bloating with pain, weight loss, blood in the stool, or a lasting change in bowel habits is a reason to see a doctor rather than keep adjusting your diet alone.

Feeling bloated after nearly every meal usually comes down to one of four things: how fast you ate, what you ate, the bacteria living in your gut, or hormonal shifts tied to your cycle or age. True daily bloating that doesn't let up is less common and worth a doctor's look.

It's frustrating when nothing you ate looks unusual and you still end the day with your waistband tight. Feeling bloated almost every single day wears on you, especially when the usual advice, eat slower, drink more water, doesn't seem to touch it. Bloating this routine isn't usually dangerous. It's just uncomfortable enough that people want an actual reason, not a repeated tip.

What actually causes bloating in the first place?

Bloating happens when gas, fluid or undigested food stretches your stomach or intestines more than usual. Your gut produces gas constantly as bacteria ferment fiber and sugars you can't fully digest on your own, and small amounts of that gas are completely normal. Bloating shows up when gas production outpaces how quickly your gut can move it along and release it.

The stretching itself causes the tight, swollen feeling, not the raw amount of gas in your body. Two people can produce a similar volume of gas and have very different experiences of it, because how sensitive your gut lining is to stretching varies a fair amount from person to person.

It also helps to separate two things people often lump together: bloating and distension. Bloating is the subjective feeling of fullness or pressure. Distension is an actual, measurable increase in waist size, sometimes visible enough that clothes feel tighter by the end of the day. You can have one without the other, though they frequently show up together, and distension that's visible and progressive over weeks, rather than within a single day, is more worth flagging to a doctor.

Constipation plays into this more than people expect. When stool moves through the colon more slowly than usual, there's more time for bacteria to ferment what's sitting there, and more material taking up space in the gut at once. People who are chronically constipated frequently describe bloating as one of their most noticeable symptoms, sometimes more bothersome than the constipation itself.

This is part of why fiber and fluid advice shows up so often in bloating guidance: they're aimed as much at keeping things moving as at anything else. If bloating reliably eases after a bowel movement and builds up again over the following day or two, slow transit is a reasonable first thing to address, through fiber, water, and movement, before assuming a food intolerance is to blame.

Can medications or supplements contribute to bloating?

Yes, and this gets overlooked often. Iron supplements are a common culprit, frequently causing gas, bloating and constipation as a side effect, not a sign something is wrong with how you're taking them. Some magnesium forms do the opposite, pulling water into the gut and causing looser stools alongside bloating. Antibiotics can disrupt the balance of gut bacteria for weeks after a course ends, which sometimes shows up as new or worse bloating during that recovery window.

Regular NSAID use (ibuprofen, naproxen) can also irritate the digestive tract enough to contribute to bloating and discomfort in some people. None of this means stopping a needed medication on your own. It means mentioning bloating to whoever prescribed it, since a dose adjustment or a different formulation sometimes solves it without dropping the medication altogether.

Is it mostly about what you're eating?

Diet explains a large share of everyday bloating. Certain carbohydrates, grouped under the acronym FODMAPs (fermentable oligo-, di-, monosaccharides and polyols), ferment quickly in the gut and produce more gas than other carbs do. Onions, garlic, wheat, beans and some fruits are common sources.

Eating fast and swallowing air along with food adds to the problem too. So does carbonated soda, which introduces gas directly instead of relying on bacteria to make it. None of this means cutting out entire food groups for good. It usually just means noticing patterns rather than banning ingredients outright.

Could it be a food intolerance rather than ordinary digestion?

Lactose intolerance and non-celiac gluten sensitivity are the two intolerances people ask about most, and both can produce bloating that looks like a simple food reaction at first. Lactose intolerance comes from a shortage of the enzyme that breaks down milk sugar, so dairy lingers in the gut and ferments longer than it should.

An elimination approach, cutting one suspected food for two to three weeks and reintroducing it deliberately, is the most reliable way to test this at home. Guessing based on how a single meal felt is unreliable, since bloating from an earlier meal can still be working its way through your system.

Does stress make bloating worse, or is that a myth?

It isn't a myth. The gut and the nervous system are closely linked, and stress hormones can slow digestion, change gut sensitivity and shift which bacteria thrive, all of which can make bloating more noticeable. People under chronic stress often report worse digestive symptoms in general, bloating included, even when their diet hasn't changed at all.

That doesn't mean stress-related bloating is imagined. The stretching and discomfort are physically real. It means the fix sometimes has as much to do with sleep and workload as it does with any single food swap.

How is bloating from IBS or SIBO different from everyday bloating?

Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) and small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO) both produce bloating that's more persistent and often more severe than a reaction to one meal. IBS bloating tends to track with bowel habit changes, constipation, diarrhea or both, and often eases somewhat after a bowel movement. SIBO happens when bacteria that normally live mostly in the colon migrate into the small intestine, where they ferment food earlier in digestion and produce gas sooner and more intensely.

Both conditions are diagnosed by a doctor, SIBO specifically with a breath test, not guessed at from symptoms alone. If bloating comes with ongoing pain, unpredictable bowel habits, or very little relief from dietary changes, that's a reasonable point to check in with a doctor rather than keep testing elimination diets on your own. Do hormones affect bloating, especially around your cycle or with age?

Yes. Many women notice more bloating in the days before their period, when fluctuating estrogen and progesterone affect fluid retention and gut motility. Perimenopause and menopause bring a version of this too, partly from hormonal shifts and partly from changes in gut bacteria that seem to occur alongside them.

Bloating that clusters predictably around a cycle, easing once the period starts, usually points to a hormonal pattern rather than a food problem, though the two can and do overlap.

What foods are the most common bloating triggers?

A short list of foods comes up again and again in bloating complaints, though tolerance varies a lot from person to person:

  • Beans, lentils and other legumes
  • Cruciferous vegetables like broccoli, cabbage and Brussels sprouts
  • Onions and garlic
  • Wheat and other gluten-containing grains, for some people
  • Dairy, for those with lactose intolerance
  • Carbonated drinks
  • Sugar alcohols like sorbitol and xylitol, found in some sugar-free gum and candy

What simple habits actually help reduce everyday bloating?

Eating more slowly and chewing thoroughly cuts down on swallowed air, which is a bigger contributor than most people assume. Smaller, more frequent meals tend to produce less bloating than two or three large ones for people prone to it.

Raising fiber gradually, rather than adding a large amount all at once, matters more than most fiber advice makes clear. A sudden jump in fiber is itself a common cause of new bloating, not just a fix for it. The same caution applies to probiotics and fermented foods: our guide to prebiotics vs probiotics covers how to introduce either one without making things worse first.

A short walk after eating, steady water intake through the day, and going easy on carbonated drinks round out the habits with the most consistent support.

Alcohol is worth a separate mention, since it's easy to overlook as a bloating cause. Beer carries carbonation and fermentable carbohydrates together, and alcohol in general can slow gut motility and irritate the stomach lining enough to make bloating worse the next day, not just the night of. Cutting back for a week or two is a cheap way to test how much it's actually contributing for you.

Keeping a short food and symptom diary for two to three weeks, noting what you ate, roughly when the bloating started, and how bad it felt, tends to reveal a pattern faster than trying to remember it after the fact. Most people assume they already know their trigger food. Written down over a few weeks, the actual pattern is often a surprise, and it's far easier to bring a few weeks of notes to a doctor than a vague sense that dairy maybe does it.

When does bloating need a doctor's visit instead of home fixes?

Most everyday bloating responds to diet and habit changes within a few weeks. Bloating that doesn't improve, that comes with unintentional weight loss, blood in the stool, persistent pain, or a change in bowel habits that sticks around, deserves a medical evaluation rather than more trial and error at home. Those symptoms don't automatically mean something serious, but a doctor can rule out treatable causes a self-guided elimination diet can't cover on its own.

Frequently asked questions

Does bloating mean you're gaining weight?

Usually not. Bloating is mostly temporary gas and fluid, not fat gain, and it typically goes down within hours rather than building up over weeks.

Can drinking more water reduce bloating?

Often, yes, especially if you've recently increased fiber intake. Dehydration can slow digestion and worsen constipation-related bloating, so steady water intake helps more than people expect.

Is it normal to feel bloated every single day?

Mild, predictable bloating after certain foods is common and not usually a concern. Bloating that is severe, daily and unrelenting, especially with other symptoms, is worth checking with a doctor.

Can anxiety alone cause physical bloating?

Yes. The gut and brain communicate closely through the nervous system, and stress or anxiety can slow digestion and heighten gut sensitivity enough to cause real, physical bloating.

Do probiotics help with bloating?

Some strains help some people, particularly with IBS-related bloating, but the evidence isn't universal and effects are strain-specific. It's worth trying one product at a time rather than switching often.

Does bloating get worse with age?

It can, partly from slower gut motility, partly from hormonal changes around menopause, and partly from medications that become more common later in life.

Can bloating be a sign of something serious?

Rarely, but persistent bloating paired with unintentional weight loss, blood in the stool, or ongoing pain should be evaluated by a doctor rather than managed at home indefinitely.
