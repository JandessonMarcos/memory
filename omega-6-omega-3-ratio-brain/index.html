Omega-6 to Omega-3 Ratio: Why It Matters for Brain Health

Getting "enough" omega-3 isn't the whole story if omega-6 is crowding it out. Here's what the ratio between the two actually means for your brain.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published August 8, 2026

Key takeaways

  • It's a balance, not just a total. Eating fish oil doesn't help much if omega-6 intake is still overwhelming it.
  • Modern diets skew heavily omega-6, largely from vegetable and seed oils in packaged and fried food.
  • An imbalanced ratio is linked to low-grade inflammation, which in turn is associated with worse long-term brain health.
  • Fatty fish, walnuts and flaxseed push the ratio in a better direction.
  • Small, repeatable swaps matter more than any single "superfood" meal.

Short answer: the ratio between omega-6 and omega-3 fats in your diet matters as much as how much omega-3 you get, because the two compete for the same enzymes and push the body toward either more or less inflammation. Most people hear "eat more omega-3" and stop there. Fewer hear that a diet loaded with omega-6 can blunt those benefits even when omega-3 intake looks fine on paper. Here's what the ratio actually is, why it matters for the brain, and how to nudge it back into a better range.

What exactly is the omega-6 to omega-3 ratio?

Omega-6 and omega-3 are both essential fatty acids, meaning the body can't make them and has to get them from food. They share the same conversion pathway, and that pathway mostly produces pro-inflammatory signaling molecules from omega-6 and anti-inflammatory ones from omega-3. Because they compete for the same enzymes, a diet with far more omega-6 than omega-3 tips the whole system toward inflammation, even if your absolute omega-3 intake would look adequate in isolation. DHA, an omega-3, is also a major structural fat in brain cell membranes, so the balance affects both signaling and the physical makeup of brain tissue.

What ratio should you actually be aiming for?

Anthropologists and nutrition researchers estimate that human diets evolved on a ratio somewhere close to 1:1 to 4:1, omega-6 to omega-3, based on what was available before modern food processing. Estimates for the typical Western diet today run much higher, commonly cited in the 10:1 to 20:1 range, driven largely by soybean, corn, sunflower and cottonseed oils showing up in fried food, packaged snacks and restaurant cooking. Nobody claims a precise "ideal" number works for every person, but the direction is consistent: most people would benefit from moving the ratio down, not chasing a specific target number.

How does an imbalanced ratio actually affect the brain?

The clearest mechanism runs through inflammation. A diet skewed heavily toward omega-6 tends to raise markers of low-grade, chronic inflammation, and chronic inflammation is consistently associated with worse outcomes across several measures of brain aging, including in some population studies of cognitive decline. It's worth being precise about what the evidence supports: researchers have solid data connecting the ratio to inflammatory markers and cardiovascular risk factors, and reasonable but less direct evidence connecting it to memory and cognition specifically. Think of it as one piece of a larger inflammation-and-brain-health picture rather than a single lever that determines outcomes on its own.

Which foods push the ratio in each direction?

Fatty fish like salmon, sardines, mackerel and anchovies are the most concentrated sources of the long-chain omega-3s, EPA and DHA, that matter most for brain tissue. Walnuts, chia seeds and ground flaxseed supply a plant-based omega-3 called ALA, which the body converts to EPA and DHA at a fairly low, variable rate, so they help but don't replace fish for most people. On the other side, cutting back on fried food, most packaged snacks and baked goods, and cooking oils like soybean, corn, sunflower and cottonseed oil does more to shift the ratio than adding fish alone. Olive oil and avocado oil are reasonable swaps for everyday cooking, since they're low in omega-6 without adding much omega-3 either.

Should you take a supplement to fix the ratio?

If you rarely eat fish, a fish oil or algae-based omega-3 supplement supplying EPA and DHA is a reasonable way to raise your omega-3 intake directly, and algae oil is a solid option for anyone avoiding fish. Supplementing omega-3 alone won't fully offset a diet still heavy in omega-6 from cooking oil and processed food, so think of it as one part of the plan rather than a fix by itself. Whole food sources also bring along other nutrients, like protein and vitamin D from fish, that a capsule doesn't replicate. As with any supplement, check with a doctor first if you take blood thinners, since high-dose omega-3 can have a mild blood-thinning effect of its own.

It isn't about eating more fish oil and calling it done. It's about not undoing that effort with a diet still drowning in seed oil, and that shift shows up in how your brain ages, not just your cholesterol panel.

Frequently asked questions

What is the omega-6 to omega-3 ratio?

It's the balance between two families of essential fatty acids in your diet. Omega-6 fats are mostly pro-inflammatory building blocks and are abundant in vegetable oils, while omega-3 fats, especially EPA and DHA from fish, tend to be anti-inflammatory and are concentrated in brain tissue. The ratio between how much of each you eat is thought to influence inflammation levels throughout the body, including the brain.

What is a healthy omega-6 to omega-3 ratio?

Researchers estimate that human diets evolved on a ratio somewhere near 1:1 to 4:1 of omega-6 to omega-3. Typical modern Western diets are commonly estimated to run much higher, often cited in the range of 10:1 to 20:1, largely because of how much vegetable and seed oil is in processed food.

Does a high omega-6 ratio hurt brain health?

An imbalanced ratio, with omega-6 far outweighing omega-3, is thought to promote a low-grade inflammatory state, and chronic inflammation is linked to worse cognitive outcomes over time. The research is stronger on inflammation markers and cardiovascular risk than on direct proof that the ratio alone drives memory decline, so treat it as one contributing factor rather than the whole story.

How can you improve your omega-6 to omega-3 ratio?

Eat fatty fish like salmon, sardines or mackerel a couple of times a week, cut back on fried food and processed snacks made with soybean, corn or sunflower oil, and consider a fish oil or algae-based omega-3 supplement if you rarely eat fish. Small, consistent swaps move the ratio more reliably than an occasional big change.
