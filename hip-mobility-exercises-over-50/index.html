Hip Mobility Exercises for Adults Over 50

The hip is the biggest joint most people stop moving through its full range once sitting takes over the day. Getting that range back changes how stairs, walks and getting out of a chair all feel.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published November 13, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Hip range of motion commonly declines with age, driven mostly by inactivity and prolonged sitting, not an unavoidable part of aging.
  • A short daily routine (5 to 10 minutes) is more effective than one long weekly session, because stiffness returns quickly without regular movement.
  • Hip mobility and hip strength address different problems. Mobility restores range of motion; strength lets you actually use it safely.
  • Limited hip mobility is one of several factors linked to higher fall risk in older adults, alongside weak hip muscles and slower balance reactions.
  • Most people notice easier movement within 2 to 3 weeks of consistent practice, with fuller gains around 6 to 8 weeks.

Hip mobility exercises for adults over 50 should combine gentle range-of-motion drills, like hip circles and leg swings, with light strengthening moves such as clamshells and bridges, done for 5 to 10 minutes most days. This combination restores the range sitting takes away and builds the control to use it safely during walking, stairs and getting up from a chair.

Below is why hips tighten up in the first place, the specific exercises with the best support behind them, and how to know whether what you're feeling is normal stiffness or something that needs a professional look.

Why do hips get tight after 50?

The hip is built to move through a wide range: forward, backward, out to the side and in rotation. Most daily life, especially a desk job or long stretches of sitting, only uses a narrow slice of that range. Muscles and connective tissue around the joint adapt to what they're actually asked to do, so the unused range gradually shortens. Add in some natural cartilage and joint-fluid changes that come with age, and hips that felt loose at 30 often feel noticeably stiffer by 55 or 60, even without any specific injury.

The encouraging part: this is largely a disuse problem, not a one-way deterioration. Range of motion responds to consistent movement at any age, which is the whole premise behind mobility training.

What are the best hip mobility exercises for this age group?

ExerciseWhat it targetsHow to do it
Hip circles (standing or on all fours)Overall joint range, warm-upSlow, controlled circles in each direction, 8 to 10 reps per side
Standing leg swingsFlexion/extension rangeHold a wall or chair, swing one leg forward and back, 10 reps per side
Figure-4 stretchOuter hip and glute flexibilityLying down, cross ankle over opposite knee, gently pull the lower thigh in, hold 20 to 30 seconds
ClamshellsHip abductor strength, which supports new rangeLying on your side, knees bent, lift the top knee while keeping feet together, 10 to 15 reps per side
Glute bridgesHip extension strengthLying on your back, knees bent, lift hips toward the ceiling, squeeze, lower slowly, 10 to 15 reps
Seated or standing hip flexor stretchFront-of-hip flexibility, often tight from sittingStep one leg back, gently lean forward keeping the back leg straight, hold 20 to 30 seconds per side

None of these require equipment, and most physical therapy protocols for hip stiffness in this age group use some version of this same set: a brief dynamic warm-up, a couple of static stretches held long enough to matter, and light strengthening to support the new range.

How often should you do hip mobility work?

Daily or close to it. Mobility gains fade faster than strength gains do, so a 5 to 10 minute routine done most days produces better results than a single 45-minute session once a week. Many people find mornings work well, since hips often feel stiffest right after waking or after long sitting, but any consistent time of day you'll actually stick with is the right one.

Can hip mobility exercises help with balance and fall risk?

There's a real mechanical reason to think so. Hip extension and rotation are both involved in the quick corrective step your body takes when you stumble, and in the push-off phase of ordinary walking. Limited hip mobility is one of several factors researchers associate with higher fall risk in older adults, alongside weak hip and ankle muscles and slower balance reactions. Mobility work alone isn't a complete fall-prevention program, but it removes one contributing factor that's genuinely fixable with regular practice.

What's the difference between hip mobility and hip strength?

Mobility is how far the joint can move. Strength is how much control and force you have through that range. A hip that has plenty of range but weak surrounding muscles doesn't necessarily translate into safer, more stable movement. That's why the exercise list above pairs range-of-motion drills with strengthening moves like clamshells and bridges rather than stretching alone. Research on fall prevention and joint health consistently favors combined mobility-and-strength programs over either piece by itself.

Are there exercises to avoid if you have hip arthritis?

Deep, forced stretching into pain and high-impact movements, like lunges with a big range or anything that produces a sharp, pinching sensation, are generally discouraged with hip osteoarthritis. Gentle, pain-free range-of-motion work is still recommended, since avoiding movement altogether tends to make stiffness worse over time, not better. If a specific exercise consistently causes pain rather than a mild stretch feeling, skip it and ask a physical therapist for an alternative that targets the same area.

How long before you notice a difference?

Many people report feeling looser within 2 to 3 weeks of doing a short routine daily, often noticing it first in easier transitions, standing up from a low chair, getting in and out of a car. More complete gains in range of motion, and the strength to use it confidently, generally take 6 to 8 weeks of consistent work, in line with typical physical therapy timelines for mobility-focused programs.

Can hip mobility exercises help lower back pain too?

Often, yes. Tight hip flexors pull on the pelvis and limited hip rotation forces the lower back to compensate during everyday movement like turning or bending, which is a frequent contributor to lower back discomfort. Many people who work on hip mobility report some easing of lower back tension as a side effect, though it won't resolve every cause of back pain, especially anything involving a disc or nerve issue that needs its own evaluation.

Hips don't stiffen overnight, and they don't loosen overnight either. The people who keep good hip range into their 70s and 80s are almost always the ones who moved through it consistently, not the ones who found one perfect stretch.

When should you see a doctor or physical therapist?

Book an appointment for pain that's sharp rather than a mild stretch sensation, pain that started suddenly after a fall or injury, a hip that catches, locks or gives way, or stiffness that hasn't improved after several weeks of consistent, gentle practice. A physical therapist can also check whether what feels like a hip problem is actually coming from the lower back or knee, which happens more often than people expect.

How do you fit this into a daily routine?

Attaching the routine to something you already do daily, right after brushing your teeth, before your morning coffee, during the opening credits of a show, makes it far more likely to stick than relying on willpower alone. Five exercises done for 30 to 45 seconds each takes well under 10 minutes total. Frequently asked questions

How often should I do hip mobility exercises?

Most physical therapy guidance suggests daily or near-daily mobility work, since stiffness tends to return quickly without regular movement. A short 5 to 10 minute routine done most days beats one long session a week.

Is it normal for hips to get tighter after 50?

Some loss of range of motion is common with age, driven by less active movement through a full range, cartilage changes and often more time spent sitting. Common doesn't mean fixed. Mobility work reliably improves range of motion at any age, including well past 50.

Can tight hips really affect balance and falls?

Yes. Hip extension and rotation are both involved in catching yourself during a stumble and in the push-off phase of a normal step. Limited hip mobility is one of several factors researchers link to higher fall risk in older adults, alongside weak hip muscles and poor balance reactions.

Should I stretch or strengthen my hips first?

Ideally both, since they address different problems. Stretching and mobility drills restore range of motion; strengthening gives you the muscle control to actually use that range safely during walking, stairs and daily tasks. Mobility without strength underneath it doesn't hold up well.

Is it safe to do hip mobility exercises with arthritis?

Gentle, pain-free range-of-motion work is generally recommended for hip osteoarthritis, and avoiding movement altogether tends to make stiffness worse. Anything that causes sharp pain, not a mild stretch sensation, should be scaled back, and a physical therapist can tailor the exact exercises to your joint.

How long before I notice a difference?

Many people notice easier movement within 2 to 3 weeks of consistent daily practice. Lasting gains in range of motion and the strength to use it typically take 6 to 8 weeks of regular work, similar to most physical therapy timelines.

Can hip mobility work help lower back pain too?

Often, yes. Tight hip flexors and limited hip rotation force the lower back to compensate during everyday movement, which is a common contributor to lower back discomfort. Improving hip mobility frequently eases some of that compensation, though it isn't a fix for every cause of back pain.
