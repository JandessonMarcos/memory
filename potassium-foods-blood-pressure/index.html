Potassium-Rich Foods and Blood Pressure: What to Eat and How Much You Need

Potassium gets far less attention than sodium, but the two work as a pair. Here's what the evidence says about eating more of it, and who needs to be careful.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published November 5, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Potassium helps the kidneys excrete sodium and eases tension in blood vessel walls, and diets rich in it are linked to lower blood pressure.
  • Most adults need roughly 2,600 to 3,400 mg of potassium a day, and most Americans get well under that from food alone.
  • Food beats supplements for this. Potatoes, beans, leafy greens, bananas and yogurt all deliver potassium along with fiber and other nutrients a pill can't.
  • Potassium works best alongside less sodium, not as a stand-alone fix. The two are opposite sides of the same equation.
  • People with kidney disease or on certain blood pressure drugs need to be careful and should ask a doctor before deliberately increasing potassium.

Potassium-rich foods are linked to lower blood pressure because potassium helps the kidneys get rid of excess sodium and eases tension in the walls of blood vessels. Bananas, potatoes, beans, leafy greens, yogurt and salmon are all strong sources. Most adults need roughly 2,600 to 3,400 mg a day, and most Americans eat far less. Getting there with food, not supplements, is the safer route for almost everyone.

Sodium gets most of the attention in conversations about blood pressure. Potassium is the quieter half of the same story, and it deserves more airtime than it gets. Here's how it works, which foods actually deliver it, and who needs to be careful before piling on more.

What does potassium actually do for blood pressure?

Potassium is a mineral your body uses to manage fluid balance, nerve signals and muscle contractions, including the muscle in your blood vessel walls. Two mechanisms matter most for blood pressure. First, potassium helps your kidneys excrete more sodium in urine, which reduces the fluid volume your heart has to pump against. Second, it appears to relax the smooth muscle lining your arteries, which can ease resistance to blood flow.

Sodium and potassium work in opposition inside your cells, through a pump that trades one for the other across cell membranes. A diet heavy in sodium and light in potassium, which describes a lot of modern eating, pushes that balance in the direction linked to higher blood pressure.

There's a third mechanism researchers point to as well: potassium appears to blunt some of the activity of the renin-angiotensin system, a hormone pathway that narrows blood vessels and signals the kidneys to hold onto sodium. It's the same system several classes of blood pressure medication target directly, which is part of why diet and medication can work along similar lines without being the same thing.

How did we end up eating so little potassium and so much sodium?

The modern food supply didn't happen by accident. Fresh produce, beans and unprocessed meat naturally run high in potassium and low in sodium. Processed and restaurant food flips that ratio: manufacturers add sodium for flavor and shelf life, and the potassium that would have come with a whole food often gets stripped out along the way. A diet built mostly around packaged and restaurant meals can end up with a sodium-to-potassium ratio many times higher than one built around produce, beans and plain proteins, even if the total calories look similar.

That shift happened gradually over decades, which is partly why it doesn't register as unusual. Older dietary patterns, including the way most humans ate before industrial food processing, were far closer to the sodium-potassium balance researchers now recommend working back toward.

What does the evidence actually show?

Large reviews pooling dozens of clinical trials have found that higher potassium intake is associated with modestly lower blood pressure, with the biggest effect in people who already have hypertension and eat a lot of sodium. The reductions in these trials are real but moderate, often in the range researchers describe as clinically meaningful rather than dramatic, closer to a few points on each number.

The strongest population-level evidence comes from dietary patterns rather than single foods. The DASH diet, built around vegetables, fruit, beans and low-fat dairy, happens to be high in potassium along with magnesium and calcium, and it has one of the best-studied track records for lowering blood pressure of any eating pattern researchers have tested. In the original DASH trial, the diet lowered blood pressure within two weeks, faster than most people expect from a dietary change alone.

Separately, observational studies following large groups of people over many years have generally linked higher potassium intake with a lower risk of stroke. That kind of study can't prove potassium itself causes the lower risk, since people who eat more potassium-rich food often have other healthy habits too, but the pattern has shown up consistently enough across different populations that most nutrition researchers take it seriously as supporting evidence rather than a coincidence.

How much potassium do you need per day?

The National Academies set adequate intake at 3,400 mg a day for adult men and 2,600 mg for adult women. Those are higher than the older 4,700 mg figure many people still see quoted online; the numbers were revised downward in 2019 based on updated evidence, though the general message, eat more, didn't change. Surveys consistently find that most American adults land well below even the lower figure, often closer to 2,300 mg a day.

Pairing that target with a sodium goal matters more than chasing potassium in isolation. Federal dietary guidelines recommend capping sodium at 2,300 mg a day, with an even lower 1,500 mg suggested for people with high blood pressure specifically. Most adults in the US eat well above the higher figure, so working on both numbers at once, more potassium-rich food and fewer heavily processed items, tends to move the needle faster than focusing on either one alone.

Potatoes and beans often surprise people. Bananas have a reputation as the potassium food, but a medium potato with the skin on actually carries more. Spreading potassium-rich foods across meals, rather than relying on one food, is an easier way to hit the daily target without really trying.

Canned versions of beans and vegetables still carry most of the potassium of their fresh counterparts, as long as you're eating the food itself rather than discarding it. Draining and rinsing canned beans cuts some sodium without losing much potassium, which makes canned beans a practical, inexpensive way to hit the daily target on a busy week. Canned soups and vegetables packed with heavy sodium are a different story; check the label rather than assuming.

Dried fruit, nuts and dairy round out a realistic weekly list beyond the top entries in the table: raisins, apricots, milk, and even plain baked beans all contribute meaningfully. None of these foods need to be eaten in large quantities on their own; the daily target is realistic to hit across a normal day of eating once a few of these show up regularly instead of rarely.

Who benefits most from eating more potassium?

People with high or borderline-high blood pressure who currently eat a typical Western diet, heavy in processed and restaurant food, stand to gain the most, since that pattern tends to run high in sodium and low in potassium at the same time. Anyone already eating a produce-heavy, DASH-style diet has likely closed much of that gap already.

Who should be careful with potassium, or skip it?

This is the part that gets skipped in casual health advice, and it matters. People with chronic kidney disease often can't clear excess potassium efficiently, and too much can build up to dangerous levels affecting heart rhythm. The same caution applies to anyone taking ACE inhibitors, ARBs, or potassium-sparing diuretics, common blood pressure medications that already raise potassium levels on their own.

If you fall into either group, talk to your doctor before deliberately ramping up potassium-rich foods, and never start a potassium supplement or salt substitute without medical guidance. This is one case where more is not automatically safer, even though the general population message is to eat more.

Too much potassium in the blood, a condition called hyperkalemia, can cause muscle weakness, tingling, nausea and, at higher levels, dangerous irregular heart rhythms. It's uncommon in people with healthy kidneys because the kidneys adjust excretion to match intake, which is exactly the regulation that breaks down in kidney disease. People with CKD are often placed on a lower-potassium diet by their care team, sometimes needing to actively limit some of the same foods, like potatoes and beans, that this article recommends for everyone else. That's not a contradiction; it's a reflection of how differently the kidneys handle potassium once their function is impaired.

Are potassium supplements a good idea?

For most healthy adults, food is the better route for a simple reason: potassium supplement doses are capped fairly low by regulation, around 99 mg per tablet in the US, specifically because high-dose potassium pills can cause dangerous heart rhythm problems if taken carelessly. You would need many pills to match what one baked potato provides, and food brings fiber, magnesium and other nutrients along for the ride.

Potassium chloride salt substitutes are a separate category, used by some people to cut sodium while adding potassium at the same time. A large randomized trial conducted in China, following thousands of adults with a stroke history or high blood pressure, found a salt substitute blending sodium chloride with potassium chloride lowered both blood pressure and the rate of stroke and cardiovascular events compared with regular salt. That's a meaningful result, but the trial excluded people with significant kidney disease for safety reasons, which is exactly the group that needs to stay away from these products without medical supervision.

How does potassium compare with just cutting sodium?

Neither one does the whole job alone. Cutting sodium lowers blood pressure in most people who eat a high-sodium diet to begin with, and the effect is well documented. Raising potassium works through a different pathway and adds an effect on top, which is part of why DASH, which does both at once, outperforms sodium reduction by itself in head-to-head trials. Think of them as two levers on the same machine rather than competing strategies.

People sometimes ask which lever to pull first if they can only manage one change. In practice, adding potassium-rich food tends to be the easier habit to start, since it's a positive change, eat more of this, rather than a restriction. Cutting sodium usually means reading labels and cooking more from scratch, which takes longer to become automatic. Starting with potassium-rich food and letting sodium reduction follow gradually is a reasonable, realistic sequence for most people, even if doing both together works fastest.

When should you see a doctor about blood pressure?

Diet changes are a reasonable first step for blood pressure that's mildly elevated, but they're not a substitute for monitoring. See a doctor if your blood pressure readings are consistently at or above 130/80, if you're already on blood pressure medication and considering dietary changes that affect potassium, or if you have any kidney or heart condition. Frequently asked questions

Does a banana have more potassium than a potato?

No. A medium potato with the skin on has more potassium than a medium banana, even though bananas get most of the attention.

Can you get too much potassium from food alone?

It's rare in people with normal kidney function, since healthy kidneys adjust how much they excrete. The risk rises sharply with kidney disease or certain medications.

Does cooking affect how much potassium a food has?

Boiling can leach a meaningful amount of potassium out of vegetables into the water. Steaming, roasting or microwaving keeps more of it intact.

Is a potassium supplement the same as eating potassium-rich food?

Not really. Over-the-counter potassium pills are capped at a low dose for safety reasons, so they can't replace the amount a potassium-rich meal provides, and food adds fiber and other nutrients a pill doesn't.

How fast can eating more potassium change blood pressure?

Some studies show measurable change within a few weeks of a sustained dietary shift, though individual results vary and the effect is usually modest rather than dramatic.

Are potassium-sparing diuretics the same as potassium supplements?

No. They're a class of blood pressure or heart medication that causes the body to retain more potassium, which is a different mechanism and a reason to avoid stacking extra potassium on top without medical advice.

Does salt substitute really help blood pressure?

Research on potassium-based salt substitutes has shown blood pressure benefits in the general population, but they are not safe for everyone, particularly people with kidney disease.
