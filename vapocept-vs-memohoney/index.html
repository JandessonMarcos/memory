VapoCept vs MemoHoney: Two One-a-Day Ginkgo Formulas, Compared Fairly

Same format, same price per bottle, same 60-day window. One shows you every milligram; the other bets on blood flow with a broader formula. Here's how they really differ.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published November 9, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Both are one capsule a day with ginkgo in them, 30 capsules a bottle, stimulant-free and sold through official checkouts.
  • VapoCept prints every dose, including 100 mg of a 4:1 ginkgo extract. MemoHoney lists ginkgo without an amount.
  • MemoHoney is the broader formula. Twelve ingredients, seven dosed, built around nitric oxide and blood flow, with bacopa, astaxanthin and blueberry undisclosed.
  • Prices match. About $2.63, $2.30 or $1.63 a day by package for either, with $9.99 shipping on the 2-bottle option.
  • The cautions overlap but aren't identical. Both: blood thinners. MemoHoney adds blood pressure drugs, nitrates and PDE5 inhibitors. VapoCept adds shellfish allergy.

VapoCept vs MemoHoney is a close call because both are one-capsule-a-day formulas with ginkgo. The difference is disclosure and approach. VapoCept prints all six doses, including 100 mg of ginkgo extract. MemoHoney's broader blood-flow formula prints seven of twelve doses and leaves ginkgo, bacopa and three others unquantified. Prices and the 60-day window match.

If you've been comparing "VapoCept reviews" and "MemoHoney reviews," you've probably noticed how similar the two pitches sound: one capsule, better focus, no stimulants, a two-month guarantee. Underneath, the two labels make very different promises about what you can check. That's what this page is about.

Why does it matter that MemoHoney hides its ginkgo dose?

Because ginkgo is the ingredient both formulas are partly selling, and dose is how you check it against the research. Ginkgo trials mostly used 120 to 240 mg a day of a standardized extract. With VapoCept, you can see the 100 mg and judge it. With MemoHoney, you can't.

Our MemoHoney review did the physical math. A standard capsule holds roughly 700 to 800 mg of powder, and about 665 mg is already taken by MemoHoney's disclosed ingredients. That leaves a small remainder to share among ginkgo, bacopa, astaxanthin, blueberry and honey. Whatever the exact split, ginkgo is likely present as an accent rather than at a research level.

To be fair, VapoCept's printed number doesn't settle everything either. Its ginkgo is a 4:1 extract, about four parts leaf to one part extract, while the trial extract EGb 761 is far more concentrated and standardized to 24% flavone glycosides and 6% terpene lactones. So 100 mg of VapoCept's ginkgo isn't equivalent to 100 mg of the trial version. The difference is that you know what you're working with, and you can weigh it. How do VapoCept and MemoHoney work differently?

Both lean on blood flow. They get there by different routes.

MemoHoney's engine is nitric oxide. Four of its seven dosed ingredients, two forms of L-arginine and two of L-citrulline, are there to raise nitric oxide, which relaxes blood vessels. Together they total 600 mg, well below the several grams used in circulation research. Undisclosed ginkgo and bacopa add the botanical side. Our review called the thesis legitimate and the execution partly right.

VapoCept leads with ginkgo itself, the botanical most studied for cerebral blood flow, and adds turmeric for inflammation, lutein as a carotenoid antioxidant and green tea polyphenols. The supporting doses are small: lutein at about 1.25 mg is near what you'd get from food, and green tea at 15 mg is a fraction of a cup. Its largest ingredient, glucosamine at 250 mg, is a joint ingredient with little evidence for thinking.

Which has better evidence behind it?

Neither has a published trial on its finished formula, so both rest on ingredient research. That's the most important thing to know, and it applies to almost every product in this aisle. Ingredient evidence isn't product evidence.

On ingredients, MemoHoney has the more interesting list. Bacopa has the strongest memory-specific trial record of any botanical. Ginkgo has the largest blood-flow literature. Astaxanthin and blueberry have smaller, real bodies of work. The catch is that none of those four carry an amount, so you can't tell whether you're getting a research dose or a trace. Its dosed half, the amino acids, sits well under studied amounts.

VapoCept's list is narrower but checkable. Its ginkgo is the most meaningful line. Turmeric, lutein and green tea are present at modest amounts, and glucosamine doesn't belong to the memory literature at all. One observational study even linked glucosamine use with faster progression in people with mild cognitive impairment, covered in our report on that study. That's an association, not proof, but worth knowing.

Our honest read: MemoHoney may contain more good ingredients, and VapoCept lets you see exactly how much of its good ingredient you get. Which matters more depends on whether you value breadth or verifiability. There's no shame in either choice. A buyer who reads labels closely will usually prefer the one with nothing to hide, while a buyer who trusts the overall idea of a formula may prefer the longer list. Just be clear with yourself about which kind of buyer you are.

Which is easier to take, and how should you test it?

They're nearly tied here, which is one of the best things about both. A single capsule a day is the format most people actually stick with for three months, and sticking with it is what decides whether any supplement gets a fair test.

The directions differ slightly. VapoCept's label asks for one capsule with an 8 oz glass of water, ideally 20 to 30 minutes before a meal. MemoHoney is one capsule with water, with no timing rule. If you like a fixed ritual, VapoCept's before-breakfast slot is easy to anchor. If your mornings are unpredictable, MemoHoney's looser instruction may suit you better.

Whichever you choose, test it the same way. Take a short memory test before the first capsule, write one line a day about sleep and focus, and retest around day 50 under similar conditions. That gives you a real before-and-after number to bring to your refund decision, instead of trying to remember how forgetful you were two months ago.

Which is safer for people on medication?

Both deserve a doctor's conversation if you take blood thinners or antiplatelet drugs such as warfarin, apixaban, clopidogrel or daily aspirin, because both contain ginkgo. Beyond that, the lists diverge.

  • MemoHoney adds blood pressure medication, nitrates such as nitroglycerin and PDE5 inhibitors such as sildenafil, because its arginine and citrulline work on the same vessel-relaxing pathway. Niacin flushing is possible but unlikely at 7.5 mg.
  • VapoCept adds shellfish allergy, since glucosamine is often shellfish-derived, and turmeric adds a little more to the clotting question. Glucosamine has also been reported to strengthen warfarin's effect. The capsule is bovine gelatin.
  • Both: bleeding disorders, surgery within two weeks, pregnancy and nursing, and anyone under 18 should check first.

If you're on blood pressure medication, VapoCept has the shorter caution list. If you have a shellfish allergy, MemoHoney avoids that specific issue. Whatever you choose, bring the actual label, or a clear photo of it, to your doctor or pharmacist. A printed panel is far easier for them to assess than a product name they may never have heard of.

Who should choose VapoCept, and who should choose MemoHoney?

A fair split looks like this:

  • Choose VapoCept if you want to verify every milligram, you take blood pressure medication or nitrates, you prefer a smaller, simpler formula, and your goal is day-to-day focus and clarity.
  • Choose MemoHoney if you're drawn to the nitric oxide and blood-flow approach, you want bacopa and antioxidants in the same capsule, you have a shellfish allergy, and you're comfortable not knowing the exact ginkgo and bacopa amounts.
  • Choose neither, for now, if you take blood thinners and haven't spoken with your doctor, or if your memory is getting noticeably worse. That needs a medical evaluation first.

Don't take both. They'd double up on ginkgo, and you wouldn't learn which one is doing anything. If you'd also like to see how a bacopa-led two-capsule formula compares with MemoHoney, our Memopezil vs MemoHoney comparison covers it.

Frequently asked questions

Does VapoCept contain honey?

No. VapoCept's six ingredients are glucosamine, ginkgo, turmeric, marigold extract for lutein, green tea and bamboo leaf. Honey is a MemoHoney ingredient, and even there it's undisclosed and present in a small amount; the name is branding rather than a description of the formula.

Is MemoHoney a honey product you eat by the spoon?

No. MemoHoney is a capsule, one a day, 30 per bottle. Honey is one of its twelve listed ingredients, without a disclosed amount. Don't confuse it with honey sold in jars.

How much ginkgo is in MemoHoney?

The label doesn't say. Ginkgo is listed without an amount. Based on capsule size and the 665 mg of disclosed ingredients, the remaining room for five undisclosed botanicals is small, so ginkgo is unlikely to reach the 120 to 240 mg used in most trials.

Is 100 mg of ginkgo in VapoCept a clinical dose?

Not directly. The milligram number is near the low end of the trial range, but VapoCept uses a 4:1 extract while most trials used EGb 761, a far more concentrated, standardized extract. Treat VapoCept's ginkgo as a gentler version of what was studied.

Can I switch from MemoHoney to VapoCept?

Yes. There's no known need for a long gap between supplements like these, but don't overlap them, since both contain ginkgo. Finish one, take a baseline memory test, then start the other so you can judge it on its own.

Which one works faster?

Neither is fast. MemoHoney's nitric oxide ingredients act within hours if they do anything, though at these doses most people won't notice. The ginkgo in both, and MemoHoney's bacopa, are cumulative and studied over 8 to 12 weeks or more.

Are VapoCept and MemoHoney made by the same company?

Their labels name different details. VapoCept's lists Gex Corporation LLP of Ogden, Utah, as distributor. We don't have evidence that the two share an owner, so judge each on its own label and terms.
