Sleep Hygiene Checklist: 12 Habits That Actually Matter

Sleep hygiene isn't one trick. It's a short list of habits that, done together for a couple of weeks, make falling and staying asleep noticeably easier for most people.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published November 2, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Sleep hygiene is a set of roughly 12 habits, not a single fix: timing, light, temperature, substances and wind-down routine all play a part.
  • A consistent wake time matters more than a strict bedtime. Waking up at the same hour, weekends included, anchors your body clock better than chasing an early lights-out.
  • Light is the strongest lever you control. Bright light in the morning and dim light at night do more for sleep timing than almost anything else on this list.
  • Caffeine has a longer tail than most people think. Its half-life runs 5 to 6 hours, so a 3 p.m. coffee is still partly in your system at 9 p.m.
  • Give any change 2 to 3 weeks before judging it. One good or bad night rarely means much; the pattern over weeks is what counts.

A solid sleep hygiene checklist covers about 12 habits: a consistent wake time, morning light, a cool dark room, an evening caffeine and alcohol cutoff, screens out of the bedroom, and a short wind-down routine. None of them works like a switch. Stacked together over two or three weeks, they tend to make sleep noticeably more consistent.

"Sleep hygiene" is a slightly clinical name for ordinary habits, and it gets blamed for more than it can fix. It helps healthy sleepers sleep better and helps mild, situational sleep trouble resolve. It is not a treatment for insomnia disorder, sleep apnea or a mood disorder keeping you up, though it's still worth doing alongside treatment for any of those.

What is sleep hygiene, and why does it matter?

Sleep hygiene is the set of daily habits and bedroom conditions that influence how easily you fall asleep and how well you stay asleep. The term comes from sleep medicine, where it's used as a first-line, low-risk step before trying medication for mild or situational sleep complaints. It matters because so much of it is within your control, unlike, say, a diagnosed sleep disorder, which usually needs its own treatment.

It also matters because the habits interact. Bright light in the morning, for instance, makes it easier for your body to produce melatonin on schedule that night. Skip the first and the second gets harder, even if you do everything else right.

Which sleep hygiene habit matters most?

If you only fix one thing, fix your wake time. Sleep researchers generally rate a consistent wake-up time as more useful than a fixed bedtime, because your body's clock reacts strongly to when the day starts and far less predictably to when you decide to lie down. People who sleep in on weekends and wake early on weekdays create a kind of mini jet lag, sometimes called social jet lag, that can leave Monday mornings feeling worse than they should.

Pick a wake time you can hold within about an hour, seven days a week, for a few weeks before judging anything else on this list. Everything else works better once that's steady.

Does screen time before bed really hurt sleep?

The honest answer is: a little, and mostly for a reason that has nothing to do with blue light. Research on screens and sleep is messier than headlines suggest. Blue light does suppress melatonin somewhat, but the bigger issue for most people is what they're doing on the screen: scrolling an engaging feed, a tense conversation, or work email that raises stress right before trying to power down.

A practical compromise beats an all-or-nothing ban. If you read on a tablet or phone, turn on night mode or a warm filter, keep the brightness low, and avoid anything genuinely stressful or stimulating in the last 30 minutes. Charging your phone outside the bedroom removes both the light and the temptation to check it at 2 a.m., which tends to matter more than the light itself.

What's the ideal bedroom temperature for sleep?

Most sleep guidance lands between 60 and 67°F (about 15 to 19°C), cooler than many bedrooms run. Your core body temperature naturally dips as you fall asleep, and a warm room works against that dip. If 60°F sounds extreme, start by nudging the thermostat down a couple of degrees and see how it feels before committing to the low end.

Breathable bedding, a fan for airflow, and a lighter duvet in warmer months do more for a lot of people than the thermostat number alone. A room that's too cold causes its own problems, so treat the range as a starting point, not a rule.

When should you cut off caffeine and alcohol before bed?

Caffeine's half-life is roughly 5 to 6 hours in most adults, though it varies by genetics, age and liver function. That means a cup at 3 p.m. still has about half its caffeine active in your system at 9 p.m. A conservative, widely cited cutoff is early-to-mid afternoon, earlier still if you're sensitive or over 50, since caffeine clearance tends to slow with age.

Alcohol is trickier because it can make you feel sleepy at first while making the second half of the night worse. It relaxes you and can speed up falling asleep, then fragments sleep a few hours later as your body processes it, often with more awakenings and lighter, less restorative sleep. If you drink, doing it earlier in the evening and stopping a few hours before bed limits the damage more than drinking the same amount closer to lights-out.

Does napping work against good sleep hygiene?

Not automatically. A short nap, 20 to 30 minutes, before mid-afternoon, generally doesn't hurt nighttime sleep for most people and can genuinely help daytime alertness. Problems start with naps that run long, happen late in the day, or become a daily crutch for sleep debt that's really a nighttime problem.

If you're sleeping poorly at night and also napping for an hour or more every afternoon, the nap is likely propping up a schedule that needs fixing at the source. Cutting naps temporarily is a reasonable experiment if your nights aren't improving.

Does when you exercise affect your sleep hygiene?

Regular exercise is one of the more reliable, if under-discussed, sleep hygiene habits. People who move most days tend to fall asleep faster and spend more time in deeper sleep stages than people who are mostly sedentary, and the effect holds across age groups. The timing question gets more attention than it probably deserves: for a long time, the standard advice was to avoid exercise for several hours before bed because it raises core temperature and heart rate.

Newer research softens that rule. Moderate exercise within a few hours of bedtime doesn't meaningfully hurt sleep for most people, and for some it helps by burning off leftover tension from the day. What still seems to matter is intensity: a hard, late-evening workout that leaves you wired and overheated close to bedtime is more likely to delay sleep than a brisk walk or an easy session. If you only have evenings free to train, don't skip exercise over this; just notice whether your particular routine and intensity seem to help or hurt, and adjust from there.

What should a wind-down routine actually look like?

Twenty to thirty minutes of low-stimulation, low-light activity: reading something undemanding, light stretching, a warm shower, or just sitting with low lights rather than bright overheads. The specific activity matters less than the consistency. A routine you repeat most nights becomes a cue, the same way a bedtime story works for a child, except the cue here is for your own nervous system.

Keep the routine realistic. A 45-minute ritual you only manage twice a week does less than a 15-minute one you actually do most nights. Simple habits beat elaborate ones if the elaborate version doesn't survive a busy Tuesday.

What's the easiest habit to start with?

Trying to adopt all 12 habits in one week is a common way to quit after four days. Pick one or two to start, run them for a week, then add another. A sensible starting pair is a consistent wake time plus getting outside for a few minutes of morning light; both are free, take almost no extra time, and reinforce each other.

Save the harder changes, cutting afternoon caffeine or moving the TV out of the bedroom, for once the easy wins feel automatic. Habits that require willpower every single night tend to fade by week three. Habits that only require one decision, like where you charge your phone, tend to stick.

How long does it take for a sleep hygiene checklist to work?

Give it 2 to 3 weeks of reasonably consistent effort before deciding whether it's working. A single rough night, or a single great one, tells you very little; your body needs repeated cues to recalibrate, and a wake-time shift alone can take several days to settle in. People often judge too fast and abandon a habit right before it would have paid off.

Track a few basics instead of guessing: roughly when you fell asleep, how many times you woke, and how rested you felt the next day. A simple note on your phone is enough; you don't need a sleep tracker to see a trend over two weeks. When is poor sleep more than a habits problem?

See a doctor if good sleep hygiene for several weeks hasn't meaningfully helped, if you're exhausted despite spending enough time in bed, if you snore heavily or a partner notices pauses in your breathing, or if low mood or anxiety seem tangled up with the sleep trouble. Insomnia disorder, sleep apnea and restless legs syndrome all need their own diagnosis and treatment, and no checklist substitutes for that.

Frequently asked questions

Is it bad to sleep with the TV on?

It's not ideal. Light and sound from a TV can interrupt the deeper stages of sleep even if you don't fully wake up and don't remember it in the morning. A sleep timer that shuts it off after you doze is a reasonable middle ground if you rely on background noise or light to fall asleep.

Can a short nap really ruin a night of sleep?

A 20 to 30 minute nap before mid-afternoon rarely does. Long naps, especially after 4 p.m., are more likely to reduce your sleep drive by bedtime and push your sleep later.

Does a weighted blanket help with sleep hygiene?

Some people find the gentle pressure calming, and small studies suggest a modest benefit for anxiety-related sleep trouble. It's a reasonable comfort tool, not a core sleep hygiene habit with strong evidence behind it.

Is reading on a phone with night mode on okay before bed?

It's a workable compromise. Night mode and low brightness cut most of the blue-light concern; the content you're reading and how stimulating it is probably matters more than the screen itself.

How fast should I expect sleep hygiene changes to kick in?

Give it 2 to 3 weeks of fairly consistent habits before judging. Some people notice a difference within days; for most, the pattern only becomes clear after a couple of weeks.

Do these habits work for shift workers?

The principles still apply, but the clock shifts: morning light becomes light right after your shift ends, and the dark, cool bedroom matters even more since you're sleeping against your body's natural rhythm. Blackout curtains and a strict wind-down routine help shift workers more than most.

Should pets sleep in the bedroom?

It depends on the pet and the person. Studies on this are mixed, some owners sleep fine with a pet in the room and others report more awakenings. If you wake up when your pet moves or scratches at the door, that's a reasonable sign to try a few nights without them and compare.
