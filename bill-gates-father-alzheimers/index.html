Bill Gates on His Father's Alzheimer's — and What It Taught Him

Bill Gates Sr. had Alzheimer's disease. His son has written and spoken about the experience in public — here is what he shared, what it meant for his research agenda, and what every family can take from it.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  June 24, 2026  ·  Updated November 19, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Bill Gates Sr. had Alzheimer's and passed away in September 2020 at age 94.
  • Gates spoke publicly about his father's illness in a January 2018 TV interview and wrote a tribute on GatesNotes.com after his death.
  • Personal motivation: Gates has tied his Alzheimer's funding to what the disease has done to members of his own family.
  • No supplement link: Gates has never endorsed a brain pill or memory supplement — ads claiming otherwise are scams.
  • Reader takeaway: lifestyle habits, early detection, and talking to your doctor are the most evidence-based tools available today.

When Bill Gates said publicly in January 2018 that his father had Alzheimer's disease, he put one of the world's best-known families behind a struggle millions of families share. Bill Gates Sr. — William H. Gates II — was a renowned attorney who co-founded the law firm now known as K&L Gates, an Army veteran who served in Tokyo after World War II, a leader of the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation who helped launch its work in global health, and one of the most respected civic figures in Seattle. This article summarizes what Gates has shared, why it shaped his research agenda, and what practical lessons any family can draw from the experience.

Did Bill Gates Sr. have Alzheimer's disease?

Yes. William H. Gates II — widely known as Bill Gates Sr. — was publicly known to have Alzheimer's disease in his later years. In January 2018, his son told NBC's TODAY that his 92-year-old father had the disease. He died of Alzheimer's on September 14, 2020, at the age of 94 at his beach home in Washington state, the family announced. In a tribute, Bill Gates wrote that his father's "wisdom, generosity, empathy, and humility had a huge influence on people around the world."

Gates Sr. remained active well into his eighties, co-chairing the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation and continuing to champion causes including progressive taxation and legal services for the poor. This makes the progressive nature of his Alzheimer's all the more striking to those who knew him. Like millions of families, the Gates family lived through the disease's slow progression.

What has Bill Gates written about his father's illness?

Gates has kept the details of his father's illness largely private. In a 2018 interview he said simply, "I have a father who's affected deeply by it." When he announced his first major Alzheimer's investment in 2017, he said the disease had struck members of his own family and that he knew how awful it is to watch people you love struggle as it robs them of their mental capacity.

The family had access to excellent medical care, yet there was still no treatment that could stop the disease. Gates's public focus since then has been on finding ways to detect and treat Alzheimer's far earlier, rather than only managing its later stages.

Critically: Gates has never said that any supplement, pill, or consumer product changed his father's outcome or protected his own brain. Viral online ads that use his image to promote "brain pills" are fabricated scams. See our full explainer on why there is no Bill Gates brain pill and how to spot the fraud.

How did his father's illness shape Gates's Alzheimer's research agenda?

Gates has tied his Alzheimer's funding to his family's experience with the disease. In November 2017, he announced a personal commitment of $100 million to Alzheimer's research, separate from the Gates Foundation: $50 million to the Dementia Discovery Fund (DDF), a venture fund backing early-stage therapies, and $50 million for startups pursuing less mainstream approaches.

In his public comments around that announcement, Gates explained his reasoning: Alzheimer's affects tens of millions of people and their families worldwide, it has no cure, and its emotional and financial toll is growing as people live longer. Research supports the urgency of acting early: in inherited Alzheimer's, amyloid deposits show up on brain scans about 15 years before expected symptoms (Bateman et al., 2012).

In July 2018 he helped launch the Alzheimer's Drug Discovery Foundation's Diagnostics Accelerator, a $30 million, three-year program aimed at developing tests, including blood tests, that can detect Alzheimer's earlier. Blood markers such as p-tau217 have since shown accuracy close to spinal fluid tests and PET scans in research settings (Palmqvist et al., 2020). What makes Alzheimer's so hard on families?

The Gates family's experience mirrors what millions of families face, and Gates's public accounts highlight several aspects of the disease that are particularly difficult:

The slow, uneven progression: Alzheimer's does not advance in a straight line. There are periods of relative clarity followed by deeper confusion. For caregivers, this unevenness creates painful cycles of hope and loss — and makes it difficult to know what to expect or how to plan.

Identity and relationship change: A person with advancing Alzheimer's may not recognize family members, may behave in unfamiliar ways, or may lose the conversational and relational capacities that defined who they were. For adult children watching a parent change in these ways, grief often begins long before physical death.

Caregiver burden: Most Alzheimer's care is provided by family members, typically over years. The demands are physical, emotional, and financial. A meta-analysis of more than 10,000 Alzheimer's caregivers found that about 34% had depression and about 44% had anxiety, higher than in the general population (Sallim et al., 2015). The Alzheimer's Association (alz.org) offers a free 24/7 helpline and local support groups for families in this situation.

Alzheimer's in his own family is one of the reasons Bill Gates shifted significant personal resources toward the disease — not to find a quick fix, but to build a scientific foundation that didn't yet exist.

What lessons does the Gates family's experience offer readers?

Gates has channeled his family's experience into funding research. The practical lessons that follow from his focus, and that the science supports, are worth summarizing:

Start earlier than you think you need to: Gates has consistently emphasized early detection as the highest-priority research goal. By the time a person shows Alzheimer's symptoms, the brain changes have typically been accumulating for many years. The earlier a diagnosis is made — ideally before symptoms appear — the more options a patient and family have. This applies to lifestyle habits as well: the brain-protective behaviors that matter most are those started years before any problems emerge.

Lifestyle factors matter: The 2020 Lancet Commission estimated that 12 modifiable risk factors account for around 40% of dementia cases worldwide (Livingston et al., 2020), and multiple large studies have found that regular aerobic exercise, a Mediterranean-style diet, quality sleep, avoiding tobacco, managing blood pressure and blood sugar, and staying socially and mentally active are associated with a meaningfully lower risk of Alzheimer's and cognitive decline. None of these guarantees prevention, but together they represent the best risk-reduction toolkit science currently offers. See our guide to memory-protective habits for specifics.

Nutrients can support the brain: Alongside diet and exercise, certain nutrients have meaningful research support for brain health — omega-3 DHA for brain structure and function, B-vitamins including B12 and folate for homocysteine metabolism, and compounds like citicoline and phosphatidylserine for supporting neuronal membrane function. These are not Alzheimer's treatments, but they support the underlying biology that a healthy brain needs. See our editors' picks for the best memory-support formulas of 2026.

Talk to your doctor about your family history: In a pooled analysis of case-control studies, having one first-degree relative with dementia was linked to about 2.6 times the risk of Alzheimer's (van Duijn et al., 1991). This doesn't mean the disease is inevitable, but it does mean the conversation about brain health, monitoring, and lifestyle optimization should happen sooner rather than later.

Frequently asked questions

Did Bill Gates Sr. have Alzheimer's disease?

Yes. William H. Gates II was publicly known to have Alzheimer's disease in his later years and passed away in September 2020 at age 94. His son Bill Gates first spoke about his father's diagnosis publicly in January 2018.

Can Alzheimer's disease be prevented?

There is no guaranteed prevention. However, research strongly supports that regular exercise, a healthy diet, quality sleep, managing cardiovascular risk, and staying mentally and socially active can meaningfully reduce risk over time. Talk to your doctor about your personal risk factors and what steps are most appropriate for you.
