Iron Deficiency in Women: Signs to Watch For and What to Eat

Fatigue, cold hands and odd cravings for ice can all trace back to low iron, and women are at higher risk than most people realize. Here's how to spot it early.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published October 31, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Fatigue, pale skin, cold hands and feet, and shortness of breath on mild exertion are the most common early signs of low iron in women.
  • Women need more iron than men, mainly because of monthly blood loss, and heavy periods are one of the most common causes of deficiency.
  • Low iron can show up before anemia does. Ferritin, the body's iron storage marker, can drop while hemoglobin still looks normal on a basic blood count.
  • Craving ice, dirt or other non-food items (pica) is an unusual but real sign that sometimes points to iron deficiency specifically.
  • A blood test is the only way to know for sure. Self-treating with high-dose iron without testing can mask other problems or, rarely, cause harm.

Iron deficiency in women shows up most often as fatigue, pale skin, cold hands and feet, trouble concentrating, and breathlessness during ordinary activity. Heavy periods, pregnancy and low dietary iron are the usual drivers. A simple blood test, specifically a ferritin level, confirms it well before a standard blood count would.

Iron deficiency is one of the most common nutrient shortfalls in women of reproductive age, and it's easy to miss because the early signs overlap with ordinary tiredness, stress and poor sleep. This guide walks through what to watch for, why women are more exposed to it, what the research supports for fixing it, and when it's worth asking a doctor for a test rather than guessing.

What are the early signs of iron deficiency in women?

Fatigue is usually the first thing women notice, and it's often described less as sleepiness and more as a heavy, low-energy feeling that doesn't lift with rest. Beyond that, a cluster of other signs tends to show up together rather than alone.

  • Unusual tiredness or weakness, even after a full night's sleep
  • Pale skin, pale gums, or pale inner eyelids
  • Cold hands and feet more often than people around you
  • Shortness of breath climbing stairs or during light exercise
  • Headaches, dizziness, or a fast heartbeat with minor exertion
  • Brittle nails, or nails that curve upward (spoon-shaped) in more advanced cases
  • Restless legs at night
  • An unusual craving to chew ice, or less commonly, dirt or paper (a pattern called pica)

That last sign sounds strange, but it's well documented. Researchers don't fully understand why iron deficiency triggers ice cravings specifically, though one theory is that chewing ice briefly increases alertness in a way that a low-iron brain seeks out. If you've found yourself reaching for ice regularly without really knowing why, it's worth mentioning at your next checkup.

How is iron deficiency different from anemia?

Iron deficiency and iron-deficiency anemia aren't the same stage of the same problem, even though people often use the words interchangeably. Iron deficiency means the body's iron stores, measured by a protein called ferritin, have dropped low. Anemia is a later stage, where iron has run so low that the body can't make enough healthy red blood cells, and hemoglobin itself drops.

That gap matters because a standard complete blood count can look completely normal while ferritin is already low. Plenty of women feel noticeably worse well before hemoglobin falls outside the normal range. If you've been told your blood count is "fine" but you still feel off, ask specifically whether ferritin was tested, because it often isn't included by default.

Reference ranges for ferritin vary somewhat between labs, and a result that technically falls within the printed "normal" range can still be low enough to cause symptoms in some women, particularly if it sits near the bottom of that range. This is a case where the number alone doesn't tell the whole story; a doctor weighing your ferritin result against your symptoms, diet and menstrual history will usually catch borderline cases that a strict cutoff would miss. Don't assume a single "normal" result closes the door if the symptoms above keep showing up.

Why are women more likely to run low on iron?

Men typically need about 8 mg of iron a day. Women ages 19 to 50 need roughly 18 mg, more than double, almost entirely because of monthly menstrual blood loss. A few situations raise the risk further:

Risk factorWhy it raises iron needs
Heavy or prolonged periodsBlood loss is the single biggest driver of iron deficiency in women of reproductive age
PregnancyBlood volume expands and the growing fetus draws on maternal iron stores
Vegetarian or vegan dietPlant iron (non-heme) is absorbed less efficiently than iron from meat
Frequent blood donationEach donation removes a measurable amount of stored iron
Endurance trainingFoot-strike impact and heavier sweating can modestly increase iron losses
Digestive conditions (celiac disease, IBD, prior gastric surgery)These can impair how much iron the gut actually absorbs

Perimenopause adds a twist. Periods often become heavier and less predictable in the years before menopause, which can push iron needs up right when many women assume their risk is dropping. See our guide to perimenopause symptoms and early signs if that overlap sounds familiar.

Pregnancy is its own category. Iron needs nearly double, from about 18 mg to 27 mg a day, because the body is building extra blood volume and supplying the placenta and growing fetus. Many prenatal vitamins already include iron for this reason, but some women still need a separate supplement on top, which is why iron is checked routinely at prenatal visits rather than left to guesswork. Untreated iron deficiency during pregnancy has been linked to preterm birth and low birth weight, which is part of why doctors take it seriously even when a mother feels mostly fine.

Can low iron cause brain fog or mood changes?

Yes, and this is one of the more under-recognized effects. Iron is needed to carry oxygen to tissue, including the brain, and it's also involved in making dopamine and other neurotransmitters. Women with low iron commonly report trouble concentrating, slower thinking, irritability and low mood alongside the physical symptoms. This connection is a genuine reason brain fog deserves a basic blood panel rather than being written off as stress, especially in women who also notice the physical signs above. It's one piece of a bigger picture, not a stand-alone diagnosis.

How much iron do women need, and which foods have the most?

Adult women ages 19 to 50 need about 18 mg of iron daily; that drops to 8 mg after menopause, once monthly blood loss stops, and rises to 27 mg during pregnancy. Iron comes in two forms: heme iron from animal sources, which the body absorbs efficiently, and non-heme iron from plants, which absorbs less well on its own.

FoodIron (roughly, per typical serving)
Beef liver (3 oz)~5 mg, among the richest sources

Pumpkin seeds (1 oz)~2.5 mg

Pairing plant iron with vitamin C, think lentils with bell peppers, or spinach with a squeeze of lemon, meaningfully improves absorption. Coffee and tea, on the other hand, contain compounds that can block iron absorption if drunk right alongside a meal; having them an hour before or after a meal sidesteps most of that effect.

Should women take an iron supplement?

That depends on whether a test actually shows low iron, not on symptoms alone, since fatigue has plenty of other causes. For women with confirmed deficiency, oral iron supplements (usually ferrous sulfate or a gentler form like iron bisglycinate) are the standard first step, often alongside dietary changes.

For mild, early deficiency caught on a routine test, some doctors will try a few months of diet changes plus a lower-dose supplement before reassessing. For more significant deficiency, or when anemia has already set in, a higher therapeutic dose is usually needed, and diet alone generally isn't enough to rebuild stores in a reasonable timeframe. In rare cases where oral iron isn't tolerated or absorbed well, such as after certain gastric surgeries or with inflammatory bowel disease, a doctor may recommend intravenous iron instead.

A few practical notes make supplementing more tolerable. Taking iron every other day, rather than daily, has been shown in some research to improve absorption and cut down on the stomach upset that makes many women quit early. Pairing a dose with a small amount of vitamin C, and taking it away from coffee, tea, calcium supplements and dairy, also helps.

What are the side effects of iron supplements?

Constipation, dark stools, nausea and stomach cramping are common, especially at higher doses. Dark stools alone aren't dangerous and are expected on iron. Starting at a lower dose and building up, or switching forms if one causes problems, often solves it. A pharmacist or doctor can help pick a form that's easier to tolerate.

Who should be careful with iron supplements?

People with hemochromatosis, a genetic condition that causes the body to absorb too much iron, should not take iron supplements without medical supervision, since iron overload can damage the liver and heart over time. Anyone with a known digestive condition, or taking other medications, should check with a doctor before starting, since iron can interact with certain antibiotics and thyroid medication.

Iron supplements are also a leading cause of accidental poisoning in young children, because the tablets can look like candy. Keep bottles out of reach and consider a child-resistant cap if there are kids in the house.

When should a woman get tested for iron deficiency?

It's worth asking for a ferritin test if you have several of the signs above, have heavy periods, follow a vegetarian or vegan diet, are pregnant or planning to be, or have a digestive condition that affects absorption. A doctor will typically order ferritin alongside a complete blood count and, depending on the results, may look into the cause of the blood loss itself rather than treating the iron level in isolation.

Don't start high-dose iron on your own before testing. If the real issue turns out to be something else, like a thyroid problem or vitamin B12 deficiency, which can cause a similar kind of fatigue, unnecessary iron supplementation delays finding that out and adds its own side effects on top. Ferritin, the body's iron reserve, can run low for months before a standard blood count flags anything unusual.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to correct iron deficiency?

Energy often improves within a few weeks of starting treatment, but it typically takes three to six months of consistent supplementation to fully rebuild iron stores (ferritin), even after symptoms ease.

Can you be iron deficient without being anemic?

Yes. Ferritin, the iron storage marker, can drop well before hemoglobin falls low enough to count as anemia. Many women feel symptoms at this earlier stage.

Does iron deficiency affect hair?

It can. Low iron is one of several causes of diffuse hair thinning or shedding, though it's rarely the only factor, so it's worth ruling out alongside thyroid and other causes.

Is it safe to take iron supplements during a period?

Yes, and for women with heavy periods it's often recommended specifically because that's when the most iron is lost. Timing doesn't need to avoid your period.

Can too much iron be harmful?

Yes, especially for people with hemochromatosis or anyone taking high doses without medical guidance. Iron overload can affect the liver and heart over years, which is why testing before supplementing matters.

Does birth control affect iron levels?

Hormonal birth control that reduces period flow, including some IUDs and combined pills, can actually lower iron loss and modestly help iron status in some women.

What blood test checks for iron deficiency?

A ferritin test is the most direct marker of iron stores. Doctors often pair it with a complete blood count, and sometimes with transferrin saturation, for a fuller picture.
