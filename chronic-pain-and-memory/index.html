Chronic Pain and Memory: Why It's Harder to Concentrate

The forgetfulness that comes with persistent pain isn't imagined, and it isn't a character flaw. Pain occupies the exact cognitive resources that forming memories requires.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published August 25, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Pain demands attention by design, and attention is the gateway to forming new memories.
  • Working memory takes the biggest hit, which is why people lose their thread mid-task.
  • Sleep disruption compounds it, since pain fragments the stages that consolidate memory.
  • Some pain medications add their own cognitive effects, particularly opioids and certain nerve pain drugs.
  • Treating the pain, the sleep and the mood together works better than targeting any one alone.

People living with chronic pain often describe a second problem that gets far less attention than the first. They can't hold a thought. They walk into rooms and forget why. Conversations slip away halfway through. Then they worry that something separate and worse is happening to their brain.

It's usually the pain. Understanding the mechanism helps, partly because it's reassuring and partly because it points at what actually helps.

Pain is an attention hijacker

Pain evolved to interrupt. Its entire purpose is to seize your attention and hold it until you deal with whatever caused it. That works well for acute injury. When pain becomes chronic and there's nothing left to fix, the interrupting continues anyway.

Attention is finite. Encoding a new memory requires attending to information long enough for the hippocampus to process it. If a meaningful share of your attentional capacity is consumed by pain signalling, less remains for everything else. The memory was never properly formed, so there's nothing to retrieve later.

This is why the complaint is usually about recent things rather than distant ones. Old memories, laid down before the pain started, are generally intact.

Working memory takes the worst of it

Working memory holds and manipulates information over seconds: the thread of a conversation, the steps of a recipe, a phone number between hearing and dialling. Research in chronic pain populations has consistently found working memory among the most affected domains, along with attention and processing speed.

The practical experience is losing your place constantly. Starting a sentence and not finishing it. Reading a paragraph three times. Our guide to what working memory is covers how the system operates when it isn't under this kind of load.

Sleep is the multiplier

Pain and sleep have a nasty circular relationship. Pain makes falling and staying asleep harder. Poor sleep lowers pain thresholds, so the following day hurts more. Round it goes.

Memory consolidation depends on deep and REM sleep, and pain fragments both. So there are two hits: less encoding during the day because attention is occupied, and less consolidation at night because sleep is broken. That combination explains a lot of the severity. Our piece on sleep and memory consolidation covers what those stages do when they run properly.

The medication question

Some pain treatments carry cognitive effects of their own. Opioids commonly cause sedation and impair attention and memory, particularly when starting or increasing a dose. Gabapentin and pregabalin, widely used for nerve pain, list cognitive effects including memory problems and confusion. Certain muscle relaxants and tricyclic antidepressants add anticholinergic burden, which affects the acetylcholine system memory relies on.

This creates a genuine bind. The medication may reduce pain enough to free up attention while also directly dulling cognition. The net effect varies between people and doses, and it's worth discussing rather than guessing.

Do not adjust or stop prescribed pain medication on your own. Several of these drugs cause withdrawal problems and some are dangerous to discontinue abruptly. Bring the concern to your prescriber, framed as a question about balance: is there an option with less cognitive effect at a similar level of pain control?

Mood belongs in this picture

Depression and anxiety are common alongside chronic pain, and both impair concentration and memory independently. Untangling how much of the fog comes from pain, how much from low mood, and how much from disrupted sleep isn't usually possible, and it isn't necessary. They interact, and treating any one of them tends to help the others.

That's worth saying plainly because people sometimes resist mental health treatment on the grounds that their pain is physical and real. Both things are true at once. The pain is real, and treating the mood component often reduces both the suffering and the fog.

What actually helps

Pacing is the first thing. Boom-and-bust cycles, where a good day gets overloaded and the next three are lost, wreck both pain and cognition. Steadier activity levels produce better outcomes on both fronts.

Offload memory onto external systems without embarrassment. Written lists, phone reminders, a single place where things live, one calendar for everything. This isn't giving up. It's freeing scarce attention for things that need it.

Do demanding cognitive work when pain is lowest. Most people have a predictable daily pattern, and matching hard tasks to the better hours makes a real difference.

Movement helps, within limits set by your condition. Graded exercise programmes have evidence in several chronic pain conditions, and exercise supports cognition through separate mechanisms. Our piece on exercise and brain health covers those. Start lower than feels necessary and build slowly.

Address sleep directly. Cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia works in people with chronic pain and doesn't add medication burden. It's usually the highest-value intervention available for this particular problem.

When to get it checked

Pain-related cognitive difficulty typically fluctuates with pain levels and fatigue. If your memory problems are steadily worsening regardless of pain, if you're getting lost in familiar places, or if others notice changes you don't, that pattern deserves separate assessment. Our guide to memory loss versus normal aging covers what distinguishes ordinary slips from signs worth investigating.

Also worth ruling out are the treatable causes that can coexist with pain: thyroid problems, B12 deficiency, sleep apnea and vitamin D deficiency. A basic blood panel answers most of those questions. Beyond that, the habits in our guide to improving your memory still apply, adapted to what your body allows.

The memory was never formed in the first place. Pain took the attention that encoding it would have required.

Frequently asked questions

Can chronic pain cause memory loss?

Chronic pain consumes attention, and attention is required to encode new memories. Research in chronic pain populations consistently finds working memory, attention and processing speed among the most affected domains. Disrupted sleep and the effects of some pain medications add to the problem.

Why can't I concentrate when I'm in pain?

Pain evolved to interrupt and seize attention until the cause is addressed. When pain becomes chronic, that interrupting continues with nothing left to resolve. The attentional capacity it consumes is no longer available for concentration, reading or holding a conversation thread.

Do pain medications affect memory?

Some do. Opioids commonly impair attention and memory, particularly when starting or increasing a dose, and gabapentin and pregabalin list cognitive effects. Certain muscle relaxants and tricyclic antidepressants add anticholinergic burden. Never adjust prescribed pain medication yourself, as some are dangerous to stop abruptly.

Does treating chronic pain improve memory?

Often, yes, though improvement usually comes from addressing pain, sleep and mood together rather than pain alone. Reducing pain frees attention, better sleep restores consolidation, and treating any accompanying depression or anxiety helps concentration independently.

When should memory problems with chronic pain be investigated?

If difficulties worsen steadily regardless of pain levels, if you get lost in familiar places, or if other people notice changes you do not, seek separate assessment. It is also worth ruling out thyroid problems, B12 deficiency and sleep apnea, which can coexist with chronic pain.
