Antidepressants and Memory: What to Know About Cognitive Side Effects

If your memory feels foggier since starting an antidepressant, you're not imagining it, and you're also not necessarily right about the cause. Here's what the evidence actually shows.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published August 27, 2026

Before anything else: don't stop or change an antidepressant on your own based on this article. Talk to the doctor or psychiatrist who prescribed it. Stopping abruptly can cause withdrawal effects and depression relapse.

Key takeaways

  • Depression itself impairs memory and concentration, a well-documented effect independent of any medication.
  • Some people report brain fog in the early weeks of starting an antidepressant, which often eases as the body adjusts.
  • Studies on SSRIs and memory in older adults are mixed, with most not finding a consistent, lasting decline.
  • One 2025 study in people who already had dementia linked certain SSRIs to faster cognitive decline, a narrower and more specific finding than "antidepressants cause memory loss."
  • Never stop a prescribed antidepressant on your own. Loop in your prescriber if you notice changes.

Short answer: it's complicated, and depression is usually a bigger part of the answer than people expect. Depression on its own causes real, measurable problems with concentration, word-finding and memory. Antidepressants can also cause cognitive side effects in some people, particularly early on, but the research doesn't support a blanket claim that these drugs damage memory long-term in the general population.

Here's how depression itself affects memory, what's actually known about the drugs, and what a newer, more specific study found.

Does depression itself affect memory?

Yes, and this gets underweighted in most conversations about antidepressants and memory. Depression is associated with measurable deficits in attention, concentration, verbal memory and executive function. The National Institute of Mental Health notes that memory and thinking problems, sometimes called pseudodementia, can be prominent in severe depression. Part of the mechanism is straightforward: depression makes it harder to concentrate while learning something new, so less gets encoded into memory in the first place. Chronic stress hormones also affect the hippocampus, the brain region most involved in forming new memories, which is covered in more depth in our guide to how stress affects memory.

Do antidepressants themselves cause memory loss?

For SSRIs, the most commonly prescribed class, a systematic review of studies in older adults found no consistent negative effect on memory functioning after treatment. Most studies in that review reported no measurable change, and a few even found modest improvement, likely because treating the depression itself removed one of the things impairing memory to begin with. That's not the whole picture, though. In long-term treatment studies, a meaningful share of patients, over one in five in one commonly cited study of long-term SSRI use, self-reported cognitive symptoms including fatigue, poor concentration and memory impairment. Self-reported symptoms and objectively measured memory performance don't always move together, which is part of why this research area stays unsettled.

Do different classes of antidepressants affect the brain differently?

They plausibly could, since SSRIs, SNRIs, tricyclics and other classes act on different neurotransmitter systems and have very different side-effect profiles overall. Older tricyclic antidepressants, for instance, have stronger anticholinergic effects, and drugs that block acetylcholine are more clearly linked to short-term memory and attention problems, a mechanism we cover in our piece on antihistamines and memory fog, since some older allergy medications work the same way. Newer SSRIs and SNRIs are generally considered to have a gentler cognitive side-effect profile, though "gentler" doesn't mean "none" for every individual.

What did the 2025 study on antidepressants and dementia actually find?

A Swedish registry study published in BMC Medicine in early 2025 looked specifically at people who already had a dementia diagnosis and were also taking antidepressants. Among roughly 18,700 patients, those on higher-than-average doses of certain SSRIs, escitalopram most notably, showed a somewhat faster rate of cognitive decline than those on lower doses or none. Mirtazapine, which works through a different mechanism, showed less of an association. The researchers were careful to note real limits: this was observational data, not a controlled trial, so it can't prove the drug caused the faster decline rather than reflecting more severe depression symptoms in that group. It's also a study of people who already had dementia, not a finding about antidepressants and memory in the general population. Experts quoted alongside the research were explicit that people with dementia should not stop their antidepressant without medical guidance.

Depression itself is a well-documented cause of memory complaints. That gets lost easily when the medication is the easier thing to blame.

Could stopping the antidepressant make memory worse instead of better?

Often, yes. Abruptly stopping many antidepressants can trigger discontinuation symptoms, including dizziness, irritability and trouble concentrating, on top of the risk that the underlying depression returns. A depression relapse brings its own well-documented memory and concentration problems, which can end up worse than whatever cognitive side effect prompted the question in the first place. If a medication change makes sense, it should be tapered and supervised, not stopped on a hunch.

What should you actually do if you notice memory changes?

Bring it to your prescriber and be specific about timing: when the symptom started relative to starting the drug, changing the dose, or switching medications. Ask about ruling out other common contributors, including thyroid function, sleep quality, alcohol use and any other new medication, since overlapping causes are common. If the antidepressant does turn out to be the likely culprit, there are usually options, including a dose adjustment or a switch to a different drug in the same or a different class. Frequently asked questions

Do antidepressants cause memory loss?

Some people notice fogginess or word-finding trouble, especially in the first weeks of treatment, but most controlled studies in adults without dementia do not find a consistent, lasting memory decline caused by antidepressants. Depression itself is a common, well-documented cause of memory complaints.

Is it the depression or the medication causing memory problems?

Often it's depression itself. Concentration and memory problems, sometimes called pseudodementia in severe cases, are a recognized symptom of depression independent of any medication. That said, some people do report cognitive side effects tied to a specific drug, which is why tracking timing with your prescriber matters.

Do all antidepressants affect memory the same way?

No. Different classes work differently in the brain, and reported cognitive side effects vary by drug and by person. Research in older adults on SSRIs specifically has generally not shown consistent memory harm, though studies in people who already have dementia have raised more concern about certain SSRIs.

Can quitting an antidepressant improve memory?

Never stop an antidepressant on your own to test this. Stopping abruptly can cause withdrawal symptoms and a relapse of depression, both of which can make concentration and memory worse, not better. Any change should go through your prescriber.

What should you do if you notice memory changes on an antidepressant?

Tell your prescriber, including when the change started relative to starting or adjusting the medication. They can check other common causes like sleep, thyroid function and other new medications, and adjust your treatment safely if the drug turns out to be the cause.
