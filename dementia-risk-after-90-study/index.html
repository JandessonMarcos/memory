Dementia Risk After 90: What a New Study Found

Reaching your 90s without dementia feels like clearing the finish line. A new long-running study says risk keeps shifting after that, and not evenly for everyone.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published September 5, 2026

Key takeaways

  • LifeAfter90, a joint UC Davis Health and Kaiser Permanente study, tracked adults 90 and older and published new findings in The Lancet Healthy Longevity.
  • Women in the study were diagnosed with dementia at roughly double the rate of men of the same age.
  • Black participants showed a notably higher dementia rate than Asian participants in the same cohort.
  • The APOE2 gene variant's protective effect appeared to continue past age 90, while APOE4, the classic Alzheimer's risk gene, was not clearly linked to higher risk in this specific group.
  • Some participants stayed cognitively healthy despite carrying known risk factors, which researchers see as a clue worth studying further.

Short version: a new analysis from the LifeAfter90 study finds that dementia risk in the oldest old, people who've already reached their 90s without a diagnosis, still varies a lot by sex, race and genetics. Women showed a notably higher rate than men. Black participants showed a higher rate than Asian participants. And the gene most strongly tied to Alzheimer's earlier in life, APOE4, didn't clearly raise risk in this age group the way it does at 60 or 70.

What is the LifeAfter90 study?

LifeAfter90 is an ongoing research effort out of UC Davis Health, run in partnership with Kaiser Permanente, that has followed a cohort of adults age 90 and older since 2018. Because so few large studies focus specifically on this age group, "the oldest old," it fills a real gap. Most dementia research is built around people in their 60s, 70s and 80s, which leaves open questions about how risk behaves once someone has already lived past 90 without cognitive decline. The latest results were published in The Lancet Healthy Longevity, drawing on several hundred participants with a median age around 92.

What did the study find about sex and race?

Two patterns stood out. Women in the cohort were diagnosed with dementia at roughly twice the rate of men of similar age, a gap researchers didn't fully explain, though longer average female lifespan and other biological or social factors may play a role. Black participants also showed a substantially higher dementia rate than Asian participants in the same study, consistent with disparities seen in younger age groups and in other large dementia studies over the years.

Neither finding is new territory for dementia research broadly. Sex and race-based differences in dementia risk have shown up in study after study across younger age brackets. What's notable here is that the gap didn't close, or even shrink much, once people had already reached their 90s. Extreme age didn't equalize the playing field.

What happened with the APOE gene?

This is the most surprising part. APOE comes in a few common variants, and APOE4 is the one most consistently linked to higher Alzheimer's risk in midlife and early old age. In this study of people who had already reached their 90s, carrying APOE4 was not associated with a significantly higher dementia rate. Meanwhile, the APOE2 variant, already known as somewhat protective, appeared to keep lowering risk even at this advanced age.

One plausible explanation researchers point to is a kind of survivorship pattern: people who carry APOE4 and are especially vulnerable to Alzheimer's may tend to develop it, or other health problems, before reaching 90 at all. That would leave a pool of 90-plus-year-olds who happen to carry APOE4 but were, for whatever reason, more resistant to its typical effects. That's a hypothesis worth more study, not a settled explanation.

What does this mean if you're already in your 80s or 90s?

It's genuinely encouraging that some participants stayed cognitively sharp well past 90 despite carrying recognized risk factors. Researchers view that group as a potential window into resilience, the traits or exposures that let some brains keep functioning well despite age and genetics working against them. That's an active area of ongoing research, not something with clear personal takeaways yet.

At the same time, this isn't a reason to assume dementia risk simply fades with age. New diagnoses kept appearing throughout the study period, and the sex and race gaps stayed wide. Family history, sex and background remain worth discussing with a doctor at any age, including the 90s. Reaching 90 without dementia is a real milestone. This study suggests it isn't the finish line some people assume, and who's most at risk after that point doesn't look the same for everyone.

What's the catch with a study like this?

As with most dementia research, this is observational: researchers followed people and recorded what happened rather than randomly assigning risk factors. That design can reveal real patterns, like the sex and race gaps, without proving exactly why they exist. The APOE4 finding in particular deserves a cautious read, since it's based on one cohort of people who had already survived to 90 without dementia, which is itself a specific and somewhat unusual group. Larger, independent replications will matter before this becomes a settled part of the science.

Frequently asked questions

What is the LifeAfter90 study?

LifeAfter90 is an ongoing research effort from UC Davis Health and Kaiser Permanente that has tracked adults 90 and older since 2018. Its latest dementia-risk findings were published in The Lancet Healthy Longevity.

Do women really have a higher dementia risk after 90 than men?

According to this study, yes. Women in their 90s were diagnosed with dementia at roughly twice the rate of men in the same age group, for reasons the researchers didn't fully explain.

Does the APOE4 gene still raise dementia risk after 90?

Not as clearly as earlier in life. In this study, carrying APOE4 was not associated with a significantly higher dementia rate among people who had already reached their 90s without dementia.

What should someone in their 80s or 90s take from this study?

Reaching very old age without dementia is encouraging but not a guarantee, since new cases still occurred. Risk isn't distributed evenly, so it's worth discussing family history, sex and background with a doctor.
