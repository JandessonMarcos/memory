Alzheimer's Brain Changes May Start 7 Years Before Scans Catch It

A long-running brain-imaging study suggests the disease process starts earlier than current scans can detect. Here's what the researchers actually found, in plain terms.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published September 6, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Researchers at the University of Oslo tracked healthy older adults with repeated MRI scans over nearly two decades.
  • Structural brain changes showed up on MRI at least seven years before amyloid plaques became detectable on PET scans in the same people.
  • This is described as the earliest brain-imaging signal linked to Alzheimer's found so far, published in Nature Neuroscience.
  • It's not a diagnostic test. The finding describes a group-level pattern, not something a doctor can use on an individual patient yet.
  • It reinforces that Alzheimer's builds gradually, which is one more reason lifestyle risk factors are worth addressing early, well before any symptoms.

A new brain-imaging study adds a wrinkle to how early Alzheimer's disease might actually begin. Researchers at the University of Oslo tracked healthy older adults with repeated MRI scans over nearly 20 years, then compared those scans against when amyloid plaques, the sticky protein clumps associated with Alzheimer's, first became visible on PET scans in the same people. The MRI scans showed measurable structural changes in the brain at least seven years before the plaques were detectable at all. The findings were published in Nature Neuroscience.

What did the researchers actually measure?

The study design is what makes this notable. Rather than comparing people with Alzheimer's to people without it at a single point in time, the researchers followed the same individuals for close to two decades, collecting MRI scans along the way. Once someone's PET scan showed amyloid plaques had crossed the detection threshold, the team went back and reexamined that person's own MRI scans from the prior ten years. That's what let them see structural brain changes that predated visible plaque buildup by at least seven years, rather than relying on comparisons across different people.

According to coverage of the findings, this represents the earliest brain-imaging signal associated with Alzheimer's identified to date. Structural changes visible on MRI, changes in the shape or thickness of certain brain regions, appear to precede the buildup that current PET scans are built to detect.

Does this mean the disease starts earlier than doctors thought?

That's the implication, and it fits a pattern that's been building in Alzheimer's research for years: the biological process behind the disease appears to unfold over a much longer timeline than the point where memory symptoms show up. Amyloid plaques themselves are already understood to build silently for years before diagnosis. This study pushes the earliest visible signal further back still, suggesting that by the time plaques are detectable on a scan, structural changes have already been underway for the better part of a decade.

It's worth being precise about what wasn't shown. The study looked at structural brain changes and the timing of amyloid detection, not memory or thinking symptoms directly. It doesn't establish that these early MRI changes cause dementia on their own, and it doesn't mean every person with similar-looking scans will go on to develop Alzheimer's.

Could this lead to earlier diagnosis?

Eventually, maybe, but not yet. Finding a group-level pattern across a research cohort is a very different thing from having a validated tool a doctor could use on an individual patient. The researchers would need to replicate this in other groups, work out how reliably the pattern predicts an individual's future amyloid status, and rule out other explanations for the structural changes they saw, before anything like this could inform a clinical test. That process typically takes years, and plenty of promising early-detection biomarkers over the past two decades haven't made it into routine practice.

What it does add, more immediately, is another data point supporting a shift already underway in Alzheimer's research: toward looking earlier and earlier, well before symptoms, rather than treating diagnosis as the starting line of the disease process.

The takeaway isn't a new test you can get tomorrow. It's a reminder that whatever drives Alzheimer's risk appears to be at work quietly for a very long time before anyone notices anything wrong.

What does this mean for you, practically?

For most people, nothing changes today. There's no new scan to request and no individual risk number this study can give you. What it reinforces is something the field has been saying for a while: if the disease process is already underway a decade or more before diagnosis, the modifiable risk factors, sleep, exercise, blood pressure, hearing, social connection, diet, are worth addressing in midlife, not after symptoms appear. If you're concerned about your own memory or a family member's, this kind of research doesn't replace a conversation with a doctor. It's one study, in one cohort, and like most brain-imaging research, it will need independent replication before its full implications are clear.
