About Memory Lab

Independent, plain-English guides on memory, focus and healthy aging — so you can tell what actually works from what's just marketing.

What we do

  • We translate cognitive-health research into clear, practical advice.
  • Every health claim is checked against published science.
  • We say plainly when evidence is weak, and we do not claim medical review we do not have.
  • We disclose when a link is an affiliate link — our rankings are never for sale.

Our editorial standards

Memory Lab exists because the brain-health space is full of hype. Our job is to cut through it. We prioritize ingredient transparency, clinically-studied dosing and real user feedback over marketing claims. When we recommend a product, it's because it earned the spot on the criteria we publish openly in each review.

We are reader-supported. Some links on our site are affiliate links, meaning we may earn a commission if you buy through them, at no extra cost to you. This never influences our rankings or conclusions.

Who writes Memory Lab

Memory Lab is written and edited by a small independent team, not by a credentialed medical panel. We think you should know that before you read anything else here, because plenty of sites in this space imply otherwise.

What that means in practice: our articles summarise published research and guidance from sources like the National Institute on Aging, the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, NCCIH and Harvard Health. They are not written by physicians and they are not reviewed by one before publication. We are careful, we cite where our claims come from, and we hedge when the evidence is weak. That is a different thing from clinical review, and we are not going to dress it up as one.

So use this site the way you would use a well-researched magazine article: as a starting point for questions to ask someone qualified. Nothing here is a diagnosis, and nothing here should delay a conversation with your doctor. We say that on every page and we mean it literally.

How we handle evidence

  • We name the limits. When a supplement ingredient has thin evidence, we say the evidence is thin, even when the ingredient is in a product we link to.
  • Ingredient evidence is not product evidence. A trial showing that a compound works at a given dose says nothing about whether a particular bottle contains that dose. We keep those separate.
  • No invented numbers. If a label does not disclose how much of an ingredient it contains, we report that it does not disclose it rather than guessing.
  • No fabricated reviews or ratings. Any editorial score is our own opinion against criteria we publish, not a survey of customers we never spoke to.
  • Corrections stay visible. When we get something wrong and someone tells us, we fix it and say what changed.

When to stop reading and see a doctor

Memory changes that you or the people around you have actually noticed are worth a proper assessment, and several common causes are treatable once identified. Thyroid problems, B12 deficiency, sleep apnoea, depression and a long list of ordinary medications all affect memory. None of them is fixed by a supplement, and time spent trying one is time not spent finding the real cause.
