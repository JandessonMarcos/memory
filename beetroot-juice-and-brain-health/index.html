Beetroot Juice and Brain Health: Can Nitrates Boost Blood Flow to the Brain?

Beetroot juice has a following among athletes for its dietary nitrate content. A smaller line of research has asked whether that same nitrate, by way of better blood flow, does anything measurable for the brain.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published October 9, 2026

Photo: Billjones94 / Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA 4.0

Key takeaways

  • Beets are one of the richest dietary sources of nitrate, which the body converts into nitric oxide, a molecule that relaxes blood vessels.
  • Small studies have found a modest, short-term increase in cerebral blood flow after drinking concentrated beetroot juice, mainly in older adults.
  • A few trials also reported small gains on specific cognitive tasks, but results are inconsistent across studies and sample sizes are small.
  • Most research uses concentrated beetroot juice shots, not the diluted juice blends sold in most grocery stores.
  • People on blood pressure medication, with low blood pressure, or with a history of kidney stones should check with a doctor first.
  • This is not a treatment for memory loss or dementia of any kind.

Short answer: beetroot juice's dietary nitrate does appear to widen blood vessels and modestly increase blood flow to parts of the brain for a few hours after drinking it, and a handful of small trials have found accompanying cognitive gains. The effect is real but narrow, and it's nowhere near a substitute for the basics that drive long-term brain health.

What's the actual mechanism here?

Beets, along with leafy greens like spinach and arugula, are unusually high in dietary nitrate. Once swallowed, some of that nitrate gets converted by bacteria on the tongue into nitrite, which the body then turns into nitric oxide. Nitric oxide is a signaling molecule that tells the smooth muscle lining blood vessels to relax, widening the vessel and easing blood flow. It's the same pathway behind why beetroot juice became popular with cyclists and runners looking for a performance edge.

The brain is a heavily vascularized organ that depends on a steady, generous blood supply, so researchers have reasonably asked whether that same vessel-widening effect reaches the brain too. Imaging studies using MRI have found that concentrated beetroot juice can increase blood flow to the frontal lobe within a few hours in older adults, a region tied to planning, attention and working memory.

What do the human studies on cognition actually show?

Beyond blood flow measurements, a smaller set of trials has looked at whether that translates into better performance on cognitive tests. Some studies in older adults found modest improvements on tasks involving reaction time or executive function after a dose of concentrated beetroot juice, compared with a nitrate-depleted placebo. Other trials found no meaningful difference. The research is still young, the samples are small, often fewer than 30 to 40 people, and the cognitive tests used vary enough between studies that it's hard to draw one firm conclusion yet.

What's reasonably well established, by contrast, is the blood flow effect itself and beetroot juice's ability to lower blood pressure slightly in many people. Whether a short-term rise in cerebral blood flow reliably turns into sharper thinking, especially with repeated daily use over months, is the part that still needs more evidence.

How much beetroot juice would you actually need?

Studies that found a blood-flow or cognitive effect generally used concentrated beetroot juice shots, formulated to deliver a measured dose of nitrate, often taken daily for a stretch of one to several weeks. That's quite different from the sweetened beet-blend juices sold at most grocery stores, which are typically diluted with apple or other fruit juice and contain far less concentrated nitrate per serving.

If you want to eat your way toward similar nitrate intake, whole beets, along with spinach, arugula and other leafy greens, are a reasonable option, and fit naturally into a produce-forward way of eating like the one covered in our Mediterranean diet and memory guide. The blood flow angle also pairs naturally with movement: regular aerobic activity is still the better-studied way to support circulation to the brain, something we cover in more depth in exercise and brain health.

A short-term bump in blood flow after a beetroot shot is a real, measurable effect. Whether it adds up to lasting cognitive benefit over months is the question research hasn't settled yet.

Who should be cautious with beetroot juice?

Beets are high in oxalates, so people with a history of calcium oxalate kidney stones are often advised to limit concentrated beet products. Because beetroot juice can lower blood pressure, people already taking blood pressure medication, nitrate-based medications for heart conditions, or those prone to low blood pressure should talk to a doctor before using concentrated beetroot juice regularly, since the combined effect could drop blood pressure further than intended. It's also worth knowing that beets can turn urine or stool pink or red in some people, a harmless but startling effect called beeturia.

Frequently asked questions

Does beetroot juice really improve blood flow to the brain?

Small studies have found that the dietary nitrates in beetroot juice can modestly increase blood flow to parts of the brain, particularly the frontal lobe, within a few hours of drinking it. Some of these studies also found small improvements on certain cognitive tasks, mostly in older adults, though results are mixed and sample sizes are small.

How does beetroot juice affect the body?

Beets are naturally high in dietary nitrate, which the body converts to nitrite and then nitric oxide, a molecule that relaxes and widens blood vessels. This is the same pathway that explains why beetroot juice is popular among endurance athletes for improving exercise performance.

How much beetroot juice do you need to drink?

Research studies have generally used concentrated beetroot juice shots providing a measured dose of dietary nitrate, often taken daily for one to several weeks. There's no official recommended amount for cognitive benefit, and commercial juice varies widely in concentration.

Who should avoid beetroot juice?

People with a history of kidney stones, low blood pressure, or those taking nitrate-based or blood pressure medication should talk to a doctor before adding concentrated beetroot juice to their routine, since the nitrate content can amplify blood-pressure-lowering effects.
