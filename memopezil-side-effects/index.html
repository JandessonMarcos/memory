Memopezil Side Effects: What to Watch For and Who Should Skip It

Most people tolerate these ingredients. The exceptions are specific enough to name, and two of them involve prescription medication.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published August 31, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Stomach upset is the most common complaint, and it comes from bacopa. Taking the capsules with food usually resolves it.
  • Two ingredients can disturb sleep. Rhodiola and ginseng are both stimulating, so evening dosing tends to backfire.
  • Ginseng is the interaction risk. It affects blood sugar and interacts with warfarin.
  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding are a hard no for this whole category. The safety data doesn't exist.
  • None of this is a substitute for asking a pharmacist if you take prescription medication. It's free and takes five minutes.

Memopezil contains five ingredients: branched-chain amino acids, bacopa monnieri, rhodiola rosea, L-theanine and panax ginseng. None of them is unusual, and for most healthy adults the side effect profile is mild. That's the honest headline.

But mild is not the same as nothing, and three of the five carry cautions that are specific enough to be worth reading properly rather than skimming. Two of those cautions involve prescription drugs. This page goes through each one.

One caveat about all of it. Side effect reporting for supplements is far weaker than for medicines. There's no mandatory trial programme and no systematic collection of adverse events, so what follows comes from studies of the individual ingredients rather than from surveillance of this product. Treat it as the best available picture, not a complete one.

The common one: stomach upset from bacopa

If something goes wrong in the first week, this is usually it. Bacopa monnieri irritates the digestive tract in a meaningful minority of people. Cramping, nausea, loose stools, and a general sense that your stomach isn't happy.

It shows up consistently enough in the clinical literature that trial protocols routinely instruct participants to dose with a meal. That single change fixes it for most people. If you've been taking the capsules first thing on an empty stomach and feeling queasy by mid-morning, try moving them to breakfast before concluding you can't tolerate the product.

If it persists past a couple of weeks of taking it with food, stop. Persistent GI symptoms aren't something to push through, and they're also worth mentioning to a doctor because plenty of other things cause them.

The sleep problem: rhodiola and ginseng

Both rhodiola and panax ginseng are stimulating. Not in the way caffeine is, but enough that timing matters.

People who take either late in the day report difficulty falling asleep, lighter sleep, and vivid dreams. Ginseng-related insomnia is well documented. Rhodiola is the more commonly reported of the two in practice, partly because it's often taken for fatigue, which makes an afternoon dose feel logical right up until bedtime.

This matters more than it sounds. Sleep is where memory consolidation happens. A supplement that improves your alertness during the day while quietly costing you an hour of sleep each night is running a net loss on the exact thing you bought it for. Morning dosing solves it.

Ginseng and your prescriptions

This is the section to read twice if you take medication regularly.

Panax ginseng has two documented interactions that matter. The first is with warfarin. Case reports and pharmacological work suggest ginseng can reduce warfarin's anticoagulant effect, which is exactly the sort of change you don't want happening quietly. Anyone on warfarin should not start this without talking to the clinician who manages their anticoagulation.

The second is blood sugar. Ginseng can lower it. On its own that's not dangerous for most people, but stacked on top of metformin, a sulfonylurea or insulin, it's an additive effect nobody planned for. If you're managing diabetes, this is a conversation with your doctor before you start, not a reason to panic afterwards.

Ginseng may also affect blood pressure, and reports go in both directions. If you take antihypertensives, mention it.

The quieter interactions

  • Bacopa and thyroid medication. Animal work suggests bacopa can raise thyroid hormone levels. The human evidence is thin, but if you take levothyroxine it's worth flagging.
  • Bacopa and anticholinergic drugs. Bacopa acts on the cholinergic system. If you're already taking something that blocks it, such as an older antihistamine or certain bladder medications, the two are working against each other.
  • Rhodiola and antidepressants. Rhodiola has effects on monoamine systems. Combining it with an SSRI or MAOI is not something to do without medical advice.
  • L-theanine and blood pressure medication. Theanine can lower blood pressure modestly. Usually irrelevant, occasionally additive.
  • Anything with a sedative. Theanine's calming effect is mild, but mild effects stack.

None of these are common, and most people taking a memory supplement will experience none of them. The point isn't to alarm you. It's that a pharmacist can check all five ingredients against your actual medication list in about five minutes, and that's a better use of your time than reading forum posts.

Who should not take this

  • Anyone pregnant, trying to conceive, or breastfeeding. Bacopa, rhodiola and ginseng all lack safety data in pregnancy. The answer here is simply no.
  • Children and teenagers. This category is formulated and studied in adults.
  • Anyone on warfarin or another anticoagulant, without clearing it first.
  • Anyone managing diabetes with medication, without clearing it first.
  • Anyone with a diagnosed bipolar disorder. Stimulating adaptogens are a poor fit and rhodiola has been flagged specifically.
  • Anyone scheduled for surgery. Stop supplements around two weeks beforehand and tell the anaesthetist. Ginseng's effects on bleeding and blood sugar are the reason.
  • Anyone whose memory has changed noticeably and recently. That needs a diagnosis, not a supplement.

That last one is the most important line on this page. A supplement is a reasonable thing to try for ordinary age-related fuzziness. It is not a reasonable response to a real change in someone's memory, and delaying a proper assessment is the actual harm. Several common causes are reversible once found, which is precisely why finding them matters.

What the first two weeks usually look like

Knowing the ordinary pattern makes it easier to tell a real problem from an adjustment.

Days one to three are where stomach complaints appear if they're going to. That's bacopa, and it's the single most likely thing you'll notice. Some people also report a mild flatness or heaviness in the first few days, which tends to settle.

Week one is also when sleep problems show up, if the timing is wrong. The tell is specific: you fall asleep later than usual but don't feel tired earlier in the day. That points at rhodiola or ginseng rather than at anything else, and moving the dose earlier is the test.

By week two most people have either settled into it or found a reason to stop. What you should not be experiencing at that point is ongoing nausea, headaches, a racing heart, or feeling wired and anxious. Those aren't part of the normal adjustment and they're a reason to stop rather than wait it out.

Why supplement side effects are harder to pin down

There's a structural reason the answers on this page come with more hedging than you'd get about a prescription drug, and it's worth understanding because it changes how you should read any supplement safety claim, including ours.

Medicines go through trials designed partly to catch harm, and once approved they sit inside a reporting system that collects adverse events at scale. Supplements do neither. In the US they're regulated as food rather than as drugs, which means a product can reach the market without the manufacturer demonstrating either efficacy or safety in advance.

The practical consequence is that nobody holds a complete list of what this or any supplement does to people. What exists is research on the individual ingredients, mostly in small studies, plus voluntary adverse event reports that are known to be heavily underused. So when a page tells you a supplement has no side effects, what that usually means is that nobody systematically looked.

The quality problem behind some reactions

A share of bad experiences in this category come not from the listed ingredients but from what else ended up in the capsule.

Rhodiola is the clearest example among these five. It's one of the more commonly adulterated botanicals on the market. Independent analyses have repeatedly found products labelled rhodiola rosea containing cheaper related species, or containing so little of the marker compounds that the plant material is questionable. If you react badly to a rhodiola product, the honest answer is that you may not know what you reacted to.

This is where third-party testing earns its cost. A USP or NSF mark doesn't tell you a product works. It tells you that what's on the label is what's in the bottle, at roughly the stated amount, without contaminants above threshold. For a category with documented adulteration, that's a meaningful thing to pay for.

It also explains why side effect experiences vary so widely between brands using nominally identical ingredients. Two bacopa products can behave differently because they are, materially, different products.

What to do if you react badly

There's no drama to this. Stop taking it. Supplement side effects almost always resolve within a few days of stopping, because none of these ingredients accumulate the way a fat-soluble vitamin does.

If you want to work out which of the five caused it, wait a week, then reintroduce nothing and just see whether the symptom stays gone. Trying to isolate a single ingredient from a five-ingredient capsule isn't possible without buying them separately, which most people won't bother with, and honestly don't need to.

Report anything serious. The FDA runs a MedWatch programme for supplement adverse events, and it's underused, which is part of why the safety picture in this category stays vague. Tell your doctor too, especially if you take other medication.

Stacking it with what you already take

Most people who buy a memory supplement are already taking something else. That's where avoidable problems tend to come from, and it's rarely the memory product on its own.

The overlap to watch for is other botanicals. Ginkgo biloba is the common one, and it affects bleeding risk. Put ginkgo alongside ginseng and you have two ingredients nudging the same system, which matters if you're also on aspirin or a prescribed anticoagulant. Ashwagandha is another frequent pairing, and stacking it with rhodiola gives you two adaptogens at once with no clear reason to expect a bigger benefit.

Caffeine deserves its own mention. Rhodiola and ginseng are both mildly stimulating, and if your baseline is already three coffees, adding them can tip you into jitteriness you'd otherwise blame on the supplement. Theanine pulls in the other direction, which muddies the picture further. If you're going to test this product properly, hold your caffeine intake steady while you do.

The simple rule: write down everything you take, including the things you don't think of as medication, and show that list to a pharmacist. Fish oil, ginkgo, St John's wort and high-dose vitamin E all belong on it. St John's wort in particular interacts with a long list of prescriptions and is worth flagging on its own.

Read as a whole, the profile is unremarkable for this category. The realistic worst case for a healthy adult is an upset stomach and a bad night's sleep, both fixable by changing when you take it. The genuine risks sit with a specific group: people on warfarin, people on diabetes medication, and people who are pregnant. If you're in one of those groups, the answer is a conversation, not a purchase.

The question worth asking isn't whether an ingredient is natural. It's whether it does something in your body that your medication is also doing.

Frequently asked questions

What are the side effects of Memopezil?

The most commonly reported issue with these ingredients is stomach upset, which comes from bacopa monnieri and usually resolves when the capsules are taken with food. Rhodiola and panax ginseng can be stimulating and may disturb sleep if taken later in the day. L-theanine and BCAAs are generally well tolerated at supplement doses.

Can I take Memopezil with my prescription medication?

Ask a pharmacist before you start. Panax ginseng has documented interactions with warfarin and can lower blood sugar, which matters if you take diabetes medication. Rhodiola has effects on monoamine systems and shouldn't be combined with antidepressants without medical advice. A pharmacist can check all five ingredients against your list in a few minutes.

Does Memopezil cause insomnia?

It can, depending on when you take it. Rhodiola rosea and panax ginseng are both stimulating, and taking them in the afternoon or evening is the usual cause of sleep trouble. Moving the dose to the morning generally fixes it. This is worth getting right, because sleep is when memory consolidation happens.

Who should not take Memopezil?

Anyone pregnant, trying to conceive or breastfeeding; children and teenagers; anyone on warfarin or another anticoagulant; anyone taking diabetes medication; anyone with bipolar disorder; and anyone scheduled for surgery within two weeks. Anyone experiencing a recent, noticeable change in memory should see a doctor rather than start a supplement.

How long do side effects last after stopping?

Usually a few days. None of these five ingredients accumulate in the body the way fat-soluble vitamins do, so effects tend to fade quickly once you stop. If a symptom persists after stopping, that's a reason to see a doctor, because something else is likely causing it.

Is stomach upset from bacopa dangerous?

It isn't dangerous, but it is common enough that clinical trials routinely instruct participants to take bacopa with a meal. Cramping, nausea and loose stools are the usual complaints. If taking it with food doesn't resolve it within a couple of weeks, stop and mention it to a doctor.
