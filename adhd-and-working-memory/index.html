ADHD and Working Memory: Why Focus and Recall Struggle Together

Losing the thread of a task, forgetting an instruction seconds after hearing it, rereading the same line. Here's why working memory and ADHD are so tightly linked, and what actually helps.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published August 2, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Working memory is one of the executive functions most often affected by ADHD, alongside attention regulation and impulse control.
  • It's not the same as long-term memory. The problem is usually holding and using information for a few seconds, not storing it permanently.
  • This explains a lot of everyday friction: losing track mid-task, forgetting instructions, or rereading the same sentence.
  • Working memory "brain training" shows mixed, modest results in research and doesn't always transfer to daily life.
  • Practical strategies that reduce cognitive load tend to help more reliably than drills alone.

If you or your child has ADHD, the forgetfulness that comes with it can feel confusing. It rarely looks like a classic memory problem. Instructions vanish seconds after they're given. A train of thought disappears mid-sentence. A multi-step task falls apart by step three. The common thread in a lot of this isn't long-term memory at all. It's working memory, the mental workspace that holds information active just long enough to use it, and it's one of the areas ADHD research points to most consistently.

Does ADHD actually affect working memory?

For many people with ADHD, yes. Working memory sits under the umbrella of executive function, the brain's management system for attention, planning and self-control, and studies comparing people with and without ADHD have repeatedly found lower performance on working memory tasks in the ADHD group, on average. This shows up in both children and adults, though the specific tasks and severity vary a lot between individuals. It's also worth saying plainly that not everyone with ADHD has significant working memory difficulty. It's a common pattern, not a universal rule.

What is working memory, exactly?

Working memory is different from the kind of memory that stores a childhood birthday or a phone number you've dialed for years. It's the short-term mental scratchpad you use to hold a new phone number just long enough to type it in, follow multi-step directions, or keep a math problem's earlier steps in mind while working on the next one. Our guide to what working memory is and why it matters covers the mechanics in more depth. When that scratchpad is smaller or less stable than average, tasks that rely on juggling several pieces of information at once become disproportionately hard, even when overall intelligence and long-term memory are completely normal.

Why does ADHD cause so much everyday forgetfulness?

A lot of it comes down to encoding rather than storage. To remember something well, your brain first has to attend to it, hold it in working memory, and process it enough that it gets filed away properly. ADHD often disrupts that first step: attention drifts before the information is ever fully registered. So it's not that the memory got lost. In many cases it was never encoded solidly to begin with, which looks and feels exactly like forgetting from the outside.

Distraction compounds this. If your attention shifts to something else while you're mid-task, whatever was held in working memory tends to drop out, the way an open tab closes and loses its unsaved content. That's why ADHD forgetfulness often clusters around interruptions: a phone buzzing while you're carrying a thought across the room, a new conversation starting before you've finished the last one.

Can working memory actually be improved?

This is genuinely debated. Structured working memory training programs, often computer-based, do produce gains on the specific tasks practiced, and some studies show modest improvement on closely related measures. Where the evidence gets thinner is transfer: whether those gains carry over into real-world functioning like schoolwork, job performance or daily organization. Reviews of the research have generally found that transfer effects are small and inconsistent, so a program that promises to "fix" ADHD memory through training alone should be treated with healthy skepticism.

What tends to help more reliably is reducing how much working memory a task demands in the first place, plus, for many people, treatment that addresses core ADHD symptoms directly. Medication and behavioral therapy aimed at attention and impulse control can improve working memory performance as a downstream effect, even though they weren't designed as memory treatments specifically.

What practical strategies help day to day?

Externalizing information is the single biggest lever: write it down, say it out loud, or put it somewhere visible the moment it enters your head, rather than trusting it to stay in working memory. Sticky notes, phone reminders, and a single trusted to-do list all do the same job, taking the burden off a system that's already working at capacity. Breaking tasks into smaller steps helps too, since each step demands less to hold in mind than the whole project at once.

Reducing background noise and interruptions protects whatever is currently loaded in working memory from getting bumped out. A quieter workspace, one task open at a time, and batching notifications instead of letting them arrive continuously all measurably reduce the kind of mid-task memory drops that are so common with ADHD. Should you talk to a doctor about it?

If working memory struggles are new, worsening, or showing up without a childhood history of attention problems, it's worth a conversation with a doctor rather than assuming it's ADHD by default. Sleep problems, anxiety, thyroid issues and other conditions can produce a similar pattern of forgetfulness and distractibility. A proper evaluation, ideally by a clinician experienced with adult or childhood ADHD, can sort out what's actually driving the symptoms and point toward treatment that fits.

ADHD-related forgetfulness is mostly a working memory and attention story, not a broken long-term memory. Reducing what you ask your working memory to hold helps more than trying to train it into holding more.

Frequently asked questions

Does ADHD affect working memory?

Yes, for many people. Working memory, the ability to hold and use information over a few seconds, is one of the executive functions most consistently found to be weaker in ADHD research, alongside attention regulation and impulse control.

Why does ADHD cause forgetfulness?

Much of it traces back to working memory and attention rather than long-term memory itself. If information doesn't get held and processed long enough to be encoded properly in the first place, it's harder to recall later, even though the underlying memory system is intact.

Can working memory be improved in ADHD?

Working memory training programs show modest, task-specific gains in research, and results don't always transfer well to everyday life. Practical strategies, like externalizing information and reducing cognitive load, tend to help daily functioning more reliably than drills alone.

What helps ADHD-related memory problems day to day?

Writing things down immediately, breaking tasks into small steps, using timers and visual reminders, and reducing background distractions all lighten the load on working memory. Many people also benefit from treatment aimed at core ADHD symptoms, which can improve memory as a side effect.
