Blood Pril Side Effects: The Interactions That Actually Matter

For most healthy adults the risk is a sore stomach. For a few groups it's a blood pressure drop or a heart history that changes the answer completely.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published October 30, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Stomach upset is the common one. Nausea, bloating, cramps or loose stools are the usual complaints with arginine and citrulline, mostly at doses far above Blood Pril's.
  • The serious risk is stacking. Combined with blood pressure drugs, nitrates or PDE5 drugs such as sildenafil and tadalafil, nitric-oxide amino acids can push pressure too low.
  • Heart attack history is a stop sign. The VINTAGE MI trial of L-arginine after heart attack was halted early after more deaths in the arginine group.
  • The label names its own exclusions. Pregnant or nursing women, anyone under 18 and anyone with a medical condition should ask a doctor first.
  • Never adjust a prescription yourself. If readings change on Blood Pril, the next step is a call to your doctor.

Blood Pril side effects are usually mild: some people get nausea, bloating or loose stools from its arginine and citrulline. The real risk is interaction. Taken with blood pressure medication, nitrates or erectile dysfunction drugs, it can lower blood pressure too far, and people who've had a heart attack should avoid L-arginine unless a cardiologist says otherwise.

What are the most common Blood Pril side effects?

Each capsule contains a 500 mg proprietary blend of L-arginine HCl, L-arginine alpha-ketoglutarate and L-citrulline malate. The label directions are one or two capsules a day, so you take 0.5 to 1 gram of amino acids in total.

At that level, side effects in healthy adults tend to be uncommon. When they do happen, they're digestive:

  • Nausea or stomach discomfort, especially on an empty stomach.
  • Bloating or gas.
  • Loose stools or diarrhea, which arginine is known for at multi-gram doses.
  • Mild headache, sometimes reported with products that widen blood vessels.

Most of these settle down within a week or go away when you take the capsule with food. Citrulline tends to be easier on the gut than arginine, because less of it is broken down in the intestine. If stomach trouble continues past the first couple of weeks, stop.

Why the small dose works in your favor here

The same thing that limits Blood Pril's effect also limits its side effects. Digestive trouble with arginine shows up mainly at several grams in one go, and the label tops out at one gram a day across all three amino acids. That's roughly what you'd get from a modest serving of chicken, fish or pumpkin seeds. Food doesn't cause these problems for most people, and a capsule with a similar amount usually doesn't either.

Can Blood Pril make your blood pressure too low?

In theory, yes, and that's the side effect worth watching. Arginine and citrulline are raw material for nitric oxide, which relaxes blood vessel walls. In someone whose pressure is already normal or low, or who's taking something else that lowers it, adding more relaxation can tip readings below a comfortable range.

Low blood pressure doesn't always cause symptoms, but when it does, they're recognizable:

  • Dizziness or lightheadedness, especially when you stand up quickly
  • Blurred vision or a feeling that you might faint
  • Unusual tiredness or weakness
  • Nausea or a cold, clammy feeling

For older adults this is more than discomfort. A dizzy spell on the way to the bathroom at night is how many falls happen. If you notice any of this after starting Blood Pril, stop taking it, sit or lie down when symptoms come on, and call your doctor. A home cuff helps you see whether it's really your pressure; the how to tell if it works section has the measuring routine.

Is Blood Pril safe with blood pressure medication?

Only your doctor can say for you. All the main classes of blood pressure medicine can add to the effect of a nitric-oxide supplement: ACE inhibitors such as lisinopril, angiotensin receptor blockers such as losartan, calcium channel blockers such as amlodipine, beta blockers, and diuretics. The combination isn't automatically dangerous, but the effects can stack, and the more drugs you're on, the bigger the chance.

Three rules apply to anyone in this group:

  • Don't stop or reduce your medication because you've started Blood Pril, even if your readings improve. Medication changes go through your doctor.
  • Tell your doctor and pharmacist that you're taking it, so it's on your medication list.
  • Watch your home readings for the first few weeks and share them at your next visit.

A pharmacist is an underused resource here. They can check your full list for interactions in a few minutes, usually at no cost. Bring every bottle you take, supplements included, rather than trying to remember names. It's also worth timing: if your blood pressure pill already makes you a little lightheaded in the morning, taking a vessel-relaxing supplement at the same moment is the worst combination.

Why are nitrates and ED drugs the biggest red flag?

Nitrates, such as nitroglycerin tablets or spray and isosorbide mononitrate or dinitrate, are prescribed for chest pain. They work by releasing nitric oxide directly. PDE5 inhibitors, such as sildenafil, tadalafil and vardenafil, are used for erectile dysfunction and some lung conditions, and they make the nitric-oxide signal last longer.

Taking nitrates together with PDE5 drugs is a well-known, serious interaction that can cause a dangerous drop in blood pressure. Adding arginine and citrulline, which feed the same pathway, puts a third player in the mix. Even at Blood Pril's modest dose, there's no reason to take that chance without medical advice.

A few other medicines relax blood vessels too and deserve the same question to your doctor. Alpha blockers used for an enlarged prostate, such as tamsulosin and doxazosin, are a common example, and they already cause dizziness on standing in some men. Adding anything that pushes in the same direction raises the odds of a dizzy spell.

If you carry nitroglycerin for chest pain or use an ED medicine, talk to your doctor before you open the bottle. And if erection problems are part of why you're looking at circulation supplements, see a doctor about them. They can be an early warning of heart disease or diabetes.

Should you take Blood Pril after a heart attack?

Not without a cardiologist's approval, and many will say no. The reason is a trial called VINTAGE MI, published in 2006. About 150 people who had recently had a heart attack took either L-arginine at several grams a day or a placebo for six months. The arginine didn't improve artery stiffness or heart function. Six people died in the arginine group and none in the placebo group, and the trial was stopped early.

A small trial can't prove that arginine caused those deaths, and Blood Pril's dose is lower than the one tested. But the result was serious enough that medical references now advise people who have had a heart attack not to take L-arginine. The same caution makes sense for anyone with heart failure, unstable chest pain or other active heart disease. For general heart topics, see our heart health section.

Is it safe to take Blood Pril long term?

Arginine and citrulline aren't foreign compounds. Both are amino acids your body makes and handles every day, and both come from ordinary food. In studies, healthy adults have taken several grams a day for weeks to a few months without serious problems beyond the digestive effects above.

What's missing is data measured in years, and data on this exact blend. Nobody has followed people taking Blood Pril for a long time, and because the label is a proprietary blend, nobody outside the company knows the exact split between the three amino acids. The total is capped, though: 1 gram a day at most.

A sensible approach for long-term use is to treat it like any other daily product. Review it with your doctor once a year, alongside your other medicines. Recheck whether you still need it if your health or prescriptions change. And stop if a new diagnosis puts you in one of the groups in the table below.

Can Blood Pril affect blood sugar?

Possibly a little. Arginine plays a role in insulin release, and some medical references note that L-arginine might lower blood sugar. At Blood Pril's dose the effect is likely small, but if you take insulin or other diabetes medicines, check with your doctor and watch your glucose readings for the first couple of weeks. How can you lower the risk if you decide to take it?

If your doctor is fine with it, a careful start makes side effects easier to spot and easier to blame on the right thing.

  • Start with one capsule a day for the first week or two, with food and a full glass of water.
  • Keep a simple log of home blood pressure readings and anything unusual, like dizziness or stomach trouble.
  • Don't stack it with other nitric-oxide products, beet supplements or pre-workout formulas that contain arginine or citrulline.
  • Stay within the label, which says not to exceed two capsules a day.
  • Stand up slowly for the first few weeks, especially in the morning or after sitting for a long time.

When should you stop Blood Pril and get help?

Stop taking it and call your doctor if you have repeated dizziness or near-fainting, home readings that drop well below your usual range, or stomach problems that don't settle. Get emergency help, by calling 911, for fainting, chest pain, trouble breathing, or swelling of the face, lips or throat, which can signal an allergic reaction.

How to report a side effect

If you have a reaction you think came from Blood Pril or any supplement, tell your doctor first. You can also report it to the FDA through its MedWatch program, which collects problems with dietary supplements, and contact the distributor using the customer-service details printed on the label. Keep the bottle so you can give the lot number. Reports like these are how safety problems with supplements come to light.

Most people who try a product like this will have none of these problems. The point of knowing them is simple: if something happens, you'll recognize it, connect it to the right cause and act quickly.

The ingredient isn't the hazard. The combination is. Most Blood Pril side effects come from what it's taken with.

Frequently asked questions

Does Blood Pril cause headaches?

It can in some people. Products that widen blood vessels sometimes cause a mild headache in the first days. If it's persistent or severe, stop taking it and talk to your doctor.

Can Blood Pril cause diarrhea?

Loose stools are a known effect of arginine, mostly at multi-gram doses. At one or two capsules a day it's less likely, and taking the capsule with food usually helps. Stop if it continues past two weeks.

Is Blood Pril safe for the kidneys?

There's no specific evidence of harm at this dose in people with healthy kidneys. The kidneys convert citrulline to arginine and clear amino acid waste, so anyone with kidney disease should ask their doctor first.

Can I drink coffee or alcohol with Blood Pril?

Blood Pril contains no caffeine, so coffee doesn't add a stimulant. Alcohol can lower blood pressure and cause dizziness, so be cautious combining it with any vessel-relaxing supplement, especially in the first weeks.

Can women take Blood Pril?

Yes, adult women can, with the same cautions as men. The label says pregnant or nursing women should consult a physician first.

What should I do if I took Blood Pril with my ED medication?

If you feel fine, don't take more of either and call your doctor or pharmacist for advice. If you feel faint, dizzy or have chest pain, lie down and call 911.

Does Blood Pril interact with blood thinners?

Arginine isn't known as a major blood thinner interaction, but anyone on warfarin or similar drugs should review every supplement with their doctor or pharmacist, since many cause unexpected problems in combination.
