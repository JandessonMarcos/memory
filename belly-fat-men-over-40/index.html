Belly Fat in Men Over 40: Why It Builds Up and What Helps

The same diet and workouts that kept your waist in check at 30 can stop working at 42. Here's what actually changes, and what to do about it.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published November 10, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Hormones shift gradually, not overnight. Slowly declining testosterone after 40 favors fat storage around the midsection over the hips and limbs.
  • Visceral fat, the kind packed around organs, matters more than the look of the belly itself. It's linked to higher health risk than fat stored elsewhere.
  • Muscle loss quietly lowers daily calorie burn, so the same eating habits that worked at 30 can add up differently at 45.
  • Sleep and stress both push belly fat storage up through cortisol and appetite hormones, independent of diet.
  • Diet change plus resistance training beats either alone for reducing visceral fat specifically.

Belly fat builds up in men after 40 mainly from a combination of slowly declining testosterone, gradual muscle loss, and a metabolism that burns fewer calories at rest than it used to. Add less sleep and more stress, both common in midlife, and the body tends to store more fat specifically around the abdomen rather than elsewhere.

If your waistband fits differently than it did five years ago despite eating roughly the same way, you're not imagining it. Something has changed, and it usually isn't a single cause. It's a handful of small shifts that add up: hormones, muscle, sleep and stress all nudging fat storage toward the midsection at once.

Why does belly fat increase in men after 40?

Testosterone declines gradually starting around this age, typically by about 1% per year, and lower testosterone is associated with more fat storage and less lean muscle. At the same time, resting metabolic rate drifts down as muscle mass shrinks, since muscle burns more calories at rest than fat does. Neither change is dramatic on its own in any given year. Stacked over a decade, they shift the body's balance toward storing more fat, and a disproportionate share of it tends to land around the abdomen.

Is it just testosterone, or is more going on?

More is going on. Testosterone is one input among several, not the whole explanation. Sleep quality, stress levels, daily activity, and diet composition all interact with those hormonal shifts. Two 45-year-old men with similar testosterone levels can have very different waistlines depending on how much they sleep, how active their jobs are, and how they eat. That's actually useful news, because it means several of the contributing factors are things you can change even if the hormonal drift itself is mostly out of your hands.

What's the difference between subcutaneous and visceral fat?

Subcutaneous fat sits just under the skin, the kind you can pinch. Visceral fat is packed deeper, around the liver, intestines and other organs, and it doesn't show up as obviously even in someone who looks only moderately overweight. Both increase with age and weight gain, but they behave differently metabolically, and that difference is a big part of why doctors care about belly fat specifically rather than just overall weight.

Why is visceral fat riskier than fat elsewhere?

Visceral fat is more metabolically active than fat stored under the skin, meaning it releases more inflammatory compounds and hormone-like substances into the bloodstream. That activity is linked in research to higher risk for heart disease, insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes, independent of total body weight. Two men who weigh the same can carry very different health risk depending on how much of that weight sits as visceral fat around the midsection versus elsewhere on the body. This is also why waist circumference, not just weight or BMI, is a measurement doctors pay attention to.

Does muscle loss make belly fat worse?

It contributes, yes. Adults can lose a meaningful share of muscle mass per decade starting around age 30 if they aren't doing resistance training, and less muscle means fewer calories burned at rest. Eat the same way you did at 30 while carrying noticeably less muscle at 45, and the leftover calories tend to get stored as fat, often disproportionately around the abdomen in men. This is one of the clearest links between strength training and midsection fat: building muscle back raises the calorie baseline your body burns just existing.

What role does sleep play in belly fat?

A bigger one than most men expect. Short, poor-quality sleep raises cortisol and disrupts hunger hormones like ghrelin and leptin, which tends to increase appetite and calorie intake the next day. Multiple studies link chronically short sleep, under six hours a night, with greater visceral fat accumulation over time, separate from diet alone. If weekday sleep regularly falls short, that's worth addressing before, or alongside, any diet change.

Can diet alone fix it, or do you need exercise too?

Diet does most of the work for overall fat loss, since a calorie deficit is required no matter how much you exercise. But exercise, especially resistance training, changes where that fat loss comes from and helps preserve the muscle that keeps metabolism from slipping further. Diet alone, without any strength training, tends to reduce both fat and muscle together. Diet paired with resistance training preserves more muscle while still losing fat, which is the better outcome for long-term metabolic health, not just appearance.

On the food side, the details matter less than consistency. A modest calorie deficit, built around protein at every meal, produce, and fewer ultra-processed snacks, outperforms any strict or trendy plan that's too hard to stick with past a few weeks. Men who track their food loosely for even a week or two, just to see where the calories are actually coming from, often find a few easy cuts they hadn't noticed, like sugary drinks or oversized portions at dinner. That kind of awareness tends to matter more long term than any specific diet label.

How much exercise actually helps reduce belly fat in men?

Research generally points to a combination: resistance training two to three times a week to preserve and build muscle, plus 150 or more minutes a week of moderate cardio, like brisk walking or cycling, for overall calorie burn and heart health. Some studies suggest aerobic exercise has a slight edge for reducing visceral fat specifically, while resistance training has the edge for preserving muscle and raising resting metabolism. Doing both covers each angle, and neither one alone outperforms the pair by much.

Belly fat after 40 isn't really about one failing. It's hormones, muscle, sleep and stress all leaning the same direction at once, which is also why no single fix solves it.

What's a realistic first step?

Pick one lever, not five. Adding two strength sessions a week, cutting liquid calories, or moving your bedtime up by 30 minutes each tends to stick better than overhauling diet, sleep and training all at once. Give any single change three or four weeks before judging it, since early results on the scale are noisy and visceral fat changes often show up before the number does. Waist circumference, measured at the navel in the morning before eating, is a more useful tracking tool than the bathroom scale for this specific kind of fat.

When should belly fat be a medical concern?

Talk to a doctor if your waist circumference is above 40 inches, if belly fat has increased quickly without a clear reason, or if it comes with other symptoms like fatigue, high blood pressure readings, or unusual thirst, which can point to blood sugar issues worth checking. Frequently asked questions

Can you spot-reduce belly fat with sit-ups or ab exercises?

No. Ab exercises strengthen the muscles under the fat but don't selectively burn fat from that area. Losing visceral and subcutaneous belly fat requires an overall calorie deficit, which reduces fat across the body, the midsection included.

Does low testosterone cause belly fat on its own?

It's a contributor, not the sole cause. Lower testosterone is linked to more fat storage and less muscle, but diet, activity level, sleep and genetics all play a role too. Fixing testosterone alone rarely resolves belly fat without changes to those other factors.

How long does it take to lose visceral fat?

Visceral fat tends to respond faster to diet and exercise changes than subcutaneous fat does. Some studies show measurable reductions in visceral fat within 8 to 12 weeks of consistent calorie deficit and exercise, even before much change shows up on a scale or waist tape.

Is intermittent fasting better than regular calorie reduction for belly fat?

Research generally shows similar fat loss between intermittent fasting and standard calorie restriction when total calories match. Fasting may help some men stick to a lower calorie intake simply by narrowing the eating window, but it isn't inherently more effective at targeting belly fat specifically.

What waist size is considered a health risk for men?

A waist circumference above 40 inches (102 cm) in men is generally considered a marker of increased health risk, independent of overall body weight or BMI.

Do belly fat burner supplements actually work?

Most show little to no meaningful effect on belly fat specifically. Ingredients like caffeine or green tea extract can produce a small, temporary increase in calorie burn, but nothing in a bottle replicates the effect of a calorie deficit, resistance training and better sleep.

Is stress really linked to belly fat?

Yes. Chronically elevated cortisol, the hormone released under stress, is linked in research to greater visceral fat storage specifically, separate from its effect on appetite and eating habits.
