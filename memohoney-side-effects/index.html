MemoHoney Side Effects and Who Should Be Careful

Most healthy adults tolerate this capsule without trouble. The exceptions are specific, and almost all of them involve a prescription.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published October 23, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Most people notice nothing. The formula is stimulant-free and the printed doses are conservative.
  • Ginkgo is the main interaction. It can add to the bleeding risk of warfarin, aspirin, clopidogrel and similar drugs.
  • The nitric-oxide group widens blood vessels. That's worth a word with your doctor if you take blood pressure pills, nitrates or PDE5 drugs like sildenafil.
  • Niacin flush is unlikely. 7.5 mg is a vitamin-level amount, far below the doses that cause it.
  • Skip it if you're pregnant, breastfeeding or within two weeks of surgery. Those are no-trial zones.

MemoHoney side effects are usually mild or absent. The most likely issues are a little stomach discomfort in the first week and, rarely, a niacin flush. The real cautions are interactions: ginkgo with blood thinners, and the arginine and citrulline ingredients with blood pressure medication, nitrates and PDE5 inhibitors.

That's the summary. The rest of this page explains why each caution exists, how seriously to take it, and who should simply skip the product. For the formula itself, see our MemoHoney review, which prints the full label.

One honest caveat first. Supplements aren't tracked the way medicines are. There's no trial on the finished MemoHoney formula and no systematic adverse-event collection, so everything below comes from what's known about the individual ingredients. It's the best available picture. It isn't a complete one.

Why might MemoHoney upset your stomach?

If anything shows up in the first week, this is the likeliest candidate. Bacopa is known for it. Trials of the herb routinely report nausea, cramping or loose stools in a minority of participants, which is why many protocols tell people to take it with a meal. Amino acids taken on an empty stomach can do something similar in sensitive people.

MemoHoney's bacopa amount isn't disclosed and is almost certainly small, so this should be milder than with a full-dose bacopa product. Still, the fix is the same. Take the capsule with breakfast or dinner instead of on an empty stomach, and give it a few days.

If discomfort continues past two weeks with food, stop. Ongoing digestive symptoms aren't something to push through for a memory supplement, and they're worth mentioning to a doctor because plenty of other things cause them.

Is ginkgo in MemoHoney a bleeding risk?

This is the caution to read twice. Ginkgo biloba can reduce how readily platelets clump together. On its own, in a healthy person, that rarely matters. Stacked on a drug that already thins the blood, it can add up.

The drugs in question include warfarin, apixaban, rivaroxaban, clopidogrel, and daily aspirin. Case reports describe bleeding events in people combining ginkgo with these, and the National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health flags the interaction. The evidence from controlled studies is mixed, but mixed evidence plus a serious outcome is exactly when you ask first.

MemoHoney doesn't disclose how much ginkgo it contains, and the capsule arithmetic suggests it's a modest amount. That's reassuring but not a pass. You can't size a risk you can't measure, and your prescriber is the one who can weigh it against your own clotting history.

The same logic applies to high-dose fish oil, vitamin E and other supplements with a mild blood-thinning effect. One may be fine. Several together are a different picture. Our ginkgo biloba guide covers the herb in more detail.

Can you take MemoHoney with blood pressure medication?

Ask your doctor first. Four of the seven dosed ingredients (L-arginine, arginine AKG and two forms of citrulline) work by raising nitric oxide, which relaxes blood vessels. That's the entire premise of the formula. Blood pressure drugs often aim at a similar end result.

At 600 mg combined, the nitric-oxide group is far below the gram doses used in circulation research, so a meaningful drop in pressure is unlikely. Unlikely isn't zero, though, and people on several pressure medicines are the ones most sensitive to small extra changes. If you take an ACE inhibitor, a calcium channel blocker, a diuretic or similar, mention the product at your next appointment or call the pharmacy.

A home cuff makes this easy to watch. Take a reading before you start and a few during the first two weeks, at the same time of day. If you see a clear, repeated drop or feel dizzy on standing, stop and tell your doctor.

Nitrates and PDE5 inhibitors: a firmer line

Nitroglycerin, isosorbide and other nitrates release nitric oxide directly. Sildenafil, tadalafil and related PDE5 drugs extend what nitric oxide does. These two classes are already dangerous together, which is why the warning on erectile dysfunction drugs is so blunt. Adding more nitric-oxide precursors on top isn't something to try without medical sign-off, even at small amounts.

Will the niacin in MemoHoney make me flush?

Probably not. Niacin flush is that warm, red, sometimes itchy feeling across the face, neck and chest that comes on 15 to 30 minutes after a dose. It's harmless but unpleasant, and it's strongly dose-dependent.

The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements sets the adult upper limit for supplemental niacin at 35 mg a day, mostly because of flushing. MemoHoney has 7.5 mg. If you already take a high-dose B-complex or a separate niacin product, add those numbers together before worrying. If you're on prescription niacin for cholesterol, tell the prescriber about anything else containing B3.

Who needs extra care: allergies, pregnancy and surgery?

Three groups sit outside the medication question but still need a pause before starting. None of them is about the product being harsh. Each is about a situation where even a mild effect carries more weight than usual.

Can people with a bee or pollen allergy take MemoHoney?

Honey is on the panel, without an amount, as a trace ingredient. Most people with hay fever eat honey without trouble. But honey can contain small amounts of pollen and bee proteins, and people with a known allergy to bee products, propolis or royal jelly occasionally react to it.

If that's you, the tiny amount lowers the odds without ruling them out. Ask your allergist, or skip it. For everyone else, the honey is irrelevant from a safety point of view: it's not enough sugar to register, and it isn't a concern for blood glucose.

Is MemoHoney safe during pregnancy or breastfeeding?

There's no safety data for ginkgo, bacopa or this combination in pregnancy or while breastfeeding. Ginkgo's effect on bleeding adds a specific concern around delivery. The straightforward answer is not to take it during that time. That isn't a judgment on the product; it applies to nearly every memory supplement.

Should you stop MemoHoney before surgery?

Yes. The usual advice for ginkgo is to stop about two weeks before a scheduled operation, including dental surgery, and to tell the surgical team what you've been taking. Bleeding risk is the reason, and vessel-relaxing ingredients are worth mentioning to the anesthesia team too. Restart only when your surgeon says it's fine.

Who should skip MemoHoney?

  • Anyone on blood thinners or antiplatelet drugs (warfarin, DOACs, clopidogrel, daily aspirin) unless the prescriber has cleared it.
  • Anyone taking nitrates or PDE5 inhibitors. Don't combine without medical advice.
  • Anyone on blood pressure medication who hasn't checked with a doctor or pharmacist.
  • People with a bleeding disorder or a history of unusual bleeding.
  • Anyone pregnant or breastfeeding.
  • Anyone with surgery in the next two weeks.
  • People allergic to bee products, unless an allergist says otherwise.
  • Children and teens. The category is formulated for adults.
  • Anyone whose memory changed suddenly or keeps getting worse. That needs a diagnosis first.

The last one matters most. Ordinary age-related fuzziness is a reasonable thing to try a supplement for. A real, recent change isn't, and several causes are treatable once they're found. Our guide to memory loss versus normal aging explains where the line sits.

What should you ask your pharmacist before starting?

You don't need an appointment for this. Bring the bottle, or a photo of the label, and a list of everything you take, including the things you don't think of as medicine. Then ask three questions.

  • Does anything I take affect bleeding? Blood thinners, antiplatelets, NSAIDs like ibuprofen taken daily, fish oil, vitamin E and turmeric all belong in that conversation.
  • Does anything I take widen blood vessels or lower blood pressure? That covers pressure pills, nitrates, PDE5 drugs and some prostate medications such as tamsulosin.
  • Am I already taking niacin, ginkgo or arginine somewhere else? Multivitamins, B-complexes and workout powders often include them.

That's a five-minute check, and it's the single most useful thing you can do before trying any supplement that touches circulation. If you take a statin, mention that too; it isn't a known interaction here, but it's a useful prompt for a wider medication review, and some people notice memory complaints on statins that are worth discussing. What should you do if you react badly?

Stop taking it. Most supplement reactions fade within a few days because none of these ingredients lingers long in the body. If bruising, bleeding gums, black stools or a severe headache appear, call your doctor that day. Swelling of the lips, tongue or throat, or trouble breathing, is an emergency.

Report anything serious to the FDA's MedWatch program. It's underused, which is part of why supplement safety data stays thin. Then use the guarantee: MemoHoney's 60-day refund works by email request, and you return all bottles at your own shipping cost. Keep the packaging so that's easy.

Most memory supplements with a stimulating herb such as rhodiola or ginseng carry sleep complaints that MemoHoney avoids, since it has neither. Its specific risks sit in a different place: circulation and clotting. That's a narrower group of people, but the ones in it should take the caution seriously.

Frequently asked questions

Does MemoHoney cause insomnia?

It's unlikely. The formula has no caffeine or stimulating herbs such as rhodiola or ginseng, so it doesn't carry the sleep complaints common with many memory supplements. You can take it in the morning or evening.

Can MemoHoney raise blood pressure?

Its nitric-oxide ingredients work in the opposite direction, relaxing blood vessels. At these small amounts a meaningful change either way is unlikely, but people on blood pressure medication should check with their doctor before starting.

Is MemoHoney safe for people with diabetes?

The honey is a trace amount and won't affect blood sugar. None of the disclosed ingredients is known to lower glucose meaningfully. If you take diabetes medication, it is still sensible to run any new supplement past your doctor or pharmacist.

Can I take MemoHoney with an antidepressant?

No direct interaction is well documented for this formula, but ginkgo combined with SSRIs may slightly raise bleeding risk in some people. Ask the prescriber, especially if you also take aspirin or another blood thinner.

How long do MemoHoney side effects last after stopping?

Usually a few days at most. The amino acids and niacin clear quickly, and nothing in the formula builds up over weeks. A symptom that persists after stopping is a reason to see a doctor, since something else is likely causing it.

Does MemoHoney interact with alcohol?

There's no specific interaction listed for this formula. Alcohol also widens blood vessels and affects clotting, so heavy drinking alongside ginkgo and nitric-oxide ingredients is best avoided.

Is MemoHoney safe for older adults?

It is aimed at adults noticing age-related changes, and the doses are conservative. Older adults are also more likely to take blood thinners or blood pressure drugs, which is where the cautions on this page apply.
