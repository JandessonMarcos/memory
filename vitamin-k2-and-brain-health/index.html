Vitamin K2 and Brain Health: An Overlooked Nutrient's Role in Cognition

Vitamin K2 built its reputation on arteries and bones, not the brain. A newer, smaller line of research is asking whether the same nutrient matters for cognitive aging too.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published November 14, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Vitamin K2 (menaquinones) is chemically distinct from the K1 found in leafy greens, and is used differently by the body.
  • K2's best-supported mechanism involves activating a protein that blocks calcium buildup in artery walls, not a direct brain pathway.
  • Healthy arteries matter for the brain too, since blood flow is what keeps brain tissue supplied, but no trial has directly tested K2 for cognition.
  • Clinical trials on K2 and vascular calcification are genuinely mixed, some positive, some finding no benefit over placebo.
  • Natto, egg yolks and certain cheeses are the main food sources, and they aren't foods most Western diets include much of.

Vitamin K2 is best known for activating a protein that keeps calcium out of artery walls and directs it toward bone instead. Healthy, flexible arteries matter for the brain because blood flow feeds it, which is the real basis for the "vitamin K2 and brain health" conversation. No clinical trial has tested vitamin K2 directly against a cognitive outcome, so the brain connection remains indirect and unproven, not disproven.

Here's how K2 differs from the more familiar K1, what the vascular research actually shows, and where the cognition angle is genuinely still a hypothesis.

What is vitamin K2, and how is it different from vitamin K1?

Vitamin K isn't one single molecule; it's a family of related compounds split into two main groups. K1, or phylloquinone, is the form found in leafy greens like spinach and kale, and it's the form the liver uses most for blood clotting. K2, the menaquinones, comes mainly from fermented foods and animal products, circulates in the blood longer than K1, and appears to be used more by tissues outside the liver, including blood vessels and bone. Both forms activate the same family of vitamin K-dependent proteins; they just reach different tissues with different efficiency.

How does vitamin K2 affect blood vessels?

The mechanism here is genuinely well established at the cellular level. A protein called matrix Gla protein, or MGP, blocks calcium from depositing in artery walls, but only once vitamin K has activated it through a process called carboxylation. Without enough vitamin K, MGP stays inactive and calcium buildup in arteries can proceed unchecked. Observational studies have linked higher vitamin K intake, K1 and K2 both, to less arterial calcification, and animal studies confirm the mechanism directly.

Does the clinical trial evidence back this up?

Evidence typeWhat it shows
Cell and mechanism studiesStrong: vitamin K activates MGP, which directly blocks arterial calcium deposits
Observational studiesSupportive: higher dietary vitamin K intake is linked to less arterial calcification
Randomized trials (systematic review of 9 trials)Mixed: one major review found no consistent effect on calcification, atherosclerosis or arterial stiffness
Other trial reviewsMixed: some report reduced arterial stiffness and calcification scores with high-dose supplementation

That disagreement between reviews is the honest state of the science: a believable mechanism, encouraging observational data, and clinical trials that don't all agree with each other. Nobody has yet run the trial that would settle whether supplementing K2 changes a hard outcome like stroke or dementia risk. Trial length may be part of the disagreement too; arterial calcification builds up over years to decades, and most K2 trials run one to three years, which may simply be too short a window to reliably detect a slow-moving process changing direction.

Why would arterial health matter for the brain specifically?

The brain is unusually dependent on steady, well-regulated blood flow, and stiff or calcified arteries are a recognized risk factor for vascular contributions to cognitive decline. That's the logical bridge between "vitamin K2 supports artery health" and "vitamin K2 might matter for brain aging." It's a reasonable hypothesis built on real vascular biology, but it's still a bridge, not a finding. No study has directly measured whether taking vitamin K2 changes cognitive outcomes in people.

Is there any direct brain research on vitamin K at all?

Separately from the K2-vascular story, researchers have found vitamin K-dependent proteins and vitamin K itself in brain tissue, involved in processes like sphingolipid metabolism, a component of the fatty sheath that insulates nerve fibers. This is early, mechanistic research, not a cognitive trial, and it's a different research thread than the MGP-calcification story covered here. Our separate article on vitamin K and brain health covers that angle in more depth.

What foods provide vitamin K2, and how much do you need?

Natto, a fermented soybean dish common in Japan, is by far the richest source, with amounts that dwarf most other foods. Egg yolks, certain aged and fermented cheeses, and some animal fats contain smaller amounts. There's no separate official intake recommendation for K2 alone; the general adult vitamin K guideline of 90 to 120 micrograms a day covers K1 and K2 combined, and most people get the bulk of that from K1-rich leafy greens rather than K2 foods, which Western diets tend to include less of.

Population studies out of the Netherlands, where K2-rich fermented foods and certain cheeses are a bigger part of the diet, are part of what first drew researchers to K2's cardiovascular effects, as a higher-K2 diet there correlated with less arterial calcification than in populations eating mostly K1. That geographic pattern is suggestive, not proof, since diets differ in many other ways too, but it's part of the backdrop behind the interest in K2 specifically rather than vitamin K in general.

Vitamin K2's brain story right now is a plausible hypothesis resting on solid vascular biology, not a finding with its own clinical trial behind it. That distinction matters more than most supplement marketing lets on.

Should vitamin K2 be taken together with vitamin D?

There's a real, mechanistic reason the two get paired in supplement marketing. Vitamin D increases the body's production of certain vitamin K-dependent proteins, including matrix Gla protein and osteocalcin, essentially making more of the raw material that vitamin K then has to activate. Taking vitamin D without enough vitamin K could theoretically leave more of that protein inactive, though this interaction hasn't been tested directly as a cognitive or even a cardiovascular outcome in a dedicated clinical trial. It's a plausible reason to not think of the two nutrients in isolation, not a proven reason to buy a combination product specifically.

Are there side effects or people who should avoid vitamin K2?

At typical dietary or supplemental doses, vitamin K hasn't been shown to cause harm in healthy adults. The major exception is anyone taking warfarin or a similar vitamin K antagonist, where consistent vitamin K intake, in either form, is essential to keeping the medication's effect stable; starting or stopping a K2 supplement without medical guidance can throw off blood-clotting control.

Who might reasonably consider a vitamin K2 supplement?

People focused on bone and cardiovascular health, where the evidence base is more developed, have a more defensible reason to look at K2 than someone specifically chasing a cognitive effect. Anyone drawn to it for brain aging should treat it as a long-shot, mechanism-based bet alongside better-studied habits, not a replacement for them, and should loop in a doctor first if they take any blood thinner.

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between vitamin K1 and vitamin K2?

K1 (phylloquinone) comes mainly from leafy greens and is used heavily by the liver for blood clotting. K2 (menaquinones) comes from fermented and animal foods, stays active in the bloodstream longer, and is used more by blood vessels and bone.

Does vitamin K2 directly improve memory?

There's no direct clinical trial showing vitamin K2 improves memory. The brain-related interest is mostly about vascular health and emerging basic science, not a proven cognitive effect.

Is MK-7 better than MK-4 for vitamin K2 supplements?

MK-7 stays in the bloodstream longer and is usually dosed once daily in smaller amounts; MK-4 clears faster and is typically dosed higher, multiple times a day. Neither has been shown to be clearly superior for a brain-specific outcome.

Does vitamin K2 interact with blood thinners?

Yes, significantly. Anyone taking warfarin needs consistent vitamin K intake, K1 or K2, and should not start or stop a vitamin K2 supplement without their doctor's involvement.

Can you get vitamin K2 from a vegetarian or vegan diet?

It's harder but possible. Natto, a fermented soybean food, is the richest widely available plant-based source. Most other food sources of K2 are animal-based, like egg yolks and certain cheeses.

How much vitamin K2 do studies typically use?

Vascular and bone trials have generally used 90 to 360 micrograms a day of MK-7, or higher daily amounts of MK-4. There's no officially established separate intake recommendation for K2 specifically; the general vitamin K guideline covers both forms combined.

Is vitamin K2 safe to take daily?

At studied doses, yes, for most healthy adults not on blood-thinning medication. Vitamin K hasn't been shown to cause harm at typical dietary or supplemental amounts in that group.
