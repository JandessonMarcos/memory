Why Am I Always Tired? Everyday Causes Worth Checking

Feeling drained isn't always about how many hours you slept. Sleep quality, nutrient levels, and a few overlooked habits often explain it better than the clock does.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published October 21, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Constant tiredness is usually explained by one of a short list of common causes: poor sleep quality, low iron or B12, thyroid issues, blood sugar swings, dehydration, or ongoing stress.
  • Sleep quality matters as much as sleep duration. Eight hours broken up by frequent waking can leave you just as tired as five solid hours.
  • Several causes show up on routine blood work, including iron, B12, and thyroid hormone levels, which is why a checkup beats guessing.
  • Afternoon energy dips are common and often diet-related, tied to how and when you eat rather than anything more serious.
  • See a doctor if fatigue is severe, lasts more than a few weeks, or comes with other symptoms like unexplained weight change, shortness of breath, or low mood.

Constant tiredness usually comes down to a short list of common, checkable causes: poor sleep quality, low iron or B12, an underactive thyroid, blood sugar swings, dehydration, or ongoing stress. Most of these show up on routine blood work or improve with a few targeted changes, which is why it's worth narrowing down rather than just pushing through.

It helps to separate two different complaints that both get called "tired." One is sleepiness: you could doze off right now if you let yourself. The other is fatigue: you're drained of energy and motivation even if you're not sleepy in the moment. They overlap, but they don't always share the same cause, and that distinction matters for figuring out what's going on.

Tiredness is one of the most common complaints people bring to a doctor, and in the large majority of cases it traces back to something ordinary and fixable rather than a serious hidden illness. That doesn't make it any less exhausting to live with day to day, which is why it's worth working through the usual suspects in order instead of guessing.

How much sleep should actually fix tiredness?

Most adults need 7 to 9 hours a night, according to sleep researchers, and getting less than that on a regular basis is the single most common reason people feel worn down. It sounds obvious, but sleep debt builds up quietly. A string of 6-hour nights doesn't feel as bad as one all-nighter, yet the research shows the cumulative effect on alertness and mood can be just as real.

If you're consistently getting 7 to 9 hours and still feel exhausted, the number itself probably isn't the issue. That's when it's worth looking at sleep quality, timing, and the other causes below instead of simply trying to sleep more.

Trying to "catch up" with a long weekend lie-in doesn't fully undo a week of short nights, though it does help somewhat. A more reliable fix is a consistent bedtime and wake time across the whole week, weekends included, since an irregular schedule confuses the body's internal clock on top of any raw sleep debt.

Could poor sleep quality be the problem even if you're in bed long enough?

Yes, and this is one of the most overlooked causes of daytime fatigue. Waking up briefly many times a night, even without remembering it, fragments the deep and REM sleep stages that do most of the restorative work. Sleep apnea is a common, under-diagnosed cause of exactly this pattern: loud snoring, gasping, or pauses in breathing that a bed partner notices more often than the sleeper does.

Alcohol before bed, a room that's too warm, an inconsistent bedtime, and late caffeine all fragment sleep without necessarily shortening it on paper. If you want a clearer sense of what a normal night's sleep should look like, our piece on how long it should take to fall asleep covers the related question of sleep onset.

A sleep tracker, or even a simple written log for a week or two noting bedtime, wake time, and how rested you felt, often reveals a pattern faster than trying to remember it after the fact. It's not a medical diagnosis, but it's a useful starting point to bring to a doctor's visit if the pattern doesn't improve on its own.

Can low iron or vitamin B12 cause constant tiredness?

Both are classic, well-documented causes of fatigue, and both are easy to miss because the tiredness builds gradually. Iron deficiency anemia reduces how much oxygen your blood can carry to tissues, which shows up as exhaustion, pale skin, and sometimes shortness of breath on exertion. It's more common in people who menstruate heavily, pregnant people, and anyone eating little iron-rich food.

Vitamin B12 deficiency works differently but produces a similar drained feeling, since B12 is needed to make red blood cells and support nerve function. It's more common in older adults, people on certain medications like metformin, and anyone eating a largely plant-based diet without a B12 source or supplement. Both are caught with a simple blood test, which is more reliable than guessing from symptoms. Our iron deficiency article covers how low iron affects focus as well as energy.

Does thyroid function affect energy levels?

Substantially. The thyroid gland sets the pace for your metabolism, and an underactive thyroid, called hypothyroidism, is one of the more common medical causes of persistent fatigue, especially in women and in people over 60. Along with tiredness, it often comes with weight gain, feeling cold, dry skin, and constipation.

Thyroid problems are diagnosed with a blood test (TSH, and sometimes additional thyroid hormone levels), not guesswork, and treatment is usually straightforward once it's identified. If fatigue has crept in gradually alongside any of those other signs, mentioning it to a doctor is worth more than trying another supplement first. Our thyroid and brain fog piece covers the cognitive side of the same issue.

An overactive thyroid can disrupt energy too, though it tends to produce a different pattern: restlessness, a racing heart, and trouble sleeping rather than straightforward sluggishness. Either direction is a reason to ask for the same simple blood test rather than guess which way things are off.

Can blood sugar swings make you tired during the day?

Yes, and this is one of the more common everyday causes, even in people without diabetes. A large, refined-carb-heavy meal can trigger a sharp rise in blood sugar followed by a steep drop an hour or two later, and that drop often feels like a wall of fatigue, sometimes called a sugar crash. Skipping meals and then overeating later can produce a similar swing.

Eating meals with some protein, fiber, and fat alongside carbohydrates tends to blunt that spike-and-crash pattern. If afternoon crashes are a near-daily thing, it's worth paying attention to what and when you're eating before assuming something more serious is going on. See our blood sugar and brain fog article for more on the glucose-energy connection.

A related trap is reaching for caffeine or an energy drink to push through a sugar crash. It can mask the dip in the moment but often sets up a second crash later as the caffeine wears off too. Breaking the cycle usually means fixing the meal pattern that caused the first dip, rather than layering a stimulant on top of it.

Is dehydration a real cause of fatigue?

It is, and it's an easy one to overlook because thirst isn't always an obvious signal, especially as people get older. Even mild dehydration, around 1 to 2% of body weight in fluid loss, has been shown in small studies to measurably affect mood and perceived energy. Coffee and tea count toward fluid intake despite the old myth that caffeine cancels it out; the diuretic effect is mild in people who drink them regularly.

A simple, rough check is urine color: pale yellow generally means you're adequately hydrated, while consistently dark urine is a decent everyday signal to drink more, though certain vitamins and medications can also darken it.

Plenty of people simply don't drink much during a busy workday, especially if their main fluid is coffee sipped between meetings rather than water taken deliberately. Hot weather, intense exercise, and some medications also raise fluid needs above the usual baseline, so the same habits that work fine most of the year can fall short during a heat wave or a harder training block.

Can stress and mental health cause physical tiredness?

Very much so. Chronic stress keeps the body's stress-hormone system running, which disrupts sleep, digestion, and energy regulation all at once. Depression and anxiety both list fatigue as one of their most common physical symptoms, not just a mood complaint, and the tiredness that comes with them often doesn't improve much with extra sleep or rest.

This kind of fatigue tends to come bundled with other changes: lower motivation, trouble concentrating, changes in appetite, or losing interest in things you normally enjoy. If that description fits, the more useful next step is usually talking to a doctor or therapist rather than troubleshooting sleep hygiene alone.

Burnout specifically, the kind that follows months of sustained overwork, often presents as this exact blend: physical fatigue, mental fog, and a flattened mood that doesn't lift much on a day off. It's worth naming to a doctor or therapist as burnout specifically, since the recovery approach differs somewhat from treating ordinary tiredness.

Which everyday habits quietly drain energy?

Beyond the bigger medical causes above, a handful of everyday habits can shave away energy without ever feeling like the obvious culprit. None of these alone usually explains severe, persistent fatigue, but stacked together over a week they add up more than people expect.

  • Caffeine too late in the day. Caffeine has a half-life of roughly 5 to 6 hours, so a 3 p.m. coffee can still be affecting sleep at 9 p.m.
  • Alcohol before bed. It can help you fall asleep faster but fragments sleep later in the night.
  • Long sedentary stretches. Paradoxically, low activity tends to lower energy rather than conserve it; short movement breaks often help more than resting does.
  • Skipping breakfast, then crashing by mid-morning. This sets up the blood sugar swing described earlier.
  • Screens right before bed. Bright light and engaging content both delay the body's natural wind-down.
  • An irregular sleep and wake schedule. Shifting bedtimes by a few hours on weekends disrupts your circadian rhythm similarly to mild jet lag.

As with most causes of fatigue, these habits rarely act alone. A late coffee plus a skipped lunch plus a stressful week is a realistic combination, and untangling it usually means adjusting more than one habit at a time rather than hunting for a single culprit.

When is fatigue a sign to see a doctor?

Most tiredness has an ordinary, fixable explanation. A doctor's visit is worth prioritizing if fatigue is severe enough to interfere with daily life, has lasted more than two to four weeks despite reasonable sleep, or comes with other symptoms like unexplained weight loss or gain, shortness of breath, chest pain, swelling, unusually heavy periods, fever, or a noticeable change in mood or memory.

A doctor can run the basic blood work that catches most of the common causes above in one visit, which is a faster path than cycling through supplements or sleep apps on your own. For more on the habits that affect day-to-day energy, see our energy and metabolism coverage.

Tiredness is rarely one thing. It's usually two or three ordinary causes stacked on top of each other.

Frequently asked questions

Why am I tired even after 8 hours of sleep?

Sleep quality matters as much as duration. Frequent waking, sleep apnea, late caffeine, or alcohol can all fragment 8 hours enough to leave you feeling unrested.

Can dehydration alone make you tired?

Yes, even mild dehydration has been linked to lower perceived energy and mood in small studies, and it's an easy cause to rule out by simply drinking more water.

Can anemia cause tiredness without any other symptoms?

It can in milder cases, though pale skin, shortness of breath, and dizziness often appear as it progresses. A blood test is the only reliable way to confirm it either way.

Does low vitamin D cause fatigue?

Some studies link low vitamin D to fatigue, though the evidence is less consistent than for iron or B12. It's reasonable to ask your doctor to check it alongside other common causes.

Can anxiety cause physical exhaustion?

Yes. Anxiety keeps the body's stress response active, which disrupts sleep and depletes energy, and the resulting fatigue is a physical symptom, not just a mental one.

Is an afternoon energy crash normal?

A mild dip in alertness in the early-to-mid afternoon is a normal part of the body's circadian rhythm for many people. A severe crash most days is more often tied to meal timing and composition.

How much water should I drink if I think dehydration is the cause?

General guidance is roughly 11 to 15 cups of total fluids a day for most adults, including food, though individual needs vary with activity, climate, and body size.
