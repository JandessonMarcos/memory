Allergy Medication and Memory Fog: The Antihistamine Link

Not all antihistamines behave the same way in the brain. The difference comes down to a second receptor they were never meant to touch.

By Sarah Coleman, Health Editor  ·  Reviewed by Dr. Marcus Reed, MD  ·  Published August 25, 2026

Key takeaways

  • First-generation antihistamines cross into the brain and block acetylcholine, the neurotransmitter memory depends on.
  • Diphenhydramine is the common example, and it's in many nighttime cold and sleep products.
  • Second-generation options behave differently, entering the brain far less and causing much less cognitive effect.
  • Cumulative anticholinergic exposure has been linked to dementia risk in observational research, though causation isn't proven.
  • Never stop a prescribed medication on your own. Bring the question to whoever prescribed it.

Someone takes an over-the-counter sleep aid for a few weeks and notices they feel slower. Words don't arrive as easily. They put it down to poor sleep or stress. Often the tablet itself is doing it, and the reason is a receptor the drug was never designed to hit.

Antihistamines split into two useful categories, and the split matters more for your brain than the allergy aisle suggests.

The mechanism behind the fog

Antihistamines block histamine receptors, which is how they stop allergy symptoms. Older ones, developed decades ago, aren't selective. They also block muscarinic acetylcholine receptors.

Acetylcholine is central to attention, learning and memory formation. It's the same system that drugs for Alzheimer's disease try to support, by slowing the breakdown of acetylcholine to keep more of it available. Blocking those receptors pushes in the opposite direction.

Add a second factor: first-generation antihistamines cross the blood-brain barrier readily. They reach brain tissue in meaningful concentrations, where histamine itself plays a role in wakefulness and alertness. Blocking it there produces the sedation these drugs are known for.

Which ones are the problem

First-generation antihistamines with notable anticholinergic activity include diphenhydramine, chlorphenamine, hydroxyzine, promethazine and doxylamine. Diphenhydramine deserves particular attention because of where it appears. It's the active ingredient in many over-the-counter sleep aids, and in the nighttime versions of combination cold and flu products.

People often don't realise they're taking it. A nighttime cold remedy, a sleep tablet and a motion sickness pill can all contain drugs from this class, and taking more than one at once stacks the effect.

Second-generation antihistamines were specifically designed to stay out of the brain. Loratadine, cetirizine, fexofenadine and desloratadine cross the blood-brain barrier far less and have much weaker effects on acetylcholine receptors. Cetirizine causes drowsiness in some people, though generally less than the older drugs. For most people managing seasonal allergies, these are the sensible default.

What the research shows about long-term use

Several large observational studies have examined cumulative anticholinergic exposure, meaning total dose across many drug classes over years, and reported associations with higher dementia risk. The association has appeared consistently enough that clinical guidelines now advise caution with these medications in older adults.

Interpret that carefully. Observational studies can't establish cause. There's a genuine possibility of reverse causation, where early cognitive changes lead to symptoms that get treated with these drugs. Researchers have tried to account for that, but it can't be fully ruled out.

What can be said more confidently is that the short-term cognitive effects are real and measurable. Studies have found impairments in attention, reaction time and working memory in people taking first-generation antihistamines, sometimes without those people noticing anything themselves. That gap between measured impairment and perceived impairment is why the driving warnings exist.

Why older adults are more affected

Two things change with age. Acetylcholine signalling declines naturally, so there's less reserve to absorb a hit. And drug clearance slows, meaning a standard dose reaches higher concentrations and stays longer.

Polypharmacy compounds it. Anticholinergic activity shows up across many drug classes: certain antidepressants, bladder medications for overactive bladder, some drugs for Parkinson's, muscle relaxants and older antipsychotics. Each may be mild on its own while the total burden is substantial. Prescribing guidance for older adults, including the Beers Criteria in the US, specifically flags first-generation antihistamines for this reason.

The sleep aid problem

Using diphenhydramine as a sleep aid is common and worth reconsidering. It does cause drowsiness, but the sleep it produces is altered rather than natural, with suppression of REM sleep reported in studies. Tolerance to the sedative effect develops within days for many people, so it stops working while the anticholinergic burden continues.

Next-day grogginess is common. So is the paradoxical outcome of taking something to sleep better and thinking worse the following day. If sleep is the underlying issue, the more useful interventions are behavioural, and our guide to sleep and memory covers why the quality of that sleep matters so much for recall.

What to do about it

Start by reading labels on everything you take, including over-the-counter products. Diphenhydramine hides in more places than most people expect.

For allergies, ask your pharmacist about switching to a second-generation option. This is a straightforward conversation and pharmacists handle it constantly. For nasal symptoms specifically, steroid nasal sprays work locally with minimal systemic effect and are often more effective anyway.

For prescribed medications, do not stop or change anything on your own. Some anticholinergic drugs cause withdrawal effects, and others treat conditions that will return. Bring a full list of what you take, including supplements and over-the-counter products, to your doctor or pharmacist and ask directly whether the total anticholinergic burden can be reduced. That question, phrased that way, tends to get a useful answer.

If fog persists after addressing medication, other treatable causes are worth ruling out. Our piece on what causes brain fog runs through the common ones, and the habits in our guide to improving your memory apply once the reversible causes are handled.

The gap between measured impairment and noticed impairment is the dangerous part. People rarely feel as foggy as the tests say they are.

Frequently asked questions

Can antihistamines cause memory problems?

First-generation antihistamines such as diphenhydramine block acetylcholine receptors as well as histamine receptors, and they cross into the brain readily. Acetylcholine is central to attention and memory formation, and studies have measured impairments in attention, reaction time and working memory during use.

Which antihistamines are safest for the brain?

Second-generation antihistamines including loratadine, fexofenadine, desloratadine and cetirizine were designed to stay out of the brain and have much weaker effects on acetylcholine receptors. They are generally the better choice for ongoing allergy management, though cetirizine causes drowsiness in some people.

Is Benadryl bad for memory?

Diphenhydramine, the active ingredient, has significant anticholinergic activity and measurable short-term effects on attention and working memory. Prescribing guidance for older adults advises caution with it. Discuss alternatives with a pharmacist rather than stopping any prescribed medication yourself.

Do antihistamines increase dementia risk?

Large observational studies have linked cumulative anticholinergic exposure to higher dementia risk, which is why clinical guidelines advise caution in older adults. These studies cannot prove cause, and reverse causation remains possible, so the association should be taken seriously without being treated as settled.

Are antihistamines safe to use as sleep aids?

They cause drowsiness, but tolerance to the sedative effect develops within days for many people while the anticholinergic burden continues. Next-day grogginess is common and REM sleep suppression has been reported. Behavioural approaches to sleep are generally a better long-term option.
