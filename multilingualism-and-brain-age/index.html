Multilingual People May Have Brains Up to 13 Years Younger

New research presented at a major European neuroscience conference suggests that speaking several languages tracks with a younger-looking brain on scans. Here's what the study actually found.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published August 27, 2026

Key takeaways

  • New research links speaking more languages to a younger biological brain age, estimated using an AI scan-based model.
  • The gap grew with each additional language: roughly 6 years younger for two languages, 7 for three, and about 13 for four.
  • Starting earlier and reaching higher fluency were linked to a stronger effect in the data.
  • This is observational, conference-presented research, not yet full peer-reviewed publication, so treat the exact numbers as preliminary.
  • It fits a broader pattern: demanding mental activity is one of the more consistent habits tied to cognitive reserve.

Short version: researchers reported that people who speak more than one language tend to have brains that look younger on scans than their actual age would predict, using a machine-learning model built to estimate biological brain age. The more languages, the bigger the apparent gap, topping out around 13 years for people who spoke four. It's an eye-catching number, and it comes with real caveats worth understanding before drawing conclusions about your own life.

What did the study actually measure?

The research, presented at the Federation of European Neuroscience Societies (FENS) Forum 2026, used what's called a brain aging clock: a model trained on brain-scan data to estimate a person's biological brain age, which can run younger or older than their actual, chronological age. Researchers then compared that estimate against how many languages each participant spoke. People who spoke two languages showed brains estimated at roughly six years younger than their chronological age. Three languages tracked with about seven years younger, and four languages with roughly 13 years younger. The effect was stronger in people who had learned their additional language earlier in life and reached higher fluency in it.

Does this mean language learning causes a younger brain?

Not on its own, and this is the part worth sitting with. This is observational data: it shows a pattern, not a controlled experiment that assigned some people to learn languages and others not to. People who become fluent in multiple languages often differ from single-language speakers in other ways too, including education level, travel, socioeconomic background and general engagement in mentally demanding activity, and any of those factors could contribute to the pattern on its own. Findings presented at a conference like FENS also haven't yet gone through full peer review, the process where independent scientists scrutinize methods and results before publication in a journal, so the precise numbers here should be treated as an early, promising signal rather than a settled fact.

How does this fit with what's already known about bilingualism and the brain?

It lines up with a research thread that's been building for years. Our own guide to bilingualism and the brain covers earlier work suggesting multilingual people tend to show a later average age of dementia diagnosis, thought to reflect greater cognitive reserve: extra capacity built up through sustained mental effort that helps the brain tolerate age-related changes before symptoms appear. This new brain-age research adds a more visible, scan-based marker to that idea, though it's measuring something related, not identical, to dementia risk itself. For more on the underlying concept, see our explainer on what cognitive reserve is and how to build it.

The exact numbers are preliminary. The broader pattern, that demanding mental effort tracks with a more resilient-looking brain, keeps showing up across very different kinds of studies.

What this means for you

If you already speak more than one language, there's no new action needed. If you don't, this single study isn't a strong enough reason on its own to start, but it's a reasonable data point in favor of something worth doing anyway: sustained, effortful mental engagement, whether that's a new language, an instrument, or another skill that genuinely challenges you rather than passive entertainment. It's also worth noting the study didn't test whether starting later in life produces the same benefit as starting young and reaching high fluency, so adult learners shouldn't expect the full 13-year figure to apply to them. Frequently asked questions

Does speaking multiple languages really make your brain younger?

New research found that people who speak more languages tend to have a lower "brain age" as estimated from brain scans, with the biggest gap, about 13 years, seen in people who spoke four languages. This is an association from observational data, not proof that language learning directly causes a younger brain.

How did researchers measure brain age?

The team used an AI-based "brain aging clock," a model trained to estimate a person's biological brain age from patterns on brain scans, then compared that estimate with each participant's actual age and the number of languages they spoke.

Is it too late to get this benefit if I start learning a language as an adult?

The research suggests starting earlier and reaching higher fluency were linked to a stronger effect, but that doesn't mean adult learning has no value. Mentally demanding, sustained learning at any age is one of the more consistent habits linked to cognitive reserve.

Was this study peer-reviewed?

The findings were presented at a research conference, the Federation of European Neuroscience Societies Forum. Conference presentations are an early step in the scientific process and typically precede full peer-reviewed publication, so the results should be considered preliminary until that happens.
