Zinc for Men: Benefits, Dosage and Food Sources

Zinc shows up in testosterone production, immune defense and wound healing, but more isn't automatically better. Here's what the evidence actually supports.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published October 30, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Zinc supports testosterone production, immune function, wound healing and prostate tissue, and a real deficiency can lower testosterone and sperm quality.
  • Most adult men need about 11 mg of zinc a day. Oysters, red meat, poultry, pumpkin seeds and chickpeas are among the richest sources.
  • Zinc mainly raises testosterone in men who are actually deficient. Topping off an already-adequate level doesn't appear to add much.
  • Mild shortfalls are more common than people assume, especially in men who eat little meat or shellfish, drink heavily, or train hard for endurance sports.
  • More isn't automatically better. High-dose, long-term supplementation can lower copper levels and has been linked to prostate concerns in some research.

Zinc for men matters most for testosterone, sperm health, immune defense and wound healing, and a real shortfall can affect all four. Most men need about 11 mg a day, easily covered by oysters, red meat, poultry, pumpkin seeds or beans. Supplements mainly help men who are genuinely deficient, not everyone who takes them.

It's a small mineral with an outsized reputation, partly deserved and partly exaggerated by supplement marketing. Here's where the evidence actually lands, how much you need, and when a supplement is worth considering instead of just adjusting your plate.

Part of the confusion comes from how zinc gets marketed. Ads aimed at men often blur the line between "zinc is essential for testosterone" (true) and "more zinc means more testosterone" (not supported for men who aren't deficient). Both statements can't be right at the same time, and the research only backs the first one.

What does zinc actually do in the body?

Zinc is a cofactor for more than 300 enzymes, which is a dry way of saying it's involved in an unusually wide range of processes: DNA synthesis, immune cell activity, wound healing, taste and smell, and hormone production, including the pathway that regulates testosterone. It's also concentrated in reproductive tissue, including sperm.

None of that means zinc is a performance enhancer for men who already have enough of it. Most of its documented benefits show up specifically when correcting a shortfall, not when adding extra on top of a normal level.

Your body doesn't store zinc the way it stores, say, vitamin A or iron. There's no large reserve to draw from, so a steady daily intake matters more than it does for some other nutrients. Miss it for a day or two and nothing happens; go weeks eating a diet consistently low in zinc-rich foods, and levels can genuinely drop.

What happens if a man doesn't get enough zinc for a long time?

Prolonged, significant deficiency, the kind seen in severe malnutrition or certain malabsorption disorders, can cause delayed wound healing, hair loss, skin changes, and in younger males, delayed puberty and reduced testicular function. True severe deficiency of that kind is uncommon in the US. Milder, borderline-low zinc, which is more common, tends to show up as subtler things: more frequent minor infections, slower recovery from illness, and in some men, a modest dip in testosterone.

How much zinc do men need per day?

The recommended intake for adult men in the US is 11 mg a day, higher than the 8 mg recommended for women, mostly because of zinc's role in male reproductive tissue. The safe upper limit from food and supplements combined is 40 mg a day for adults; going well past that regularly is where problems start. Those numbers come from the National Academies' dietary reference intakes, the same body that sets recommendations for most vitamins and minerals in the US.

What foods give men the most zinc?

FoodZinc (roughly, per typical serving)
Oysters, cooked (3 oz)30–75 mg, by far the richest source
Beef chuck roast (3 oz)~7 mg
Pumpkin seeds (1 oz)~2.2 mg
Chickpeas, cooked (1 cup)~2.5 mg
Cashews (1 oz)~1.6 mg
Chicken breast (3 oz)~1 mg
Fortified breakfast cereal (1 serving)varies widely; check the label

Red meat and shellfish are the most concentrated sources, and their zinc is also more easily absorbed than the zinc in beans, nuts and grains, which contain phytates that bind some of it. That's a bigger deal for vegetarian and vegan men, covered below.

Cooking method and food pairing make a smaller difference too. Soaking or sprouting beans and grains before cooking reduces their phytate content somewhat, which modestly improves how much zinc the body can actually absorb from a plant-heavy meal. It's a minor lever, not a replacement for eating zinc-dense foods in the first place. Spreading zinc-rich foods across the week, rather than relying on one big serving of oysters a month, is a more realistic way to stay consistent.

Does zinc affect testosterone?

The connection is real but narrower than the headlines suggest. In men who are genuinely zinc-deficient, correcting that deficiency has, in several small studies, raised low testosterone back up. In men whose zinc levels are already adequate, adding more zinc on top hasn't reliably moved testosterone any higher.

In other words, zinc behaves like a floor, not a lever. If you're below it, fixing that matters. If you're already standing on solid ground, more zinc isn't the next step up. That distinction gets lost in a lot of marketing aimed at men, which tends to imply that any zinc supplement will raise testosterone for anyone who takes it.

Zinc also plays a supporting role in sperm production and motility, independent of its effect on testosterone itself. Some fertility clinics check zinc status as part of a broader workup for men dealing with fertility concerns, though it's one factor among many and rarely the whole story on its own.

Can zinc help with prostate health?

The prostate naturally holds one of the highest zinc concentrations of any tissue in the body, and low prostate zinc has been observed in some prostate cancer research, which is part of why zinc gets marketed for prostate health. The picture is more complicated than that single fact suggests.

Some observational research, including a large long-running health professionals study, found that very high cumulative zinc intake from supplements, well above typical dietary levels, was associated with a higher risk of advanced prostate disease over time, not a lower one. The practical takeaway: getting adequate zinc from food is reasonable, but megadosing supplements specifically to protect your prostate isn't supported and may not be a good idea. Talk to a doctor before taking high-dose zinc for this reason, and see our guide to enlarged prostate symptoms and when to get checked if that's your actual concern.

This is a good example of why more of a nutrient isn't automatically protective just because the nutrient is important. The prostate needing zinc to function normally and a supplement flooding the body with extra zinc are two different things, and research on the second one has not been reassuring enough to recommend it as a prostate strategy.

What are the signs of low zinc?

  • Frequent infections or colds that linger longer than they used to
  • Slow-healing cuts or wounds
  • A dulled sense of taste or smell
  • Thinning hair or brittle nails
  • In more pronounced deficiency, low testosterone or reduced sperm count

These signs overlap with a long list of other, unrelated issues, so they're a reason to ask a doctor about testing, not a reason to self-diagnose and start megadosing a supplement. A doctor will usually weigh these symptoms together with your diet, medications and any digestive conditions rather than relying on a single lab number.

Who is most at risk of falling short?

Men who eat little meat, poultry, or shellfish are at higher risk, since plant-based zinc is harder for the body to absorb. Heavy alcohol use increases zinc loss through urine. Endurance athletes can lose extra zinc through sweat. Older men, and anyone with a digestive condition like Crohn's disease that limits absorption, are also more likely to run low. If that describes you, it's worth mentioning at your next checkup rather than guessing.

Certain medications add to the risk too. Long-term use of proton pump inhibitors for acid reflux, some diuretics, and ACE inhibitors used for blood pressure can all lower zinc status somewhat over months or years. None of this means stopping a needed medication; it's a reason to mention zinc if you're on one of these long-term and start noticing symptoms.

Can you take too much zinc?

Yes. Short-term, too much zinc can cause nausea, stomach cramps and headaches. The bigger long-term concern is copper. Zinc and copper compete for absorption, and sustained high-dose zinc supplementation is a well-documented cause of copper deficiency, which can in turn cause anemia and nerve problems. This is almost always a supplement issue, not a food one; it's hard to overdo zinc from diet alone.

Zinc nasal sprays and gels are a separate, specific risk worth flagging: several were pulled from some markets or relabeled after reports of permanent loss of smell linked to zinc contacting nasal tissue directly. That's a different exposure route than swallowing a capsule, but it's a reminder that how a supplement is taken matters, not only how much.

Should men take a zinc supplement?

If you eat meat, poultry, or shellfish regularly, you're probably already close to covered and a supplement likely won't add much. If you're vegetarian, vegan, an older adult eating less overall, train at an endurance level, or drink heavily, a modest supplement in the 10 to 15 mg range, or simply eating more zinc-rich foods, is a reasonable step. Our Men's Health hub covers other nutrients worth checking alongside zinc, including protein intake, which matters for similar reasons; see how much protein you need per day after 50.

If testosterone is the actual concern, zinc is one small piece of a bigger picture that includes sleep, body weight and overall health. Our guide to testosterone levels by age covers what's considered normal and when it's worth getting tested, which is a more direct answer than guessing from a supplement aisle.

When shopping for a supplement, pay attention to the form on the label, not just the milligram number. Zinc gluconate and zinc sulfate are common, inexpensive and reasonably well absorbed; zinc picolinate and citrate cost more but may absorb somewhat better for some people. None of them need to be taken on an empty stomach, and in fact doing so can cause nausea in some men, so pairing a dose with a small meal is a reasonable default.

Zinc doesn't turn an adequate level into a bigger one. It mostly matters for the men who don't have enough to begin with.

Frequently asked questions

Is zinc from supplements absorbed as well as zinc from food?

It depends on the form. Zinc picolinate and zinc citrate tend to absorb somewhat better than zinc oxide, a common cheap form. Food sources also come with other nutrients that supplements don't provide.

Can zinc help with hair loss?

Only if an actual deficiency is contributing to it. Evidence that zinc reverses typical male pattern hair loss in men with normal zinc levels is weak.

Does zinc interact with any medications?

Yes. It can reduce the absorption of certain antibiotics and interact with some diuretics. Take zinc supplements a couple of hours apart from these medications, and check with a pharmacist if you're unsure.

Can vegetarian and vegan men get enough zinc?

Yes, with some planning. Plant-based zinc is less bioavailable than zinc from meat and shellfish, so vegetarian and vegan men need to eat zinc-rich plant foods more intentionally, and may want to ask a doctor about checking their levels.

Does zinc actually help with colds?

Some research on zinc lozenges, taken within a day of symptoms starting, suggests they can shorten a cold by roughly a day. It's not a general immune fix for everyone, and the effect depends on the form and timing.

Is it safe to take zinc with a daily multivitamin?

Usually, but add up the zinc from every source you're taking and keep the combined total under the 40 mg daily upper limit for adults.

Can a blood test tell me if I'm low in zinc?

A standard serum zinc test is not perfectly reliable, since blood levels don't always reflect what's stored in tissue. A doctor can still use it alongside your symptoms and diet to decide whether a supplement trial makes sense.
