Chunking: The Memory Trick Behind Phone Numbers and Passwords

Ten random digits will beat most people's working memory. Group them into three short chunks and the same information suddenly feels easy. That's not a trick of willpower, it's how memory is built.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published September 10, 2026

Photo: Amila Tennakoon / Wikimedia Commons / CC BY 2.0

Key takeaways

  • Chunking groups individual pieces of information into fewer, larger units, so working memory has less to juggle at once.
  • Phone numbers, credit card numbers and license plates are formatted this way on purpose, not by accident.
  • The classic "seven plus or minus two" rule comes from decades-old research; more recent work suggests real working memory capacity is closer to four chunks under strict conditions.
  • Chunking works best when the groups you form already mean something to you, not just when they're shorter.
  • It helps you hold information long enough to use it, it doesn't by itself make that information stick for weeks.

Try to hold ten random digits in your head as ten separate items and most people lose track by the sixth or seventh. Group those same digits into three short chunks, an area code, an exchange, a line number, and the task suddenly feels manageable. That's chunking: breaking information into fewer, larger units so working memory has less to track. It's one of the oldest findings in memory research, and it's quietly built into more of daily life than most people notice.

What chunking actually means

Chunking is the process of grouping individual pieces of information into a smaller number of meaningful units. A string like 4 7 2 9 1 5 is six separate items to track. Regrouped as 472-915, it becomes two. Your brain isn't holding six things anymore, it's holding two, and each one now carries more information than it did before.

The same idea works with letters. FBICIAIBM looks like nine meaningless characters until you notice it's three familiar chunks: FBI, CIA, IBM. Nothing about the raw information changed. What changed is how it's organized, and organization is most of what makes something memorable.

Why chunking works: a quick look at the psychology

The idea traces back to a well-known 1956 paper by psychologist George Miller, often summarized as "the magical number seven, plus or minus two." The claim was that short-term memory holds roughly five to nine items at once, regardless of what those items are. A chunk could be a single digit or a whole word; the number of chunks mattered more than their size.

Later research has pushed back on the exact number. Some studies, notably work by Nelson Cowan in the early 2000s, suggest that under stricter test conditions, real capacity is closer to four chunks. The precise figure is still debated among researchers, and it isn't the part worth memorizing here. The useful takeaway is simpler: working memory has a hard ceiling on how many separate things it can hold, no matter how motivated or focused you are, and grouping information reduces how many things you're actually asking it to hold.

Where you already rely on chunking without noticing

Phone numbers are split into a three-digit and a four-digit group for exactly this reason. Credit card numbers come printed in blocks of four. Social security numbers and license plates follow the same logic. None of that formatting is arbitrary. It's a decades-old, low-cost way to make a long string of digits easier for a human to read back correctly, and it predates any of the psychology research that later explained why it works.

Passwords are a more modern twist on the same problem. Security guidance has shifted in recent years toward longer passphrases built from several unrelated words rather than a short string of random characters, numbers and symbols. A four-word passphrase is, functionally, four chunks instead of twelve or sixteen individual characters. It can be both easier to recall and harder to guess than a shorter jumble, which is a rare case where a security best practice and a memory shortcut point the same direction.

How to chunk something you actually need to remember

Start by looking for a natural grouping, three or four items tends to work better than trying to force one big chunk. A grocery list of twelve items is hard to hold as twelve; sorted into produce, dairy and pantry, it becomes three small groups instead of a long flat list.

Next, give each chunk meaning rather than leaving it arbitrary. A date like 1-9-4-5 is four digits until you recognize it as 1945, a single meaningful unit. Numbers, lists and instructions all become easier once you can tie the pieces to something you already know, a rhythm, a rhyme, an existing fact, or even just a phrase that sounds right when you say it out loud.

This pairs naturally with other study techniques. Chunking organizes what you're trying to learn; it doesn't by itself determine how well that material survives a week from now. For material you need to keep past a single use, like a presentation or an exam, combining a good chunking structure with spaced repetition and active recall covers both halves of the problem: organizing the information and actually retaining it.

Where chunking runs into limits

Chunking is mainly a working-memory trick. It helps you hold something in mind long enough to use it right now, dial a number, type a code, recall a short list during a conversation. That's a different job from building a long-term memory that survives days or weeks, which depends more on sleep, repetition and how deeply you engage with the material the first time. It also depends on the pieces meaning something to you specifically. Grouping random gibberish into blocks of four doesn't make it meaningful, it just makes it shorter. If you can't attach any existing knowledge or association to a chunk, the grouping alone won't carry much of the load. And capacity still varies person to person and task to task, so treat any specific number as a rough guide rather than a hard personal limit.

A simple way to practice it

Next time you need to remember a short list, a code or a set of talking points, pause before you try to memorize it line by line. Look for a way to sort it into two or three groups first. Name each group something short. Then try to recall the groups before you try to recall every item inside them. Most people notice the difference within a few tries, mainly because they've stopped fighting their own working memory limit and started working with it instead.

The trick was never memorizing more. It was needing to hold less at once.

Frequently asked questions

What is chunking in memory?

Chunking is the technique of grouping individual pieces of information, like digits or letters, into fewer, larger, more meaningful units so that working memory has less to track at once.

How many items can you hold in working memory?

A widely cited estimate is five to nine items, sometimes called "seven plus or minus two," though more recent research suggests the real number may be closer to four under strict test conditions. Either way, the capacity is limited and grouping information into chunks helps you work within it.

Why are phone numbers grouped into chunks?

Phone numbers, credit card numbers and similar formats are split into short groups because it's far easier for people to read, say and recall three or four small chunks than one long unbroken string of digits.

Does chunking help you remember something long term?

Chunking mainly helps you hold information in working memory long enough to use it right away. For long-term retention, it works best paired with habits like sleep, repetition and active recall rather than as a standalone technique.

Can chunking improve password memorization?

Yes, in a practical sense. A passphrase built from several unrelated words functions as a handful of memorable chunks rather than many individual characters, which is often easier to recall and can be harder to guess than a shorter string of random characters.
