Leaky Gut: What the Science Actually Says

"Leaky gut" sits in an unusual spot: the underlying biology is real and studied, but the sweeping syndrome built around it online goes well past what that biology has actually shown.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published November 15, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Increased intestinal permeability is a real, measurable phenomenon, studied under that exact name in peer-reviewed research for decades.
  • "Leaky gut syndrome" as a standalone diagnosis is not recognized by mainstream medical organizations as the cause of fatigue, brain fog, acne or weight gain the way some wellness marketing claims.
  • Permeability changes are genuinely linked to specific conditions, including celiac disease, inflammatory bowel disease and some autoimmune disorders.
  • No supplement has been shown to reliably "heal" a leaky gut in well-controlled human trials, despite how often that promise is marketed.
  • Diet, stress, alcohol and certain medications are the factors most consistently linked to changes in gut permeability.

Increased intestinal permeability, the biological process behind "leaky gut," is real and has been measured in peer-reviewed research for decades. "Leaky gut syndrome," the broader idea marketed online as the hidden cause of fatigue, brain fog, acne and weight gain, is not a diagnosis recognized by mainstream medical organizations. The biology is legitimate; the syndrome built on top of it mostly isn't.

That split confuses a lot of people, understandably, since both versions use the same two words. Here's where the real science ends and the marketing picks up.

What is "leaky gut," exactly?

The intestinal lining is a single layer of cells held together by structures called tight junctions, which control what passes from your gut into your bloodstream. "Leaky gut" refers to those tight junctions loosening more than normal, a state researchers call increased intestinal permeability, letting larger molecules, including bacterial fragments, slip through more easily than they should. That's the biology. The word "syndrome" gets attached when this process is blamed, often without direct evidence, for a long list of unrelated symptoms.

Is increased intestinal permeability a real, measurable thing?

Yes. It's studied using specific lab methods, including the lactulose-mannitol test, which tracks how two differently sized sugar molecules pass through the gut wall, and blood markers like zonulin, a protein involved in regulating tight junctions. These tools show up throughout peer-reviewed gastroenterology and immunology research, not just in wellness content. The measurable phenomenon is not in dispute among researchers.

Is "leaky gut syndrome" a recognized medical diagnosis?

No. Major medical and gastroenterology organizations don't recognize "leaky gut syndrome" as a standalone diagnosis that explains a broad set of unrelated symptoms. The distinction matters: intestinal permeability is an observed, measurable change seen alongside certain diseases, while "leaky gut syndrome" is a broader causal story, that this permeability itself is the root cause of fatigue, skin problems, weight gain, joint pain and brain fog, which hasn't been established with the kind of evidence a diagnosis requires.

Can leaky gut cause brain fog, fatigue or skin problems?

It's plausible as one contributing factor in specific situations, not established as a general explanation. The gut-brain connection is real and increasingly well-studied, and some research does suggest that gut-derived inflammation can influence mood and cognition in certain conditions. That's a meaningfully narrower claim than "your leaky gut is causing your brain fog," which assumes a direct, provable chain that research hasn't confirmed for most people experiencing these symptoms. What triggers or worsens intestinal permeability?

Research points to several overlapping factors: a diet low in fiber and high in ultra-processed food, chronic psychological stress, heavy alcohol use, certain medications including long-term NSAID use, and active infections or illness. None of these acts alone in most cases, and the size of the effect varies by person. This is also where the evidence is strongest for doing something about it, since most of these factors are modifiable through ordinary health habits rather than a specialized "gut-healing" protocol.

How is intestinal permeability actually tested?

In research settings, through tools like the lactulose-mannitol ratio test or zonulin blood measurement, mentioned above. In practice, mainstream doctors don't order a "leaky gut test" as part of routine care, partly because these research tools haven't been validated well enough to guide individual treatment decisions, and partly because an abnormal result wouldn't currently point to a specific, proven fix beyond the general habits already known to support gut health. Commercial at-home "leaky gut tests" marketed directly to consumers are not the same validated tools used in research, and their accuracy and usefulness are not well established.

The biology of a more permeable gut lining is well documented. The leap from that biology to "this explains your fatigue, your skin and your weight" is where the evidence runs out.

Can you heal or improve gut permeability?

Some habits have real, independent evidence for supporting gut barrier health, even without a specific "leaky gut" product attached. A high-fiber diet feeds the bacteria that help maintain the gut lining; see our high-fiber foods list for where to start. Reducing ultra-processed food, moderating alcohol and managing chronic stress are all independently linked to better gut health markers. Specific supplements marketed as "leaky gut healers," including many glutamine and collagen products, have limited and inconsistent human evidence rather than the strong, direct proof their marketing often implies.

What should you be skeptical of when "leaky gut" comes up online?

  • Any claim that leaky gut is the single root cause of a long, unrelated list of symptoms.
  • A specific supplement or expensive protocol promising to "seal" or "heal" your gut lining in days.
  • An at-home test marketed as diagnosing leaky gut without reference to standard clinical tools.
  • Content that skips real, diagnosable conditions (IBS, celiac disease, IBD) to jump straight to "leaky gut" as the explanation.

When should you see a doctor about gut symptoms?

See a doctor for persistent bloating, pain, diarrhea, constipation or unexplained weight change, especially if symptoms last more than a few weeks or come with blood in the stool, fever or unintended weight loss. These deserve a real diagnostic workup, which can rule in or out conditions like IBS, celiac disease or inflammatory bowel disease, rather than a self-applied "leaky gut" label. The honest summary: the gut lining's permeability is a real, studied piece of biology, worth taking seriously in the context of specific diseases. Treating it as the hidden explanation for almost any symptom, and the justification for an expensive supplement stack, is where the science and the marketing part ways. Frequently asked questions

Is leaky gut the same as IBS?

No, they're different things, though they can overlap. IBS is a recognized diagnosis based on specific symptom patterns. Increased intestinal permeability is a measurable physical change that has been found in some, but not all, people with IBS and many other conditions.

Can supplements really "heal" leaky gut?

No single supplement has been shown to reverse intestinal permeability on its own in well-designed human trials. Some nutrients, like certain fibers and glutamine, have preliminary research behind them, but the strong, specific healing claims made by many leaky gut supplements outpace the evidence.

Does leaky gut cause autoimmune disease, or does autoimmune disease cause leaky gut?

Researchers genuinely aren't sure, and it may run both ways. Increased permeability shows up in several autoimmune conditions, but whether it's a cause, a consequence, or part of a shared underlying process is still being studied rather than settled.

Is there a reliable blood test for leaky gut?

Not one used in routine clinical care. Research tools like the lactulose-mannitol test and zonulin measurement exist, but mainstream medical groups don't consider any current commercial "leaky gut panel" reliable enough to diagnose or guide treatment.

Can stress alone cause leaky gut?

Chronic stress is one of several factors linked to increased intestinal permeability in research, alongside diet, alcohol, certain medications and illness. It's one contributing factor among several, not a standalone proven cause on its own.

Does a "leaky gut diet" actually have evidence behind it?

Specific commercial leaky gut diets haven't been tested as a package in rigorous trials. The individual habits many of them recommend, more fiber, less ultra-processed food, more diverse plants, do have independent evidence for supporting gut health generally.

Is leaky gut linked to food allergies?

There's a biological link worth taking seriously: a more permeable gut lining could theoretically let more intact food proteins reach the immune system. Research on this specific chain in humans is still early, and it hasn't translated into a validated test or treatment for allergies.
