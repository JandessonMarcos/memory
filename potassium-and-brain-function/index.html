Potassium and Brain Function: An Overlooked Electrolyte for Cognition

Magnesium and omega-3s get all the attention. Potassium, the electrolyte your neurons actually run on, rarely makes the list. Here's what it does for the brain, and how much you really need.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published September 17, 2026

Photo: Nenad Stojkovic / Wikimedia Commons / CC BY 2.0

Key takeaways

  • Potassium is an electrolyte, and neurons need the right balance of it to build the electrical charge they fire signals with.
  • Most U.S. adults don't get enough. The adequate intake is around 2,600–3,400 mg a day, and processed diets tend to fall short.
  • Potassium's best-documented brain link is indirect: it supports healthy blood pressure, and blood vessel health matters for how well the brain is supplied over decades.
  • Food beats supplements for almost everyone. Bananas, potatoes, beans, leafy greens and yogurt are strong sources.
  • Supplements carry real risk for people with kidney disease or on certain blood pressure medications. Ask a doctor first.

Potassium doesn't show up on many brain-health lists. Omega-3s, B vitamins and magnesium get the spotlight, while potassium stays filed under "blood pressure nutrient" and not much else. That's a narrower view than the biology supports. Potassium is one of the handful of electrolytes that every neuron in your body depends on just to send a signal. Here's what the evidence actually says about potassium and thinking, and where the real caution points are.

What does potassium actually do for the brain?

Neurons communicate using tiny electrical pulses, and those pulses exist because of a charge difference between the inside and outside of the cell. Potassium sits on the inside; sodium sits mostly outside. The cell spends energy constantly pumping these ions back into place, called the sodium-potassium pump, and that pump is what keeps a neuron "primed" to fire. Without enough potassium in the right balance, that resting charge gets harder to maintain. This isn't a brain-specific fact. It's how every excitable cell in the body works, muscle cells included, which is part of why low potassium shows up as muscle weakness before it shows up as anything cognitive.

How much potassium do adults actually need?

The National Academies set the adequate intake at roughly 2,600 mg a day for adult women and 3,400 mg for adult men. That's higher than most people assume, and higher than what a typical American diet delivers. Diets built around processed and packaged food tend to run low in potassium while running high in sodium, which is a pattern nutrition researchers have flagged for years as a mismatch worth correcting, not because of any single dramatic study but because it shows up consistently across national dietary surveys.

What happens when potassium runs low?

A mild shortfall from diet alone often causes no obvious symptoms. A more significant drop, called hypokalemia, is a different story: muscle weakness, cramping, fatigue and, in more serious cases, an irregular heartbeat. Hypokalemia is usually caused by something more specific than diet, such as certain diuretics, vomiting, diarrhea or other medical conditions. It's worth being honest here: brain fog and poor concentration have dozens of possible causes, and low potassium is rarely the first one to suspect. If you're generally healthy and eating a reasonable variety of food, dietary potassium deficiency severe enough to affect thinking is uncommon.

Can eating more potassium actually help your memory?

The strongest, most consistent research links potassium intake to blood pressure, not directly to memory scores. Diets higher in potassium relative to sodium are associated with healthier blood pressure in population studies, and blood pressure control matters for brain health because the brain depends on a steady, well-regulated blood supply. Chronic high blood pressure is a recognized risk factor for vascular contributions to cognitive decline. So the honest chain of evidence looks like this: potassium supports blood pressure, blood pressure affects vascular health, and vascular health affects the brain over the long term. That's a real, biologically sound pathway. It is not the same as saying potassium will sharpen your recall this week, and no legitimate source claims that it does.

Best food sources of potassium

Bananas are the famous one, but they're far from the richest source. Potatoes and sweet potatoes, with the skin on, deliver more potassium per serving than a banana does. White beans, lentils and other legumes are strong sources too, along with leafy greens like spinach and Swiss chard, avocado, yogurt, salmon and oranges. A varied diet built around whole produce, dairy or dairy alternatives, and legumes will generally get most healthy adults much closer to the target than any single "superfood" will.

Is a potassium supplement a good idea?

For most healthy adults, food is the better route, and there's a practical reason for that beyond general nutrition advice: single-ingredient potassium supplements sold over the counter are capped at low doses by regulators specifically because high-dose potassium taken quickly can be dangerous. Getting to the adequate intake through food spreads the mineral out across the day in a way a pill can't safely replicate. If a doctor has diagnosed a genuine deficiency, they'll typically prescribe a dose and monitor it with blood tests, which is a different situation entirely from picking up a bottle at a pharmacy on your own.

Potassium's link to the brain isn't mysterious. It's electrical, and it's cardiovascular. What it isn't is a nootropic, and no honest source will tell you a banana works like one.

Who needs to be careful with potassium?

People with reduced kidney function are the biggest group, since the kidneys are what clear extra potassium from the blood. Anyone taking an ACE inhibitor, an ARB, or a potassium-sparing diuretic should talk to a doctor before increasing potassium intake substantially, because those drugs already push potassium levels up on their own. Too much potassium, called hyperkalemia, can affect heart rhythm and is a genuine medical concern, not a minor footnote. If you have any of these conditions or take these medications, treat potassium like any other nutrient that needs a doctor's input, not a shortcut to better focus. Frequently asked questions

Does potassium really affect brain function?

Yes, indirectly and directly. Potassium is one of the electrolytes that sets up the electrical charge neurons use to fire, so it plays a basic role in nerve signaling. It also supports healthy blood pressure, and blood vessel health is closely tied to how well the brain gets oxygen and nutrients over the long run.

How much potassium do adults need each day?

Current U.S. guidance sets the adequate intake around 2,600 mg a day for adult women and 3,400 mg for adult men. Most Americans fall short of that, largely because processed food is low in potassium while whole fruits, vegetables and legumes, the biggest natural sources, make up a smaller share of the typical diet.

What are signs of low potassium?

Mild shortfalls from diet alone are often symptom-free. More significant drops, called hypokalemia, can cause muscle weakness, cramping, fatigue and heart palpitations. Since fatigue and poor concentration overlap with dozens of other causes, low potassium is rarely something to self-diagnose from brain fog alone.

Is it safe to take a potassium supplement?

Not without medical guidance. High-dose potassium supplements can be dangerous for people with reduced kidney function or those taking ACE inhibitors, ARBs or potassium-sparing diuretics, and excess potassium can affect heart rhythm. Most healthy adults are better off closing the gap with food and asking a doctor before considering a supplement.
