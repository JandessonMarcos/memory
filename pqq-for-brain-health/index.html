PQQ for Brain Health: Benefits, Dosage and Safety

Pyrroloquinoline quinone, or PQQ, has become a fixture in "brain energy" supplements. Here's what it actually does in the body and how strong the human evidence really is.

By Sarah Coleman, Health Editor  ·  Reviewed by Dr. Marcus Reed, MD  ·  Published August 13, 2026

Key takeaways

  • PQQ triggers mitochondrial biogenesis in lab studies, meaning it may help cells build new mitochondria.
  • Human trials are small and few, so the case for brain benefits is promising, not proven.
  • Typical doses run 10 to 20 mg a day, the range used in most existing research.
  • It's found naturally in foods like natto, parsley, green tea and kiwi, in small amounts.
  • It's often paired with CoQ10, another mitochondria-focused nutrient, in supplement blends.

Short answer: PQQ has a genuinely interesting mechanism and some encouraging early signals, but the human research base is thin. Pyrroloquinoline quinone is a naturally occurring compound found in trace amounts in fermented soybeans, parsley, green tea and a handful of other foods. What's made it popular in supplement aisles is its role in mitochondrial biogenesis, the process your cells use to grow new mitochondria, the structures that generate cellular energy. Brain cells rely heavily on a steady energy supply, which is the logic behind marketing PQQ as a "brain energy" nutrient. The mechanism is real. Whether it translates into a noticeable difference in memory or focus for most people is the part still being worked out.

What does PQQ actually do in the body?

In cell and animal studies, PQQ activates signaling pathways involved in creating new mitochondria and appears to protect existing mitochondria from oxidative damage. It also acts as an antioxidant in its own right, neutralizing some of the reactive molecules that build up during normal cell activity. Because neurons are dense with mitochondria and burn through energy quickly, researchers have been curious whether supporting mitochondrial health with PQQ could translate into better cognitive performance, particularly as mitochondrial efficiency tends to decline with age.

Does PQQ actually improve memory or focus?

The honest answer is that the human evidence isn't strong enough yet to say so with confidence. A handful of small trials have tested PQQ, sometimes alone and sometimes combined with CoQ10, and reported modest improvements on measures like working memory, attention and self-reported fatigue. These studies tend to involve a few dozen participants at most and run for a matter of weeks, which limits how much weight they can carry. Nothing here should be read as a fabricated breakthrough; it's an early, plausible line of research rather than a settled conclusion. Larger, independently replicated trials would go a long way toward clarifying who, if anyone, actually benefits.

Where does PQQ come from, and can you get it from food?

PQQ occurs naturally in a range of plant foods, with natto (fermented soybeans), parsley, green peppers, kiwi, papaya and green tea among the richer sources. The amounts are small, typically measured in micrograms per serving, so reaching the 10 to 20 milligram doses used in research through diet alone isn't realistic. That gap is exactly why it's sold as a standalone supplement or folded into broader energy and focus formulas rather than promoted as a "just eat more of X" nutrient.

How does PQQ compare to CoQ10?

They're often sold together because they work on related but distinct parts of the same system. CoQ10 helps mitochondria that already exist run their energy-production machinery more efficiently. PQQ's proposed role is different: encouraging the cell to build new mitochondria in the first place. Some small studies have tested the pair together on the theory that they're complementary rather than redundant, though this combined-effect research is even sparser than the data on either ingredient alone.

What's a sensible dosage, and is it safe?

Most commercial supplements land in the 10 to 20 milligram range, mirroring what's been used in the limited clinical research. There is no established recommended dietary allowance for PQQ, since it isn't classified as an essential nutrient. Reported side effects in studies have been mild and infrequent, mostly occasional headache or a feeling of sleepiness, though safety data beyond a few months of use in humans is limited. As with most emerging supplements, it makes sense to start at a lower dose, watch how you respond, and check with a doctor first if you take other medications or have an existing health condition.

PQQ has a genuinely interesting mechanism and some encouraging early data. It hasn't yet earned the kind of large, independently replicated evidence base that ingredients like citicoline or omega-3 have built up over decades.

Who might consider trying PQQ?

People specifically curious about mitochondrial-support supplements, or who already take CoQ10 and want to explore a complementary ingredient, are the most natural fit. It isn't a first-line recommendation the way sleep, exercise and core nutrients are. Frequently asked questions

What does PQQ do for the brain?

PQQ appears to trigger mitochondrial biogenesis, the process cells use to build new mitochondria, and it has antioxidant properties in lab studies. Brain cells are especially energy-hungry, so healthier mitochondria may support focus and mental stamina, though human evidence is still limited.

Is PQQ backed by human research?

Most of the strongest evidence comes from cell and animal studies. A small number of human trials, generally short and with few participants, have looked at markers like sleep quality, attention and working memory with mixed but generally encouraging results. Larger trials are needed.

What is a typical PQQ dosage?

Most supplements provide 10 to 20 milligrams a day, the range used in the small human studies that exist. There is no established medical dose, so it's worth starting at the lower end and talking to a doctor before adding it to a routine.

Is PQQ safe to take daily?

Short-term use at typical doses appears well tolerated in the available studies, with occasional reports of mild headache or sleepiness. Long-term safety data in humans is limited, so it's best used with medical guidance, especially alongside other medications.
