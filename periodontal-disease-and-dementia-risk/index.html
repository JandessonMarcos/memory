Periodontal Disease and Dementia Risk: What the Research Says

Chronic gum disease has been found in surprising places, including brain tissue from people who had Alzheimer's. Here's what that actually means, and what it doesn't.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published October 5, 2026

Photo: Frankie Fouganthin / Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA 4.0

Key takeaways

  • Population studies consistently find that periodontal disease and tooth loss track with higher dementia risk, though the size of that link varies a lot between studies.
  • A widely cited 2019 study found gum-disease bacteria and their toxins in brain tissue from Alzheimer's patients, and showed the same bacteria could reach and affect the brain in mice.
  • That doesn't prove gum disease causes Alzheimer's in people. Shared risk factors like smoking, diabetes and inflammation complicate the picture.
  • The relationship may run both ways: early cognitive decline can also make it harder to keep up with brushing, flossing and dental visits.
  • Treating gum disease is good medicine regardless, since it's a painful, damaging infection with its own well-documented health risks.

It sounds like the kind of claim that gets exaggerated online, but this one has real research behind it. Is periodontal disease actually linked to dementia risk? The honest answer is yes, there's a genuine association, and a plausible biological story behind it, but it falls well short of proof that treating your gums will protect your memory.

What's the actual evidence connecting gum disease and dementia?

Two different kinds of evidence feed this story, and it helps to keep them separate. The first is population-level: multiple long-term studies have found that people with periodontal disease, or who have lost more teeth, show a higher rate of cognitive decline and dementia diagnosis over time compared with people with healthier gums. These are observational studies, meaning they show a pattern, not a guaranteed cause.

The second is more mechanistic. A widely cited 2019 study published in Science Advances examined brain tissue from people who had Alzheimer's disease and found Porphyromonas gingivalis, the bacterium most responsible for chronic gum disease, along with gingipains, the toxic enzymes it produces. In follow-up experiments with mice, oral infection with the bacterium led it to show up in brain tissue and was associated with higher amyloid-beta production, a hallmark protein tied to Alzheimer's.

Why isn't this considered settled science yet?

A few real gaps keep this from being proven. Human and animal tissue findings are compelling, but they're not the same as a clinical trial showing that treating gum disease in people actually slows cognitive decline. No study like that has reported clear results yet. There's also the confounding problem: smoking, diabetes, poor diet and limited access to dental care all raise the risk of both gum disease and dementia on their own, so some of the overlap may come from these shared drivers rather than gum disease itself.

Reverse causality is worth considering, too. Someone in the early, often unnoticed stages of cognitive decline may simply find it harder to keep up with daily brushing, flossing and dental appointments, which could mean poor oral health is sometimes a consequence of early decline rather than only a cause of later decline.

The strongest honest statement here is that gum disease and brain health are connected in ways researchers are still mapping, not that flossing is a form of dementia prevention.

What's the practical takeaway for someone reading this?

Treat your gums seriously on their own terms. Periodontal disease causes real damage: receding gums, loose teeth, chronic infection and, in some studies, links to heart disease. None of that needs a dementia angle to justify regular brushing, flossing and dental checkups. If ongoing research eventually confirms a protective effect on cognition too, that's simply one more reason layered on top of reasons that already stand on their own.

If you or someone you're caring for has noticeable gum disease and other risk factors, it's worth mentioning to a doctor as part of an overall health picture, alongside the other habits in our guide on how to improve your memory. Frequently asked questions

Does gum disease cause Alzheimer's disease?

That hasn't been proven. Researchers have found a biologically plausible link and consistent associations in population studies, but periodontal disease shares many risk factors with dementia, including smoking, diabetes and lower access to healthcare, which makes a direct cause-and-effect claim premature.

What is the gum bacteria theory everyone mentions?

A widely cited 2019 study found Porphyromonas gingivalis, the bacterium most associated with chronic gum disease, along with its toxic enzymes, in brain tissue from people who had Alzheimer's disease. In mice, oral infection with the bacterium led to brain colonization and higher amyloid-beta levels. It's a real and interesting finding, but it comes from a mix of human tissue analysis and animal models, not a clinical trial in people.

Should I be worried if I already have gum disease?

Worried isn't the useful reaction, but it's worth treating. Periodontal disease is a real infection that damages gums and bone and has its own well-established health risks. Getting it treated is good medicine regardless of what it eventually turns out to mean for dementia risk.

Does brushing and flossing actually lower dementia risk?

Nobody can promise that yet. What's true is that good oral hygiene and regular dental care prevent a painful, damaging disease with its own serious health consequences, and carry no downside. If it also turns out to protect cognition, that's a bonus on top of reasons that already hold up on their own.
