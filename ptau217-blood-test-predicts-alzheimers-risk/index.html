Blood Test Tied to Alzheimer's Protein Predicts Decline a Decade Early

A large study presented at the Alzheimer's Association International Conference and published in JAMA finds a blood marker called p-tau217 forecasts cognitive decline risk years before any symptoms appear.

By Sarah Coleman, Health Editor  ·  Reviewed by Dr. Marcus Reed, MD  ·  Published July 29, 2026

Key takeaways

  • p-tau217 is a blood biomarker linked to Alzheimer's-related brain changes, measurable from a standard blood draw.
  • A study presented at AAIC 2026 and published in JAMA followed thousands of cognitively healthy older adults across multiple research cohorts.
  • Higher p-tau217 levels tracked with meaningfully higher risk of cognitive decline over both 5 and 10 years of follow-up.
  • This is a research finding, not a diagnosis tool for the public yet. Researchers themselves call it early and not ready for routine use.
  • It builds on funding pushes, including from the Gates-backed Diagnostics Accelerator, aimed at exactly this kind of affordable, early-detection blood test.

One of the more closely watched Alzheimer's stories this summer came out of the Alzheimer's Association International Conference (AAIC) 2026 in London, where researchers presented findings, published simultaneously in JAMA, on a blood biomarker called p-tau217. The pitch is straightforward: a simple blood draw that flags risk of future cognitive decline years, sometimes a decade, before a person or their family would notice anything wrong.

What did the study actually find?

Researchers analyzed data from thousands of cognitively unimpaired older adults, drawn from several research cohorts with an average age around 70, following participants for close to five years on average and, for some, well beyond a decade. Reporting on the findings describes a clear pattern: people with the highest p-tau217 levels faced a substantially elevated risk of developing cognitive impairment over the following 5 to 10 years, compared with those whose levels stayed low. Even more modestly elevated levels carried a noticeably higher risk than the lowest levels, suggesting the relationship isn't an all-or-nothing cutoff so much as a graded risk signal.

Why is a blood test such a big deal here?

Confirming Alzheimer's-related brain changes has historically meant a PET brain scan or a spinal tap, both expensive, invasive or hard to access outside specialty clinics. A blood test that correlates with that same underlying biology could let far more people get an early risk read from a routine visit. That matters because most current and experimental Alzheimer's treatments are thought to work best before extensive brain damage has occurred, which means identifying at-risk people early is arguably as important as any single new drug.

How does this connect to earlier blood-test research?

This finding lands in the middle of a longer push toward affordable, scalable Alzheimer's diagnostics, one that includes philanthropic funding efforts like the Diagnostics Accelerator, co-founded with backing from Bill Gates specifically to speed up development of tests like this. We covered that funding push in our piece on the early Alzheimer's blood test Bill Gates helped fund and in our look at the Diagnostics Accelerator itself. The AAIC/JAMA findings are a distinct, independent study, not a Gates-funded result specifically, but they land squarely in the space that funding was meant to accelerate.

Should you go get this test?

Not on your own initiative, at least not yet. The researchers behind the work are careful to frame this as strong prognostic evidence at a population level, useful for identifying people who might benefit from closer monitoring or enrollment in prevention trials, rather than a settled, individual diagnostic tool ready for routine screening. A high result doesn't guarantee decline, and a low one doesn't guarantee safety. If you have a family history of Alzheimer's or specific concerns, that's a conversation to have with a doctor, who can weigh whether any testing, blood-based or otherwise, makes sense for you.

What this means for you today

Diagnostics like this are moving faster than treatments, which is exactly why the daily habits with strong existing evidence still matter most. Sleep, regular movement, blood pressure and blood sugar control, staying socially connected, and closing obvious nutrient gaps remain the levers you can pull right now, regardless of what a future blood test might someday tell you. A blood test that flags risk a decade early is a genuinely useful research tool. It isn't, yet, something to request at your next checkup on a whim.

Frequently asked questions

What is p-tau217?

p-tau217 is a specific form of the tau protein that shows up in blood in proportion to Alzheimer's-related brain changes. Researchers measure it with a blood draw rather than a spinal tap or brain scan, which makes large-scale screening far more practical.

How accurate is the p-tau217 blood test at predicting decline?

Findings presented at AAIC 2026 and published in JAMA followed cognitively unimpaired older adults across several cohorts and found that higher p-tau217 levels were associated with meaningfully higher risk of cognitive decline over 5 and 10 years, with the highest levels tied to the greatest risk. Researchers describe this as strong prognostic value, not a certainty for any one person.

Can I get this blood test today to find out my risk?

Some p-tau217 tests are becoming available through doctors for diagnostic workups, but researchers are clear that using it purely to predict future risk in healthy people is not yet standard clinical practice. Talk to a doctor about whether testing makes sense for your situation rather than seeking it out on your own.
