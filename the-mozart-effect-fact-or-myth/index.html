The Mozart Effect: Does Classical Music Really Make You Smarter?

One small 1993 study spawned a billion-dollar industry of baby CDs and dorm-room playlists. Here's what researchers actually found, and why the popular version of the story doesn't hold up.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published September 25, 2026

Photo: Oa.coello / Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA 4.0

Key takeaways

  • The 1993 study found a temporary boost on one narrow spatial-reasoning task after listening to Mozart for 10 minutes, lasting roughly 10 to 15 minutes.
  • It never claimed a permanent IQ increase. That claim came from media coverage and marketing, not the research itself.
  • Larger studies mostly failed to replicate a music-specific effect once mood and arousal were controlled for.
  • The same short boost showed up with other engaging audio, like an upbeat story, suggesting arousal and mood explain it better than Mozart specifically.
  • Music can still help with focus and mood while working, but it depends heavily on the music, the task, and the person.

No, listening to Mozart does not make you smarter in any lasting sense. The original 1993 study found a brief, narrow boost in spatial-reasoning performance that lasted about 10 to 15 minutes, not a permanent gain in intelligence. Everything beyond that, the baby CDs, the idea that classical music rewires a developing brain, grew out of media coverage and marketing that went well past what the research actually showed.

What the original 1993 study actually found

Psychologist Frances Rauscher and colleagues had a small group of college students listen to 10 minutes of a Mozart piano sonata, 10 minutes of silence, or 10 minutes of a relaxation tape. Afterward, the students took a set of reasoning tasks drawn from a standard IQ test. The Mozart group scored higher, but only on the spatial-temporal reasoning portion, the kind of task involving mentally folding paper or picturing how shapes fit together. The boost measured a few IQ points on that one subtest and disappeared within roughly a quarter of an hour. The study never tested infants, never measured general intelligence, and never claimed the effect would last.

How a small effect became a giant myth

The gap between what the study showed and what the public heard grew fast. Newspapers ran headlines suggesting classical music makes you smarter, full stop. In 1998, the governor of Georgia arranged for every newborn in the state to receive a classical music CD, citing the supposed brain benefits. A wave of products built entirely around this premise followed, marketed directly at parents who wanted a head start for their kids. None of that had support from the original research, which tested young adults on a narrow, short-lived task, not babies on long-term cognitive development.

Why replications kept coming up empty

Later researchers tried to reproduce the effect, and the picture that emerged was much less exciting. A widely cited meta-analysis pooling data across many studies found the effect size shrinks close to zero once other factors are accounted for. Other researchers found the same short-term boost appeared after students listened to a Stephen King story they found engaging, or their own favorite music, regardless of genre. That pattern points away from anything special about Mozart or classical music and toward something simpler: whatever puts you in a more alert, engaged mood right before a task seems to give a small, temporary edge on that task.

So what's actually going on?

The leading explanation is called the arousal-mood hypothesis. Music you enjoy tends to raise your arousal level and put you in a better mood, and both of those states are independently linked to slightly better performance on certain cognitive tasks right afterward. It's less about Mozart's structure or complexity and more about whatever gets you into an alert, engaged state before you need to think. A favorite pop song can do the same thing for one person that a sonata does for another. Genre isn't the active ingredient. Your response to it is.

Does music actually help you study or work?

It depends, and the honest answer is less tidy than either "yes" or "no." Familiar background music with minimal lyrics tends to support mood without pulling much attention away from reading or writing tasks. New or lyric-heavy music is a different story, since words compete for the same verbal working memory you're using to read or write, and that competition can measurably slow you down. If you're doing something more mechanical, exercise, repetitive work, tasks that don't lean on verbal processing, music seems to interfere far less and can genuinely help with motivation. The Mozart effect was never about Mozart. It was about mood and alertness, dressed up in a composer's name that made for a better headline.

None of this means music has no place in your day. It just means the specific claim, that a particular composer or genre reshapes cognition, isn't what the evidence supports. If you enjoy working with classical music on, keep doing it. Just don't expect it to do more than put you in a slightly better headspace, the same job a favorite playlist can do for someone else. Frequently asked questions

What was the original Mozart effect study?

In 1993, researchers had college students listen to 10 minutes of a Mozart sonata, silence, or a relaxation tape, then tested spatial-reasoning skills. The Mozart group scored temporarily higher on that one task, an effect that faded within about 10 to 15 minutes.

Does listening to Mozart actually raise your IQ?

No. The original study never claimed a lasting IQ increase, only a brief boost on one narrow spatial task. That distinction got lost as media coverage and marketing turned it into a much bigger, permanent claim.

Does playing classical music make babies smarter?

There is no credible evidence for this. The original study tested college students on a specific reasoning task, not infants or general intelligence, and later research found no support for a baby-IQ effect from passive listening.

Why did later studies fail to replicate the Mozart effect?

Meta-analyses found the effect size shrinks close to zero once mood and arousal are accounted for. The same short-term performance boost showed up after listening to an upbeat story or a favorite song, suggesting the effect isn't specific to Mozart or classical music.

Can music still help you focus or study?

It can, depending on the music and the task. Familiar, low-lyric background music tends to support mood without competing much for attention, while unfamiliar or lyric-heavy music is more likely to interfere with reading and writing tasks.
