Chronic Stress and Cognitive Decline: New Study Finds the Pattern Differs by Sex and Ethnicity

A large, long-running study on aging and brain health found chronic stress doesn't affect everyone's thinking skills the same way, and social support only helps with part of it.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published October 1, 2026

Photo: Kwameghana / Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA 4.0

Key takeaways

  • Researchers followed 3,873 adults age 50 and older in the Health and Aging Brain Study-Health Disparities (HABS-HD), a long-running cohort designed to study brain aging across diverse populations.
  • Higher baseline chronic stress tracked with steeper decline in attention and language for women compared with men over the study's follow-up period.
  • Higher baseline chronic stress also tracked with greater memory decline for Hispanic participants compared with non-Hispanic white participants.
  • More social support was linked to less decline in attention, but it didn't erase the other stress-related effects the researchers found.
  • This is observational data, not a controlled trial, so it shows an association rather than proof that stress itself causes these differences.

A study published in the journal Alzheimer's & Dementia is adding detail to a question researchers have circled for years: does chronic stress affect everyone's brain aging the same way? The answer from this new analysis is no. The pattern of decline tied to stress looked different depending on sex and ethnicity, and social support helped with only part of the picture.

What did the researchers actually study?

The team, led by researchers including Jillian K. Lee and colleagues at Wake Forest University School of Medicine, drew on data from the Health and Aging Brain Study-Health Disparities, known as HABS-HD, a cohort built specifically to study brain aging across a demographically diverse population. The analysis included 3,873 adults age 50 and older, with an average age of about 65, who were tracked over time with repeated cognitive testing covering attention, memory, executive function and language.

Using statistical models built to handle repeated measurements over years, the researchers looked at how each person's baseline level of chronic stress and baseline social support related to changes in these specific cognitive domains, rather than treating thinking skills as one single score.

What did they find about stress and sex?

Women with higher baseline chronic stress showed steeper declines in attention and language over the follow-up period than men with similarly high stress. That's a domain-specific finding. It doesn't mean women's cognition declined faster across the board, it means these two particular skill areas tracked more closely with stress levels in women than in men in this sample.

What did they find about stress and ethnicity?

The study also found that higher baseline chronic stress was associated with greater memory decline specifically among Hispanic participants compared with non-Hispanic white participants. Again, this was domain-specific. The effect showed up in memory rather than uniformly across every cognitive measure tested.

Health disparities researchers have long pointed out that stress exposure, including the chronic, cumulative kind tied to discrimination, financial strain or caregiving burden, isn't distributed evenly across populations. A finding like this one adds a specific, measured data point to that broader conversation rather than settling it.

Does social support cancel out the effect of stress?

Partly, and only in one domain. Higher baseline social support was linked to less decline in attention. It did not meaningfully offset the stress-related declines the study found in language or memory. That's a more nuanced picture than the common assumption that a strong support network simply buffers all the cognitive effects of a hard life. Here, support looks like it helped with one specific piece of the puzzle, not the whole thing.

The researchers also reported that stress and social support acted independently of each other in their models. In plain terms, having strong support didn't change how much chronic stress a person reported, and the two factors seemed to influence cognitive scores through separate pathways rather than one simply canceling out the other.

Why does a cohort like HABS-HD matter here?

A lot of long-running brain-aging research has historically leaned heavily on participants who are white, well-educated and relatively affluent, which limits how much any single finding can be generalized. HABS-HD was built specifically to include a more demographically diverse group of older adults, including larger numbers of Hispanic and Mexican American participants than most comparable studies. That design is exactly what made a finding like the ethnicity-specific memory pattern possible to detect in the first place. A less diverse sample simply wouldn't have had enough participants in that subgroup to analyze with any statistical confidence.

How strong is this kind of evidence?

This is an observational, longitudinal study. Researchers measured stress and social support once at the start and followed participants' cognitive scores over subsequent visits, rather than randomly assigning people to different stress or support conditions, which obviously isn't possible to do ethically. That design can reveal real associations and is genuinely useful for spotting patterns worth investigating further. It can't prove that stress itself directly causes the differences in decline, since people with higher chronic stress may also differ in other ways, health conditions, access to care, sleep quality, that weren't fully captured by the model.

The findings also come from one cohort. Replication in other diverse, long-running studies would strengthen confidence that this specific pattern, women and attention or language, Hispanic participants and memory, holds up broadly rather than reflecting something particular to this sample. The study also measured chronic stress and social support once, at the start, rather than tracking how either changed over the years that followed, so it can't say whether a change in someone's stress level partway through would have shifted their cognitive trajectory.

What this means for you

If you're managing significant chronic stress, whether from caregiving, financial pressure or something else, this study is one more reason to take that seriously as a brain-health issue, not only a mental-health one. It's not a reason to assume decline is inevitable. Stress management, strong relationships and medical support for the specific pressures in your life are reasonable responses regardless of which cognitive domain a given study happens to flag. Frequently asked questions

What did the new chronic stress study find?

Researchers following 3,873 adults age 50 and older found that higher baseline chronic stress was linked to steeper decline in attention and language for women compared with men, and to greater memory decline for Hispanic participants compared with non-Hispanic white participants.

Does social support protect against stress-related cognitive decline?

Partly. Higher social support was linked to less decline in attention specifically, but it didn't offset the stress-related declines found in language or memory in this study.

Is this study proof that stress causes cognitive decline?

No. It's an observational, longitudinal study that found associations, not a controlled trial. It can't rule out other factors that might explain the patterns researchers found.

Where was this research published?

In the journal Alzheimer's & Dementia, based on data from the Health and Aging Brain Study-Health Disparities (HABS-HD), led by researchers including Jillian K. Lee at Wake Forest University School of Medicine.

What should I do with this information?

Treat ongoing, significant stress as a brain-health concern worth addressing, through medical support, stress management or strengthening relationships, rather than something to wait out. It's not a reason to assume decline is inevitable.
