Does Journaling Improve Memory? What the Evidence Shows

Writing things down feels like it should help you remember them. Here's what research on expressive writing, note-taking and handwriting actually says.

By Sarah Coleman, Health Editor  ·  Reviewed by Dr. Marcus Reed, MD  ·  Published August 4, 2026

Key takeaways

  • No single study nails it down, but the mechanics of journaling line up with how memory forms.
  • Handwriting may beat typing for retention, since it forces slower, more selective processing.
  • Recall-style journaling works better than a plain activity log, because it practices retrieval.
  • Expressive writing lowers stress, which indirectly protects the hippocampus.
  • Rereading old entries is itself a form of spaced retrieval practice.

Journaling probably won't sharpen your memory the way a good night's sleep or a brisk walk will, but the short answer is that it can help, and the reasons are more interesting than "writing is good for you." Putting a day, a lesson or a worry into words forces your brain to select, compress and re-encode it, which is close to what a good study technique does on purpose. It's not a miracle fix. It's a habit with a plausible mechanism and some supportive evidence behind it.

What does journaling actually do for memory?

Memory researchers talk about the "generation effect": information you produce yourself sticks better than information you just read. Journaling is generation. When you write about your day, you're not copying it, you're reconstructing it, deciding what mattered and putting it in your own words. That reconstruction is a form of retrieval practice, the same principle behind flashcards and practice tests. For a deeper look at why retrieval beats rereading, see our guide to active recall.

Is handwriting better than typing for memory?

Several studies on note-taking suggest yes, at least for retaining ideas rather than just facts. Students who write notes by hand tend to remember concepts better than those who type, and one common explanation is that handwriting is slower, so it forces you to summarize and prioritize instead of transcribing word for word. Typing is fast enough to copy almost verbatim, which skips the mental work that helps memory stick. If you journal on a laptop or phone, that's still better than not journaling. But if you're journaling specifically to remember something, pen and paper have a real edge.

What's the best way to journal for memory?

A plain log of what happened today, meetings, errands, meals, doesn't ask much of your brain and won't do much for recall. Journaling that leans on memory works harder: write what you learned, not just what you did. Summarize a book chapter or a work project in your own words before checking your notes. Ask yourself what you'd want to remember about today a year from now, and write toward that. Even five minutes of this kind of reflective writing engages more of the encoding process than a longer, purely descriptive entry.

Journaling helps memory less because of the paper and more because of the thinking it forces. Reconstructing an idea in your own words is a memory exercise wearing a diary's clothes.

Does gratitude journaling help memory too?

Gratitude journaling has the best evidence for mood and stress reduction, not memory directly. That still matters here. Chronic stress raises cortisol, and cortisol is well documented to interfere with the hippocampus, the brain region most involved in forming new memories. So lowering stress through gratitude writing could support memory indirectly, even without a direct mechanism of its own. It's worth pairing with a more recall-focused entry rather than relying on it alone. Our piece on how stress affects memory covers that pathway in more depth.

Can rereading old journal entries help you remember more?

Yes, and this part has solid backing. Spaced repetition, reviewing information again after time has passed, is one of the best-studied ways to move something into long-term memory. Flipping back through old entries a week or a month later functions as an unplanned spaced-review session. You're retrieving the memory again, which strengthens it further, especially if you try to recall the details before rereading them. For more on why spacing works, see our explainer on spaced repetition.

What does the research still not know?

Nobody has run a large trial that puts "journaling" and "memory scores" head to head the way sleep or exercise studies have. Most of what supports journaling for memory comes from adjacent research: studies on writing and encoding, on handwriting versus typing, and on stress and the hippocampus. It's reasonable evidence, but it's indirect. Treat journaling as a low-risk, plausible habit worth trying, not a proven memory treatment on its own.

Frequently asked questions

Does journaling actually improve memory?

There's no single study proving journaling boosts memory the way a nutrient or a night of sleep might. But the mechanics of writing, the deliberate encoding, the freed-up mental bandwidth, and the retrieval practice built into rereading old entries, all line up with how memory actually forms and holds.

Is handwriting better than typing for memory?

Several studies suggest people who write by hand, especially students taking notes, retain material better than those who type it, possibly because handwriting is slower and forces more selective, effortful processing. Typing tends to invite verbatim copying, which engages memory less.

What's the best type of journaling for memory?

For memory specifically, journaling that involves recall and reflection, summarizing your day, writing what you learned, or working through a problem, tends to be more useful than a simple activity log, because it forces retrieval rather than passive recording.

Does gratitude journaling help with memory too?

Gratitude journaling is best supported for mood and stress, not memory directly. Since chronic stress is known to impair the hippocampus, lowering stress could support memory indirectly, but that's a step removed from a direct memory benefit.
