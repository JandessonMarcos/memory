MemoHoney vs Prevagen: Blood-Flow Formula vs Single-Ingredient Pill

Both are one capsule a day. That's about where the similarity ends. One bets on circulation with twelve ingredients, the other on a single protein, and each has a different weak spot.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published October 25, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Different bets entirely. MemoHoney targets cerebral blood flow with nitric oxide amino acids, ginkgo and bacopa. Prevagen relies on one ingredient, apoaequorin, a protein originally found in jellyfish.
  • Prevagen has a long legal history. The FTC and New York Attorney General sued its maker in 2017 over memory claims, and in 2024 a federal court ordered it to stop making the challenged claims.
  • MemoHoney's weak spot is disclosure. Seven of its twelve ingredients have printed doses, but ginkgo and bacopa, the ones with real memory research, don't.
  • Buying is different too. Prevagen sits on pharmacy shelves under the store's return policy. MemoHoney is sold direct, with a 60-day guarantee that has strict return terms.
  • MemoHoney carries more interaction flags. Blood thinners, blood pressure drugs and nitrates all need a doctor's check first.

In the MemoHoney vs Prevagen matchup, MemoHoney is the broader formula: twelve ingredients built around blood flow to the brain, with seven doses printed. Prevagen is a single protein, apoaequorin, whose memory claims were challenged by federal and state regulators. Neither has a published trial on the finished product that we'd call convincing for healthy adults.

That's the short version. The longer one matters, because these two products fail in different ways and suit different buyers. Below we put them side by side on formula, evidence, safety, price and buying experience, then say plainly who should pick which, and who should skip both.

What's the main difference between MemoHoney and Prevagen?

It comes down to mechanism. MemoHoney is organized around one question: can better blood flow to the brain help cognition? Four of its seven dosed ingredients (L-arginine, L-arginine AKG, L-citrulline HCl and L-citrulline malate) feed nitric oxide, which widens blood vessels. Ginkgo, the classic circulation botanical, sits alongside them, together with bacopa, astaxanthin, blueberry and a little honey.

Prevagen takes the opposite approach. One active ingredient, apoaequorin. It's a calcium-binding protein first identified in a glowing jellyfish, and the marketing story is that supporting calcium regulation in brain cells supports memory. Everything rides on that single idea.

Both are one capsule a day. Both are stimulant-free. After that, they share very little.

What is Prevagen, and what happened with the FTC?

Prevagen is made by Quincy Bioscience and is one of the most heavily advertised memory products in the US. You'll find it at most large pharmacies, in more than one strength, with the apoaequorin amount printed on the box.

In January 2017, the Federal Trade Commission and the New York Attorney General sued the company over its marketing. The agencies alleged that claims such as "clinically shown" to improve memory weren't backed by adequate evidence. According to their complaint, the company's own study failed to show that Prevagen worked better than a placebo on any measure of cognitive function across the full study group, and its safety research showed apoaequorin is broken down in the digestive tract like other dietary proteins.

The company disputed the case, and it ran for seven years, including an appeal and a jury trial. In December 2024, the FTC announced that the federal court in New York had ordered Quincy Bioscience to stop making the claims challenged in the lawsuit. Prevagen remains on sale.

To be fair about what that means: the case was about advertising claims and the evidence behind them. It wasn't a finding that the product is dangerous. As we wrote in our look at Prevagen alternatives, our position is that it's an expensive, narrow bet rather than a fraud.

What is MemoHoney, and what's actually on the label?

MemoHoney is a once-daily, stimulant-free capsule, 30 to a bottle, sold direct from the seller's site and made in the US in an FDA-registered facility that follows GMP standards. Despite the name, honey is a minor, undosed ingredient low on the list. The name is branding.

The printed doses per capsule are L-arginine 200 mg, L-arginine AKG 200 mg, L-citrulline HCl 100 mg, L-citrulline malate 100 mg, beta-alanine 40 mg, niacin 7.5 mg and calcium 18 mg. That's roughly 665 mg of disclosed material. Ginkgo, bacopa, astaxanthin, blueberry and honey share whatever room is left in the capsule.

Our complete MemoHoney review goes through each of those against the doses used in research. The short summary: the nitric oxide group adds up to 600 mg, against several grams in circulation studies, and beta-alanine at 40 mg doesn't have a memory rationale. The thesis is sound. The execution is partial.

Which has better evidence for memory?

Neither product has convincing published evidence as a finished formula, so this question is really about the ingredients. And evidence for an ingredient isn't evidence for a product.

On ingredients, MemoHoney has the stronger hand. Bacopa has the most consistent memory-specific trial record of any supplement botanical, with modest gains in delayed recall after 8 to 12 weeks (our bacopa monnieri guide covers those trials). Ginkgo has decades of research on blood flow, with mixed results for memory in healthy people. The catch is that you can't tell whether either is present at a studied amount.

Apoaequorin has a much thinner record. The main human study was funded by the maker, and that's the study regulators argued didn't support the advertising. Independent replication is limited. There's also the biological question the regulators raised: a protein swallowed by mouth is normally digested into amino acids, which makes it hard to see how it would reach the brain intact.

So the honest scoring is this. MemoHoney: better ingredients, unverifiable doses. Prevagen: verifiable dose, weak case for the ingredient itself.

Which is safer to take?

For most healthy adults, both are well tolerated. The differences show up if you take medication.

MemoHoney's circulation design is also its main safety caution. Ginkgo has a documented interaction with blood thinners and antiplatelet drugs such as warfarin, aspirin or clopidogrel. The nitric oxide ingredients act on the same vessel-widening pathway as blood pressure medication, nitrates like nitroglycerin and PDE5 inhibitors like sildenafil. If any of those apply to you, talk to your doctor before starting. The same goes for a bleeding disorder, surgery within two weeks, pregnancy or breastfeeding. Niacin at 7.5 mg is low, so flushing is uncommon.

Prevagen's single protein gives it fewer obvious interaction flags on paper. That isn't the same as proven safe alongside every drug, so a two-minute check with your pharmacist is still worth it.

Is it easier to buy and return Prevagen or MemoHoney?

Prevagen wins on convenience. You can pick it up with your groceries, and returns follow the store's policy, whatever that happens to be. There's no special process to learn.

MemoHoney is sold only through its official site. That keeps the multi-bottle pricing and lets the seller run its own 60-day guarantee, but the terms are stricter than the marketing suggests: you request a refund by email, return all bottles including empties, and pay the return shipping. Processing takes roughly 5 to 10 business days after they receive the package, and some versions of the terms ask for 30 days of use first. It's a real guarantee. It just isn't a no-effort one.

Who should choose MemoHoney, and who should choose Prevagen?

MemoHoney makes more sense if:

  • the blood-flow idea appeals to you and you'd rather have ginkgo and bacopa in the mix than a single protein
  • a once-a-day routine is what you'll actually stick with for three months
  • you don't take blood thinners, blood pressure drugs or nitrates, or your doctor has cleared it
  • you're comfortable with a direct-to-consumer purchase and its return process

Prevagen makes more sense if you strongly prefer buying at a pharmacy, value a fully printed single dose, and have read the regulatory history and still want to try it. We'd point most people elsewhere, but it's your call.

Skip both if your memory has changed suddenly or is getting worse. That's a medical appointment. And if what you want is a research-level ginkgo dose, a standalone ginkgo product gives you exactly that, more cheaply. Our guide to ginkgo biloba for memory explains what it can and can't do.

How to test whichever one you pick

Comparisons only go so far. The real test is what happens in your own life over a few months, and memory is the one faculty that's bad at judging itself. Set the trial up so the answer is clear either way:

  • Get a baseline before the first capsule. A short, repeatable test beats a vague feeling. Write the score down with the date.
  • Change one thing at a time. If you start a new supplement, a new sleep routine and a new exercise plan in the same week, you won't know which one helped.
  • Keep a two-line weekly note. Names forgotten, words that wouldn't come, how the afternoons felt. It's more reliable than trying to recall a month later.
  • Don't judge in week two. Anything you feel that early is more likely normal day-to-day variation or expectation. The botanicals in MemoHoney need 8 to 12 weeks.
  • Mark the refund date. With MemoHoney, the 60-day window closes before the 12-week mark, so decide around week seven. With Prevagen, check the store's return limit on the day you buy, because some are much shorter.

If twelve weeks of steady use produces nothing you can point to, stop. That's money saved on month four, and it frees you to try something with a different mechanism instead of doubling down on one that isn't doing anything for you.

Frequently asked questions

Can I take MemoHoney and Prevagen together?

There's no good reason to, and we don't recommend stacking memory products you haven't tested on their own. If you do consider it, ask your doctor or pharmacist first, especially if you take blood thinners, blood pressure medication or nitrates, because of MemoHoney's ginkgo and nitric oxide ingredients.

Is Prevagen still sold after the FTC case?

Yes. The court order announced by the FTC in December 2024 required the maker to stop making the specific claims challenged in the lawsuit. It didn't take the product off the market, and you'll still find it in most large pharmacies.

Does MemoHoney contain apoaequorin?

No. MemoHoney's twelve ingredients are L-arginine, L-arginine AKG, L-citrulline HCl, L-citrulline malate, beta-alanine, niacin, calcium, ginkgo biloba, bacopa monnieri, astaxanthin, blueberry and honey. There's no apoaequorin or jellyfish-derived protein in it.

Is Prevagen made from jellyfish?

Apoaequorin was first identified in a bioluminescent jellyfish. Supplement makers generally produce it in a lab rather than harvesting jellyfish. The point regulators raised wasn't the source but whether a protein taken by mouth survives digestion intact.

Which one works faster?

Neither should be expected to work quickly. MemoHoney's nitric oxide ingredients act within hours if at all, but at these doses most people won't notice them. Its ginkgo and bacopa need 8 to 12 weeks. Plan on about three months before judging any memory supplement.

Is MemoHoney approved by the FDA?

No dietary supplement is FDA approved. MemoHoney is made in an FDA-registered facility that follows Good Manufacturing Practice standards, which describes the factory. It isn't approval of the product or its effects.

What if neither one helps?

That's a useful answer, not a failure. Sleep, exercise, blood pressure control and hearing care have stronger evidence for memory than any capsule. If your memory is getting worse, see a doctor, since several common causes like thyroid problems or B12 deficiency are treatable.
