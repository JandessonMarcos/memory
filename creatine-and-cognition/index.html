Creatine and Cognition: Who Actually Gets a Mental Benefit?

Averaged across everyone, creatine's effect on thinking looks modest. Split the data by who's taking it, and a much clearer picture appears.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published August 25, 2026

Key takeaways

  • The average effect is small. Pooled across healthy, well-fed adults, creatine moves memory scores only slightly.
  • Baseline stores predict response. The less creatine you already carry, the more supplementing tends to do.
  • Vegetarians and vegans respond most, because diet is the main source and theirs supplies almost none.
  • Stress states matter. Benefits show up more consistently when the brain is taxed by sleep loss or mental fatigue.
  • 3–5 g daily is the standard dose, and the brain saturates more slowly than muscle does.

Ask whether creatine helps memory and you'll get two confident, opposite answers. Both are drawing on real data. The disagreement comes from averaging: creatine's cognitive effect depends heavily on who is taking it, and pooling everyone together buries that. Our guide to creatine for brain health covers the mechanism and dosing basics. This piece looks at a narrower question, and a more useful one. Who actually notices anything?

What creatine does inside a brain cell

Your brain runs on ATP, and it burns through it fast. Creatine works as a rechargeable buffer: stored as phosphocreatine, it donates a phosphate group to regenerate ATP within seconds, faster than your cells can make more from scratch. Neurons lean on this buffer hardest during bursts of demand. Think effortful reasoning, holding several things in mind at once, or pushing through when you're already depleted.

That framing explains the pattern in the research. If your buffer is already full and you're rested, topping it up further does very little. If the buffer is low, or demand has outstripped supply, there's room for supplementation to matter.

Why the average result looks unimpressive

Most trials recruit healthy young adults who eat meat. That group starts with near-full stores. They also tend to be tested while rested, on tasks they find manageable. Every one of those choices shrinks the measurable effect. Systematic reviews in this area generally report small average improvements in memory, with wide variation between studies and clearer signals for short-term and working memory than for other domains.

Small average, wide spread. That combination usually means the group contains both responders and non-responders, and the interesting work is figuring out which is which.

Vegetarians and vegans: the clearest responders

Creatine comes from two places: your body makes some in the liver and kidneys, and you eat the rest in meat and fish. Remove animal products and one of those two sources disappears. Vegetarians and vegans consistently show lower baseline muscle and, by extension, brain creatine than omnivores.

Studies that separate vegetarians from meat-eaters have generally found larger cognitive responses in the vegetarian groups. This is the single most reliable predictor of who benefits. If you don't eat meat and you're curious about creatine, you're in the group with the most to gain.

Sleep deprivation and mental fatigue

The second pattern involves stressing the system. Under sleep restriction, the brain's energy economy tightens and cognitive performance drops. Several studies have tested creatine specifically in that state, and results there have looked more favourable than in rested participants. Some work suggests a single larger dose may blunt part of the deficit after a night of poor sleep, though these are small studies and the finding needs replication before anyone should rely on it.

Treat this as promising rather than settled. It's a reasonable explanation for why some people swear creatine sharpens them up while others notice nothing at all. If you're chronically short on sleep, the more valuable intervention is still the sleep itself. See what happens to recall when sleep and memory come apart.

Older adults

Ageing brings a gradual decline in tissue creatine alongside reduced mitochondrial efficiency. In principle that makes older adults another low-baseline group, and some trials have reported cognitive improvements in this population. The evidence here is thinner and less consistent than the vegetarian data. Worth watching, not yet worth promising.

Dose, and why the brain lags behind muscle

The standard dose is 3 to 5 grams of creatine monohydrate daily. Muscle saturates within a few weeks at that rate. Brain tissue takes longer, because creatine crosses the blood-brain barrier slowly and the brain also synthesises some of its own. Studies looking specifically at brain creatine levels have often used higher doses or longer timelines to see clear changes.

Practically, that means patience. If you're trying creatine for cognitive reasons, judging it after a week isn't a fair test. Give it a couple of months of consistent daily use before deciding.

Safety and who should check first

Creatine monohydrate is among the most heavily studied supplements available, with a long track record in healthy adults. The most common effect is a small weight gain from water drawn into muscle. Some people report stomach upset at higher single doses, which usually resolves by splitting the dose or taking it with food.

If you have kidney disease or reduced kidney function, talk to your doctor before starting. The concern is mostly theoretical in healthy people, but it's a genuine caution in anyone whose kidneys are already under strain. Same advice applies if you're pregnant, breastfeeding, or taking medication that affects kidney function.

How it fits alongside other memory nutrients

Creatine addresses cellular energy supply. That's a different job from what citicoline does for acetylcholine production, or what omega-3s do for membrane structure. They aren't competing options so much as different parts of the same system, which is why combination formulas exist. One caveat worth stating plainly. Creatine rarely appears in memory blends at a meaningful dose, because 3 to 5 grams is bulky and most capsules can't carry it. If creatine is what you want, buy it separately as monohydrate powder. It's cheap, and the ingredient is well characterised.

So should you take it?

If you're vegetarian or vegan, the case is reasonable and the downside is minimal. If you're an omnivore who sleeps well, expect little from it cognitively, though the training benefits are a separate and better-established matter. If you're chronically sleep-deprived, fix the sleep first and treat creatine as a possible partial buffer, not a solution.

None of this replaces the habits that move memory most. Sleep, movement and diet still do the heavy lifting, and our guide to how to improve your memory ranks them by what the evidence supports.

Creatine's cognitive effect isn't small or large in the abstract. It depends almost entirely on how much you were carrying before you started.

Frequently asked questions

Does creatine actually improve memory?

On average the effect in healthy, well-fed adults is small. It becomes more noticeable in people with low baseline creatine stores, particularly vegetarians and vegans, and in states of sleep deprivation or mental fatigue. Effects on short-term and working memory appear more consistently than effects on other cognitive domains.

How long does creatine take to affect the brain?

Longer than it takes to affect muscle. Brain creatine rises slowly because the compound crosses the blood-brain barrier at a limited rate. Give consistent daily supplementation at least four to eight weeks before judging whether it does anything for you.

Do vegetarians benefit more from creatine?

Yes, and this is the most consistent finding in the area. Meat and fish are the main dietary sources, so vegetarians and vegans start with lower stores. Studies that separate them from omnivores generally report larger cognitive responses in the vegetarian groups.

Is creatine safe to take every day?

For healthy adults, creatine monohydrate has a long safety record at 3 to 5 grams daily. Mild water-weight gain is common and stomach upset occurs in some people at larger single doses. Anyone with kidney disease or reduced kidney function should speak to a doctor first.

How much creatine should I take for cognitive benefits?

Most research uses 3 to 5 grams of creatine monohydrate per day. Some brain-focused studies have used higher amounts to shift brain levels measurably, but those doses raise the chance of stomach upset and are not necessary for most people.
