Protein Intake and Brain Aging: How Much Do You Need?

Protein gets talked about for muscle, not memory. But the amount you eat as you get older may matter more for your brain than most people realize.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published August 5, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Older adults generally need more protein than the standard RDA, not less, to hold onto muscle and metabolic health.
  • Protein doesn't feed the brain directly the way glucose does, but it shapes the conditions your brain depends on.
  • Spreading protein across meals works better than one large serving at dinner.
  • Both animal and plant sources can meet the target; variety and total amount matter more than picking a side.
  • Pairing protein with movement is what actually protects both muscle and cognitive function long-term.

Most people don't think about protein until a doctor mentions muscle loss, but the amount you eat as you age has quieter effects too, ones that reach the brain by a roundabout path. The short answer: adults over roughly 65 usually need more protein than younger adults, not less, and getting too little is linked to the kind of fatigue, poor blood sugar control and physical decline that make thinking feel harder. Protein won't sharpen memory on its own. It supports the systems that keep your brain running well.

Why does protein matter for an aging brain?

The brain itself runs mostly on glucose, so protein isn't fuel for thought in any direct sense. Its role is more structural. Protein maintains muscle mass, which affects blood sugar regulation and mobility. It supports the production of neurotransmitters, since several, including dopamine and serotonin, are built from amino acids you get through food. And it helps the body repair tissue and manage inflammation, both of which have downstream ties to brain health. Cut protein too low, and those supporting systems start to wobble first, well before anyone notices a change in memory.

How much protein do you actually need as you get older?

The official U.S. recommended dietary allowance, 0.8 grams per kilogram of body weight, was set to prevent deficiency in the general population, not to optimize aging. Many geriatric nutrition researchers argue that older adults do better closer to 1.0 to 1.2 grams per kilogram, and some go higher for anyone recovering from illness or surgery. For a 150-pound adult, that works out to roughly 68 to 82 grams a day. It sounds like a lot until you break it down: a palm-sized piece of chicken, a cup of Greek yogurt and a serving of lentils can get most people close on their own.

What happens when you don't get enough?

Chronic low protein intake accelerates sarcopenia, the age-related loss of muscle mass and strength. Less muscle means worse blood sugar control, since muscle tissue is where a large share of glucose gets absorbed after a meal. Poor glucose regulation, in turn, has been linked in research to a higher risk of cognitive decline over time. There's also a simpler, more immediate effect: low protein meals leave blood sugar and energy less stable through the day, and that instability shows up as afternoon fog and trouble concentrating, even in people who aren't clinically deficient.

Which protein sources are best for brain health?

No single food is required. Fatty fish like salmon does double duty, supplying protein alongside omega-3s that are separately linked to brain health, which is worth reading about in our guide to omega-3 and brain health. Eggs contribute choline, a nutrient tied to memory and covered in our piece on choline and brain function. Legumes, tofu and dairy round things out with fiber or calcium on the side. The pattern that shows up again and again in nutrition research isn't one hero food. It's a diet built mostly from whole, minimally processed sources, similar to what we cover in our Mediterranean diet and memory guide.

Is animal protein or plant protein better for brain aging?

Animal protein is "complete," meaning it contains all nine essential amino acids in one serving, so you generally need less volume to hit your target. Plant protein, from beans, lentils, tofu and whole grains, usually requires combining a couple of sources across the day, but it arrives with fiber, plant compounds and less saturated fat, which matters for the blood vessels that feed the brain. Neither approach has a clear cognitive edge over the other in the research so far. Most dietitians land on the same practical advice: eat a mix, and don't overthink the split.

Can you get too much protein?

For most healthy adults, moderately high protein intake looks safe based on current evidence, and there's no solid data suggesting it harms memory. The caveats matter, though. People with reduced kidney function need a doctor's guidance, since damaged kidneys process protein waste less efficiently. And piling protein onto a plate at the expense of vegetables and healthy fats isn't a win either; balance still counts. Doubling your intake isn't going to double the benefit. It's a floor to hit, not a ceiling to chase.

How does protein work with exercise to protect the brain?

Protein and movement reinforce each other. Resistance exercise creates the stimulus for muscle repair, and protein supplies the raw material to rebuild it; without enough of either, the other doesn't do much. That combination also supports the kind of metabolic and cardiovascular health that shows up repeatedly in research on cognitive aging. If you're looking for where to start, our guide to exercise and brain health covers the movement side in more depth, and pairing it with steady protein intake through the day is the piece people tend to skip.

Protein isn't a brain nutrient in the way omega-3 or B12 are talked about. It's closer to scaffolding, quietly holding up the muscle, blood sugar control and repair systems your brain depends on.

Frequently asked questions

How much protein do older adults need for brain and muscle health?

General guidance for healthy older adults runs higher than the standard RDA, often in the range of about 1.0 to 1.2 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day, spread across meals rather than loaded into one. Anyone with kidney disease or another condition affecting protein metabolism should get a personalized target from their doctor.

Can too little protein affect memory and thinking?

Not directly in most cases, but low protein intake contributes to muscle loss, fatigue and poor blood sugar control, all of which can leave you feeling foggy and less sharp. It's more of an indirect drag on cognitive energy than a direct hit to memory circuits.

Is animal or plant protein better for brain aging?

Both can work well. Animal protein delivers a complete amino acid profile in a smaller serving, while plant sources like legumes and soy bring fiber and polyphenols that also support cardiovascular and brain health. A mix of both is a reasonable, well-supported approach.

Can eating too much protein cause any harm?

For most healthy adults, moderately higher protein intake appears safe, but very high intakes sustained over years haven't been studied enough to call risk-free, and people with reduced kidney function should be cautious. More isn't automatically better past a certain point.
