How to Take Memopezil: Dosage, Timing and With or Without Food

The serving size is printed on the bottle. The two decisions that matter more than the number are when you take it and what you take it with.

By Sarah Coleman, Health Editor  ·  Reviewed by Dr. Marcus Reed, MD  ·  Published August 31, 2026

Key takeaways

  • The serving is two capsules a day. A 60-capsule bottle is 30 days.
  • Take it in the morning. Two of the five ingredients are stimulating and will cost you sleep if taken late.
  • Take it with food. Bacopa on an empty stomach is the main cause of the queasiness people report.
  • Give it 12 weeks before judging. Bacopa's evidence window is 8 to 12 weeks, and the guarantee runs out at about 8.5.
  • A missed day doesn't matter. Missing three weeks does, because the main ingredient works by accumulating.

The dosing instruction is simple: two capsules per day. A bottle holds 60 capsules, which is where the 30-day supply comes from. That's the whole of the official guidance, and it leaves the two questions that actually affect your result unanswered.

Those questions are when to take it and whether to take it with food. Both have clear answers, and both come from the behaviour of the individual ingredients rather than from anything printed on the bottle.

The short answer

Take both capsules in the morning, with a meal, every day, for at least twelve weeks. If you only read one line on this page, that's it. The reasoning follows. Everything below is the reasoning behind each part of that sentence, and the places where getting it wrong quietly costs you the result you paid for.

Why morning, and why it matters more than it sounds

Two of the five ingredients are stimulating. Rhodiola rosea and panax ginseng both have a mild activating effect, and both are associated with sleep disruption when taken later in the day. Ginseng-related insomnia is documented well enough to be a standard caution.

This creates a trap that's easy to fall into. You buy a memory supplement partly because your afternoons feel foggy. So an afternoon dose feels like the sensible choice, aimed at the problem. Then you sleep badly, and the next afternoon is worse, and you take it again.

Sleep is not a side issue for memory. Consolidation, the process that moves what you learned during the day into durable storage, happens during sleep. A supplement that buys you alertness at three in the afternoon while costing you an hour at night is running a net loss on the exact outcome you're paying for.

L-theanine works in the other direction, which is why some people take theanine alone at night. In this formula it's outnumbered. Morning dosing is the right call.

Why with food, not on an empty stomach

Bacopa monnieri is hard on an empty stomach for a meaningful minority of people. Cramping, nausea and loose stools are the usual reports, and they show up consistently enough that clinical trials routinely instruct participants to dose with a meal.

There's a second reason beyond comfort. Bacosides, the compounds bacopa is standardised for, are better absorbed alongside dietary fat. Taking the capsules with a breakfast that contains some fat gives you both a settled stomach and better uptake. Eggs, yoghurt, nuts, avocado on toast, any of it works. You don't need a large meal, just not a black coffee.

If you've been taking it dry first thing and feeling off by mid-morning, change that before you decide the product doesn't agree with you. It's the single most common fixable complaint in this category.

Should you split the two capsules?

Some people prefer to take one capsule at breakfast and one at lunch. That's reasonable, with one caveat: keep the second one early enough that the stimulating ingredients aren't still working at bedtime. Lunch is fine. Four in the afternoon is pushing it.

Splitting has a modest argument in its favour for L-theanine, which acts acutely and clears within hours, so two smaller doses spread its effect. It has no real argument for bacopa, which accumulates over weeks and doesn't care how you distribute a daily amount.

If a single morning dose upsets your stomach even with food, splitting is worth trying before giving up on it.

What if you miss a day?

Nothing happens. Take the next dose as normal and don't double up.

This is worth stating plainly because supplement marketing often implies a fragile process that collapses if you slip. It doesn't. Bacopa's effect builds over weeks of regular intake, so what matters is the pattern across a month, not any individual day. Missing one day in thirty is irrelevant. Missing twenty is a different product.

The practical risk isn't the occasional gap, it's quiet abandonment. Most people who buy a twelve-week supplement stop somewhere in week three, usually because nothing has happened yet and nothing was supposed to. Put the bottle where you already do something daily, next to the kettle or the toothbrush, and the problem largely solves itself.

How long before you judge it

Twelve weeks. That number comes from bacopa, the ingredient with the strongest memory evidence in the formula, and it's not negotiable if you want a fair test. The trials that found effects on delayed recall ran 8 to 12 weeks. The shorter ones mostly found nothing, which tells you the effect needs time to appear rather than that it isn't there.

You may notice something earlier from theanine, and possibly from rhodiola on the fatigue side within a couple of weeks. Don't mistake either for the memory effect. They're different ingredients doing different jobs on different timescales.

There's an awkward tension here worth planning around. The money-back guarantee runs 60 days, which is roughly 8.5 weeks. Bacopa's evidence window extends past that. If keeping the refund option matters to you, you'll be making a decision at around week eight with incomplete information, and you should know that going in rather than discover it on day 61.

How to actually test it on yourself

Twelve weeks is a long time to rely on an impression. Memory is exactly the faculty you can't use to judge whether your memory improved, and expectation does a lot of work when you've spent money on something.

A rough baseline fixes most of that. Before your first capsule, write down three or four specific, recurring situations where your memory annoys you. Not "I'm forgetful", which is unmeasurable. Something closer to: I lose the thread mid-sentence in meetings, I can't retrieve names of people I met last week, I walk into rooms and forget the errand, I reread the same paragraph twice.

Then score each one weekly, roughly, on a scale you invent. The number itself doesn't matter. What matters is that you wrote it down in week one, before you had any stake in the answer, and that you're comparing like with like in week twelve instead of comparing today against a memory of how bad things used to feel.

Hold the obvious confounders steady while you do it. Don't start the supplement in the same week you start a new exercise routine, change your caffeine intake, or begin fixing your sleep. All three of those have better evidence for memory than anything in a capsule, and if you change everything at once you'll never know which one did the work. If you can only do one thing, fix the sleep and skip the supplement.

Mistakes people make in the first month

  • Judging it in week two. The main ingredient hasn't done anything by then, in any published trial. This is the most common reason people conclude a bacopa product doesn't work.
  • Taking it dry, first thing. Then blaming the formula for the nausea that bacopa on an empty stomach reliably produces.
  • Doubling up after a missed day. Pointless, and more likely to upset your stomach.
  • Adding a second supplement at the same time. Now you have two variables and no way to attribute anything.
  • Taking it late because the fog is worst in the afternoon. Understandable, and it costs you sleep, which makes tomorrow worse.
  • Forgetting the guarantee date. Sixty days arrives faster than twelve weeks. Put it in your calendar on day one.

One more that isn't on the list because it's less a mistake than a category error: expecting a supplement to compensate for something structural. If you're sleeping five hours, drinking heavily, or under sustained stress, those are doing more to your recall than any capsule will undo. The supplement is at best a small addition on top of the basics, and it's a poor substitute for them.

Do you need to cycle it?

Cycling means taking a supplement for a period, then stopping for a period. It's common advice in nootropic circles and it's mostly applied without much thought.

For bacopa there's no good case for it. The mechanism depends on continuous intake and the trials dosed daily throughout. Stopping every few weeks would work against the one ingredient with the best evidence.

For ginseng there is a case. Traditional use often involves breaks, and some practitioners suggest a week off every couple of months, partly on tolerance grounds and partly out of caution about long-term continuous use. The evidence either way is thin. If you want to cycle, cycle the whole product rather than trying to isolate ingredients, and do it after you've completed a full twelve-week trial rather than during it.

And if twelve weeks goes well?

Say you complete the trial and your week-twelve notes look better than your week-one notes. What then?

The honest answer is that nobody knows what the right long-term duration is, because the trials didn't run that long. Most bacopa studies stopped at twelve weeks, which is where the evidence stops too. Continuing is reasonable. So is stopping for a month to see whether the improvement holds without it, which doubles as a cheap check on whether you were measuring a real effect or a hopeful one.

Storage is worth a line while we're here. Keep the bottle closed, out of direct sunlight, and away from the humidity of a bathroom cabinet. Botanical extracts degrade with heat and moisture, and a kitchen cupboard away from the hob is better than the shelf above it. Check the expiry date on arrival, particularly if you bought a six-bottle pack, since the last bottle needs to still be in date when you reach it.

On multi-bottle packs generally: they lower the cost per day, which is real, but they also commit you to a product you haven't finished testing. Buying six bottles before you've completed one is a bet on an outcome you don't have yet. One or three is the more sensible way to start, and the six-pack makes sense once you actually know it does something for you.

When to stop taking it

  • You completed twelve weeks and noticed nothing. That's a real result. Stop and put the money elsewhere.
  • Persistent stomach trouble that taking it with food hasn't fixed within two weeks.
  • Sleep got worse and moving the dose to the morning didn't help.
  • You're within two weeks of surgery. Stop and tell the anaesthetist.
  • You started a new prescription. Pause and check the interactions before resuming.
  • Your memory changed noticeably. That's a doctor's appointment, and it shouldn't wait on a supplement trial finishing.

On the last point, be specific with yourself about what you're testing. This category is a reasonable thing to try for the ordinary fuzziness that comes with age, stress and poor sleep. It is not the right response to a genuine change, and several of the things that cause genuine change are treatable once someone identifies them.

Consistency beats precision here. Taking it at roughly the same time every day matters more than taking it at the perfect time some days.

Frequently asked questions

What is the correct Memopezil dosage?

The labelled serving is two capsules per day. A bottle contains 60 capsules, which is a 30-day supply at that serving. Taking more than the labelled amount is not advisable, and there's no evidence that a higher dose of these ingredients produces a larger effect.

Should I take Memopezil in the morning or at night?

Morning. Rhodiola rosea and panax ginseng are both mildly stimulating and are associated with disturbed sleep when taken later in the day. Since sleep is when memory consolidation happens, an evening dose can work against the result you're after.

Should Memopezil be taken with food?

Yes. Bacopa monnieri commonly causes stomach upset on an empty stomach, and bacosides are better absorbed alongside dietary fat. A normal breakfast containing some fat handles both issues. This is the most common fixable complaint people have in the first week.

Can I split the two capsules across the day?

You can. One at breakfast and one at lunch is fine, as long as the second dose isn't late enough for the stimulating ingredients to affect your sleep. Splitting slightly favours L-theanine, which acts acutely, and makes no difference to bacopa, which accumulates over weeks.

What happens if I miss a dose?

Take the next one as usual and don't double up. Bacopa works by building up over weeks of regular use, so an occasional missed day has no meaningful effect. Consistency across a month is what matters, not perfection on any given day.

How long should I take Memopezil before deciding whether it works?

At least twelve weeks. Bacopa's clinical trials ran 8 to 12 weeks before recall scores moved, and shorter studies largely found nothing. Note that the 60-day guarantee expires at roughly 8.5 weeks, so if the refund matters to you, plan for a decision at that point.
