Sundowning: Why Confusion Gets Worse in the Evening

A person with dementia can seem relatively steady all day, then grow confused, restless or anxious as the light fades. This pattern has a name, and it's more common, and more explainable, than it looks.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published September 12, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Sundowning is a pattern of worsening confusion, agitation or restlessness in the late afternoon and evening, most often seen in people with Alzheimer's or another dementia.
  • It's a symptom pattern, not a separate diagnosis, and it doesn't happen to everyone with dementia.
  • Likely contributors include disrupted circadian rhythm, fatigue, low light and long shadows, and a stretch of stimulation piling up over the day.
  • A sudden change or a new bout of sundowning can have a treatable cause, like a urinary tract infection, pain, or a medication side effect, so it's worth a doctor's attention.
  • Routine, daytime light, and a calmer evening environment are the best-supported, non-drug ways to ease it.

Sundowning describes a pattern seen in some people with dementia where confusion, agitation, anxiety or restlessness noticeably worsen in the late afternoon and evening, often easing again later at night or by morning. It isn't a diagnosis on its own. It's a term caregivers and clinicians use for a recognizable pattern that shows up alongside Alzheimer's disease and other forms of dementia, and it doesn't affect every person who has one.

What is sundowning?

The name comes from timing: symptoms tend to build as daylight fades, roughly late afternoon through evening, then often settle down again once someone is asleep or well into the night. What it looks like varies a lot from person to person. Some people become more anxious or tearful. Others pace, ask to "go home" even while already home, or become suspicious of familiar people. Some get physically restless, wanting to leave the house or rearrange furniture, and a smaller share become verbally or physically agitated.

It's worth separating sundowning from ordinary end-of-day tiredness, which nearly everyone experiences to some degree. The distinction is really about severity and pattern: sundowning refers to a noticeable, fairly consistent worsening of dementia-related symptoms at a predictable time of day, not just feeling a bit more worn out by dinner.

Who gets it, and when

Sundowning is most often described in the middle stages of Alzheimer's disease, though it can appear earlier or later, and some people with dementia never show the pattern at all. It's also reported, less commonly, in other conditions that affect cognition, and can show up temporarily during a hospital stay in older adults without dementia, sometimes called sundowning-like delirium, which has overlapping but not identical causes.

How often it happens and how severe it gets tends to shift over time. It can also come and go, better on some days than others, which makes it harder for caregivers to plan around and easy to mistake for something the person is doing on purpose rather than a symptom.

Why does confusion get worse in the evening?

There isn't one confirmed cause, and researchers are still working out how much each factor contributes. A few explanations come up repeatedly:

  • Circadian rhythm disruption. Dementia can affect the brain's internal clock, the system that governs the sleep-wake cycle, which may make the transition from day to evening genuinely more disorienting.
  • Accumulated fatigue. By late afternoon, a person has been processing sights, sounds and decisions all day, and dementia often reduces the mental reserve available to keep filtering that input. Fatigue lowers the threshold for confusion and irritability in anyone; the effect can be sharper with dementia.
  • Low light and shadows. Dimming light changes how a room looks and can make it harder to interpret shapes and faces correctly, which may feed into misperception or fear, especially for someone whose visual processing is already affected.
  • Disrupted sleep. Poor sleep the night before, or a late nap that leaves someone groggy and out of sync, can make the evening harder regardless of the underlying cause.
  • Hunger, thirst or unaddressed pain. Needs that are easy to miss, especially in someone who can't clearly communicate them, tend to surface as agitation rather than a direct request.

In many cases it's probably several of these stacking together rather than one single trigger, which is part of why there's no single fix that works for everyone.

When it's something else: ruling out a medical cause

A sudden new bout of sundowning, or a sharp worsening of an existing pattern, deserves a call to the person's doctor rather than an assumption that it's simply the dementia progressing. Common, treatable triggers include a urinary tract infection (which in older adults can show up as confusion before any of the more typical symptoms), constipation, dehydration, unmanaged pain, a medication side effect or interaction, or an infection elsewhere in the body. Ruling these out matters both for comfort and because some of them are straightforward to treat once identified.

What actually helps

None of these are guaranteed fixes, and what works tends to be individual, but caregivers and clinicians commonly find these approaches useful:

Keep a predictable routine

Consistent wake times, meal times and bedtime reduce the number of transitions a person has to process, and predictability itself seems to lower anxiety for many people with dementia.

Get daylight exposure earlier in the day

Time outside or near a bright window during the morning and early afternoon may help reinforce a more typical circadian rhythm, and some care settings use structured light exposure for this reason. It's a reasonable, low-risk thing to try, though the evidence base is still developing.

Watch caffeine and late naps

Caffeine later in the day and long naps close to evening can both make nighttime sleep, and the following evening, harder.

Simplify the evening environment

Turning on lights before the room actually gets dark, closing curtains to cut down on confusing reflections, and reducing background noise and clutter, a loud television, an overly busy room, all give the brain less to interpret at exactly the point in the day when interpretation is hardest.

Redirect rather than argue

When someone is confused or insistent about something that isn't accurate, correcting them directly often increases distress. Gently redirecting attention, to a task, a walk, a familiar object, tends to work better than trying to reason someone out of a false belief in the moment.

When to talk to a doctor

Bring it up if sundowning is new, worsening, involves any risk of the person leaving the house unsupervised, or is wearing down the caregiver's own sleep and health, which happens often and matters in its own right. A doctor can check for the treatable causes above, review medications, and in some cases discuss short-term options if non-drug approaches aren't enough. This is genuinely a conversation to have with a physician rather than something to manage entirely alone; every situation is different, and a clinician who knows the person's history is better placed to weigh the options than a general guide can be.

The bigger picture

Sundowning can be frightening the first few times it shows up, both for the person experiencing it and for whoever is caring for them. Knowing it's a recognized, fairly common pattern, not a sign that something new or catastrophic is happening, tends to make it easier to respond calmly. Frequently asked questions

What is sundowning in dementia?

Sundowning is a pattern of worsening confusion, agitation, anxiety or restlessness in the late afternoon and evening, seen in some people with Alzheimer's disease or another dementia. It's a symptom pattern rather than a separate diagnosis.

What causes sundowning?

There's no single confirmed cause. Likely contributors include disrupted circadian rhythm, accumulated fatigue over the day, dim light and shadows that make rooms harder to interpret, poor sleep, and unmet needs like hunger, thirst or pain.

Is sundowning a sign dementia is getting worse?

Not necessarily on its own. It's commonly seen in the middle stages of Alzheimer's and can fluctuate day to day. A sudden new episode or sharp worsening is worth a doctor's attention, since it can also point to a separate, treatable cause like an infection.

How can caregivers manage sundowning?

A predictable daily routine, daylight exposure earlier in the day, limiting late caffeine and naps, brightening the room before it gets dark, and gently redirecting rather than arguing during confused moments are the most commonly recommended, non-drug approaches.

When should sundowning be checked by a doctor?

When it's new, getting worse, raises safety concerns like wandering, or is affecting the caregiver's own health. A doctor can rule out treatable triggers such as a urinary tract infection, pain, constipation or a medication side effect.
