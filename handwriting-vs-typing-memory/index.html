Handwriting vs Typing: Which Helps You Remember More?

The research does favour handwriting. What it actually shows, though, is that the advantage comes from being forced to think, not from holding a pen.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published August 25, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Handwritten notes tend to be remembered better, particularly for conceptual understanding rather than verbatim facts.
  • Speed is the mechanism. Writing is slower, so you must summarise, and summarising is processing.
  • Typing verbatim is the real problem, since transcribing bypasses comprehension entirely.
  • Brain imaging shows richer connectivity patterns during handwriting, though what that means for learning is still debated.
  • You can get most of the benefit while typing by deliberately restricting yourself to summarising.

The claim circulates constantly: write by hand and you'll remember more. It comes with brain imaging pictures and confident advice to ditch the laptop. The underlying research is real, and it's more interesting than the headline, because it points at something you can apply regardless of which tool you use.

What the studies found

The best-known work in this area compared students taking notes by hand with students typing on laptops, then tested them afterwards. Both groups performed similarly on factual recall questions. On conceptual questions, the ones requiring understanding rather than retrieval, the handwriting group did better.

The researchers also examined the notes themselves and found the pattern that explains the result. Laptop users wrote considerably more, and much of it was verbatim transcription of what the lecturer said. Handwriting students wrote less and rephrased more.

That's the finding worth carrying away. The advantage tracked with how much students reworded, not with the writing implement.

It's worth noting that replication attempts have produced mixed results, with some studies finding smaller effects or none. The direction of the evidence favours handwriting, and the size of the advantage is less certain than popular coverage implies.

Why slower is better here

Nobody handwrites fast enough to transcribe speech. That constraint forces a decision on every sentence: what matters, what can be dropped, how to compress this into fewer words. Making those decisions requires understanding the material.

Typing removes the constraint. A competent typist can nearly keep pace with a speaker, and when transcription becomes possible it becomes tempting. You end up with an excellent record of the lecture and no memory of it, because the words travelled from ear to fingers without stopping anywhere useful in between.

This connects to a broader principle in learning research. Effort during encoding predicts retention. Techniques that make learning feel harder in the moment often produce better recall later, which is why active recall outperforms rereading and why spaced repetition beats cramming.

What brain imaging adds

Studies using EEG have found more widespread connectivity across brain regions during handwriting compared to typing, particularly involving areas associated with sensory and motor processing. Forming a letter by hand involves a complex, variable motor sequence. Pressing a key does not.

Whether that richer activity causes better learning, or simply reflects a more complex motor task, isn't settled. Correlating brain activity with cognitive benefit is harder than it looks, and the imaging findings are best read as consistent with the behavioural results rather than as proof of the mechanism.

In children learning to read and write, the evidence for handwriting is somewhat stronger. Producing letters by hand appears to support letter recognition in ways that typing doesn't, likely because the motor act reinforces the visual form.

How to get the benefit while typing

Most people can't or won't return to paper for everything, and they don't need to. The mechanism is summarising, so build summarising into typing deliberately.

Set a rule that you won't type full sentences from a speaker. Bullet points and fragments only. This is uncomfortable at first and it's supposed to be, because the discomfort is the processing.

Try closing the laptop periodically and writing what you just heard from memory. That converts note-taking into retrieval practice, which is stronger than either method of recording.

Leave a wide margin, physical or otherwise, for your own questions and connections. Notes that contain only the speaker's ideas are a transcript. Notes containing your reactions are thinking.

Review within a day. Notes taken and never revisited help far less than either method suggests, regardless of how they were produced.

When typing is the better choice

Handwriting isn't universally superior and pretending otherwise is unhelpful. Typing wins when you need searchable records, when volume genuinely matters, when handwriting is painful or slow due to arthritis or injury, and for anyone whose handwriting is illegible enough that the notes can't be used later.

Accessibility matters here too. For people with dysgraphia or motor difficulties, typing removes a barrier that would otherwise consume the very attention the handwriting advantage is supposed to free up.

The honest position: handwriting has a modest edge for conceptual learning when both are done thoughtfully, and thoughtful typing beats mindless handwriting comfortably.

Beyond note-taking

The same principle applies to remembering things generally. Writing a shopping list by hand and then leaving it at home still works better than not writing it, because the act of writing encoded it. Journaling by hand has been associated with better recall of the events recorded, and our piece on journaling for memory covers that.

For names, writing someone's name down after meeting them helps for the same reason it helps in a lecture. You have to attend to it. Our guide on how to remember names covers the other techniques worth combining with it.

None of this substitutes for the foundations. Sleep, exercise and diet drive more of your memory performance than any note-taking method, and our guide to improving your memory ranks them by evidence.

The pen isn't magic. Being too slow to transcribe is, because it forces you to decide what actually matters.

Frequently asked questions

Is handwriting better than typing for memory?

Research generally favours handwriting for conceptual understanding, with both methods performing similarly on straightforward factual recall. The advantage appears to come from writing being slower, which forces summarising rather than verbatim transcription. Replication studies have found mixed effect sizes.

Why do handwritten notes help you remember more?

Handwriting is too slow to transcribe speech, so it forces you to decide what matters and rephrase it in fewer words. Making those decisions requires understanding the material, and that processing is what improves retention. Typing allows near-verbatim transcription, which bypasses comprehension.

Can I get the same benefit from typing?

Largely, yes, if you deliberately restrict yourself to summarising rather than transcribing. Use bullet points and fragments instead of full sentences, close the laptop periodically to write what you remember, and add your own questions alongside the speaker's points.

Does handwriting change brain activity?

EEG studies have found more widespread connectivity across brain regions during handwriting than typing, particularly in sensory and motor areas. Whether that richer activity causes better learning or simply reflects a more complex motor task has not been established.

When is typing the better option?

When you need searchable records, when volume genuinely matters, when handwriting is painful due to arthritis or injury, or when your handwriting is too illegible to use later. For people with dysgraphia or motor difficulties, typing removes a barrier that would otherwise consume attention.
