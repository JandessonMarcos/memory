Memopezil vs Prevagen: Formula, Evidence and Cost Compared

One bets on a single protein you can buy at any pharmacy. The other bets on five studied ingredients sold only online. Here's how they actually stack up for a buyer.

By the Memory Lab editorial team  ·  How we research and write  ·  Published October 22, 2026

Key takeaways

  • Prevagen is a one-ingredient product. Its active is apoaequorin, a protein first found in jellyfish, at 10 mg in the regular-strength capsule.
  • Memopezil is a five-ingredient stack with every dose printed: BCAAs, bacopa, rhodiola, L-theanine and panax ginseng across two capsules.
  • Prevagen's marketing has a legal history. In 2017 the FTC and the New York Attorney General sued its maker over its memory claims.
  • Prevagen wins on convenience. It's on pharmacy shelves. Memopezil is official-site only, with a 60-day guarantee attached to the product.
  • Neither has a strong trial on the finished product that settles the question. Bacopa, inside Memopezil, has the deeper independent research base.

Memopezil vs Prevagen comes down to two different bets. Prevagen relies on one ingredient, apoaequorin, whose memory claims drew an FTC lawsuit. Memopezil combines five ingredients with printed doses, anchored by bacopa, which has independent trials behind it. Prevagen is easier to buy in a store; Memopezil carries a 60-day guarantee from the seller.

That's the short version. The longer one matters because these two products appeal to the same person: someone over 50 who's started losing names and car keys and wants to do something about it without a prescription. They just go about it in very different ways, and the differences show up in the evidence, the price per day and what happens if you want your money back.

What is Prevagen, and how is apoaequorin supposed to work?

Prevagen is made by Quincy Bioscience, a Wisconsin company, and it's one of the most heavily advertised memory products in the country. If you've watched evening news on network TV, you've seen the ads.

Its single active ingredient is apoaequorin. The protein was originally identified in a glowing jellyfish, though the commercial version is produced in a lab rather than harvested. In the jellyfish, it binds calcium. The marketing logic follows from that: calcium regulation inside nerve cells changes with age, so a calcium-binding protein might support memory.

The obvious question is digestion. Apoaequorin is a protein, and the gut breaks proteins down into amino acids before absorbing them. For the theory to hold, enough of it would have to survive digestion and reach the brain intact. That's the point critics, and later regulators, kept coming back to. We aren't making a clinical claim either way. We're telling you why careful buyers hesitate.

To be fair to Prevagen, it has things going for it. It's simple: one capsule, one ingredient, nothing to cross-check against your medicine cabinet. It's sold by a company that's been around for years, at stores you already shop in, and plenty of people find a one-a-day pill easier to stick with than two capsules. Those are real advantages. They just aren't evidence that it improves memory.

What's in Memopezil, and why five ingredients?

Memopezil's two-capsule serving holds 1,030 mg of active material, every milligram printed on the panel: BCAAs 540 mg, bacopa monnieri 200 mg, rhodiola rosea 100 mg, L-theanine 100 mg and panax ginseng 90 mg. There's no caffeine and no proprietary blend.

Bacopa is the anchor. It's the herb with the most randomized trials for delayed recall in adults, usually at 300 mg of a standardized extract over about 12 weeks. Memopezil's 200 mg is two-thirds of that. Rhodiola and L-theanine sit inside their studied ranges and work on fatigue and calm focus. Ginseng at 90 mg and BCAAs at 540 mg are well under research amounts, which is the main criticism in our review.

So it isn't a perfect label. What it is, though, is a checkable one. You can put each line next to the research and see where it falls short. That's the core contrast with Prevagen. Prevagen's single dose is also disclosed, but there's very little independent literature to check it against. Which one has better evidence behind it?

Neither has the kind of proof you'd want. But the gap between them is real.

Prevagen's case rests mainly on the Madison Memory Study, a 90-day trial the company ran in a little over 200 adults. According to the FTC's complaint, the study didn't find a statistically significant improvement across the full group on its memory tasks. The company highlighted results from subgroups, which is the part regulators objected to. Slicing a failed trial into smaller groups until one shows a result is a well-known way to find a pattern that isn't really there.

Memopezil has no trial at all on its finished formula. That's a real weakness and we won't dress it up. What it has is ingredients with independent research: bacopa in particular has been tested by many separate groups, in different countries, with results pooled in meta-analyses. The effect is modest. It's also more consistent than anything published on oral apoaequorin.

One rule applies to both products and to the whole category. Evidence that an ingredient works at a certain dose isn't evidence that a specific bottle works. Our explainer on whether memory supplements work at all goes into that distinction.

What happened with the Prevagen FTC lawsuit?

In January 2017, the Federal Trade Commission and the New York State Attorney General jointly sued Quincy Bioscience. They alleged that ads claiming Prevagen improves memory, and that it was clinically shown to do so, weren't backed by adequate evidence.

The complaint focused on two things. First, the full-sample results of the company's own study. Second, the claim about the protein reaching the brain, given how proteins are digested. Quincy disputed the allegations and the case went through years of litigation, including appeals. Prevagen stayed on sale throughout. Separate consumer class actions over the same marketing were also filed. For the current status, the FTC's own case page is the place to check.

Is that a reason not to buy Prevagen? It's a reason to discount the advertising. A lawsuit over marketing claims doesn't mean the capsule is unsafe, and we've never suggested it is. It means the strongest claims you've heard on TV were contested by regulators.

Fairness cuts both ways here. Memopezil's own affiliate ads have been aggressive too, and our review scored its marketing honesty low. The difference is that Memopezil's label lets you ignore the ads and judge the panel. That's the habit we'd recommend for any product in this aisle.

Which is cheaper per day?

Prevagen is a single capsule a day, and its price depends on where you buy it and which strength you pick. The math is simple: divide the shelf price by 30 for the regular bottle. Coupons and store promotions can move it a lot, so check the current price at your pharmacy.

When you compare, compare a full trial period rather than a single bottle. Three months of Prevagen at the regular strength is three bottles. Three months of Memopezil is three bottles too, since each lasts 30 days. If you move up to a higher Prevagen strength, the monthly cost climbs with it, so work the numbers for the version you'd actually buy.

Memopezil is two capsules a day, 60 per bottle, so one bottle is a month. At the published pack prices, that's about $2.63 a day on the 2-bottle pack (plus shipping), about $2.30 on 3 bottles and about $1.63 on 6, both with free US shipping. There's no subscription and no auto-ship.

Which pack makes sense? Bacopa's research measures results at 8 to 12 weeks, so two bottles is shorter than a fair test. Three is the realistic minimum if you're serious about finding out. Six only makes sense once you're fairly sure you'll take it every day for half a year.

How do the refund policies compare?

This is a quiet but meaningful difference. Prevagen bought at a pharmacy or big-box store is covered by that retailer's return policy, which varies and is often short for opened supplements. Quincy may offer its own terms on direct orders, so read them on their site before relying on them.

Memopezil's 60-day money-back guarantee travels with the product, not the store, and our review notes it applies whether bottles are full, partly used or empty. The catch: it only covers official orders. Marketplace listings using the name are resellers or counterfeits, and they aren't covered.

There's also a timing tension worth planning for. Sixty days is shorter than bacopa's 12-week evidence window. Start the day the bottles arrive and make your keep-or-refund call around week eight.

Who should skip each one?

Prevagen's single protein has a short list of reported side effects, mostly mild. The bigger issue for Prevagen buyers is expectation: paying for months on the strength of TV claims that regulators challenged.

Memopezil's cautions come from its ingredients. Bacopa can upset the stomach on an empty stomach. Rhodiola and ginseng can be activating if taken late. Talk to your doctor first if any of these apply:

  • Blood thinners such as warfarin. Ginseng interacts.
  • Diabetes medication. Ginseng can affect blood sugar.
  • Antidepressants, especially MAOIs or SSRIs, or sedatives. Rhodiola has interaction potential.
  • Thyroid medication, pregnancy, breastfeeding, or surgery within two weeks.

For both products, one more rule. If memory changes are sudden, getting worse or affecting daily life, see a doctor before buying anything. Our guide to telling memory loss from normal aging covers where that line sits. Neither product treats or prevents dementia.

So which should you choose?

If convenience is your top priority and you want to pick something up today, Prevagen is the one you can buy on the way home. Go in knowing that the evidence for it is thin and the advertising was contested in court.

If you'd rather judge a product by its label, Memopezil gives you more to work with. Five numbers you can check, one herb with a real research base, two ingredients that work on stress and fatigue, and a guarantee that doesn't depend on a store clerk. It has clear weak spots, and you'll need about three months to know whether it's doing anything for you.

On balance, for a buyer over 50 who's willing to give it a proper trial, we'd lean toward Memopezil. Not because it's proven. Because it's verifiable, and its main ingredient has been tested by people who weren't selling it. Frequently asked questions

Is Prevagen safe to take with Memopezil?

We don't recommend stacking them without your doctor's input. There's no known direct interaction between apoaequorin and Memopezil's ingredients, but combining two products makes it impossible to tell which one is doing anything, and Memopezil's ginseng and rhodiola have their own medication interactions. Pick one, run it properly, and judge it on its own.

Can I buy Memopezil at CVS or Walmart like Prevagen?

No. Memopezil is sold only through its official site. It isn't stocked by Amazon, Walmart, CVS, Walgreens or GNC, and marketplace listings under that name aren't covered by the 60-day guarantee. Prevagen, by contrast, is widely available in pharmacies and big-box stores.

Does Prevagen still sell after the FTC lawsuit?

Yes. Prevagen remained on the market while the case moved through the courts. A lawsuit over advertising claims doesn't remove a supplement from sale. For the latest status of the case, check the FTC's own case page.

How many capsules a day does each take?

Prevagen is one capsule a day. Memopezil is two capsules a day, taken with water, ideally in the morning with or shortly before a meal. A Memopezil bottle holds 60 capsules, so it lasts 30 days.

Which works faster, Memopezil or Prevagen?

Neither should be expected to work fast. Memopezil's L-theanine and rhodiola may produce a calmer, steadier feeling within days, but its main memory ingredient, bacopa, is measured in trials at 8 to 12 weeks. Prevagen's own study ran 90 days. Plan on about three months for either.

Is Memopezil a jellyfish supplement too?

No. Memopezil contains no apoaequorin. Its five ingredients are BCAAs, bacopa monnieri extract, rhodiola rosea, L-theanine and panax ginseng extract. Some review sites list ingredients that aren't on its label, so check the panel itself.

Is either one a treatment for Alzheimer's?

No. Both are dietary supplements, and no supplement treats, prevents or reverses Alzheimer's or dementia. If memory problems are worsening or affecting daily life, see a doctor. Several causes of memory trouble, like thyroid problems, B12 deficiency and medication side effects, are treatable.
